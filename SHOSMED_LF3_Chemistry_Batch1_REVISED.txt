What is the maximum number of electrons that can occupy a single s orbital?

  A) 1
  B) 2
  C) 6
  D) 10

### Correct Answer: 2

According to the **Pauli exclusion principle**, any single orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons, and they must have opposite spins (+1/2 and -1/2).

  | Subshell | Number of Orbitals | Max Electrons per Orbital | Max Electrons per Subshell |
  | s | 1 | 2 | 2 |
  | p | 3 | 2 | 6 |
  | d | 5 | 2 | 10 |
  | f | 7 | 2 | 14 |

An s subshell contains only 1 orbital (spherical shape), so it holds a maximum of 2 electrons. A p subshell has 3 orbitals (dumbbell shapes along x, y, z axes), holding up to 6 total.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 1:** One electron can occupy an orbital, but this is not its maximum capacity. A second electron can occupy the same orbital if it has the opposite spin.

**C. 6:** Six is the capacity of an entire p subshell, which contains three orbitals. Each individual p orbital, like an s orbital, holds at most two electrons.

**D. 10:** 10 is the maximum number of electrons in an entire d subshell (5 orbitals x 2 electrons each). A single orbital of any type (s, p, d, or f) can hold at most 2 electrons with opposite spins, per the Pauli exclusion principle.

### Key Educational Points:

  - One orbital can hold at most two electrons, with opposite spins.
  - An s subshell contains one orbital; a p subshell contains three.
  - Subshell capacities are s: 2, p: 6, d: 10 and f: 14 electrons.

>> Bottom Line: Every orbital holds max 2 electrons (Pauli principle). The number of orbitals per subshell increases: s=1, p=3, d=5, f=7 (odd numbers). Total max electrons = 2 x number of orbitals.

At 25°C, a dilute aqueous solution has a pH of 4. Approximately how many times greater is its hydrogen ion concentration than that of pure water at pH 7?

  A) 3 times
  B) 30 times
  C) 300 times
  D) 1000 times

### Correct Answer: 1000 times

The pH scale is **logarithmic**. For a dilute aqueous solution, pH ≈ -log₁₀[H⁺], so each one-unit decrease in pH corresponds to about ten times the hydrogen ion concentration.

pH 7: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M (pure water)

pH 4: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴ M

Ratio = 10⁻⁴ / 10⁻⁷ = 10³ = **1,000 times the hydrogen ion concentration**

  | pH Difference | Fold Change in [H+] |
  | 1 unit | 10x |
  | 2 units | 100x |
  | 3 units | 1,000x |
  | 4 units | 10,000x |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 3 times:** A difference of 3 pH units does not mean 3 times more acidic. This is a common mistake from treating the pH scale as linear. Because pH is logarithmic (base 10), each unit change represents a 10-fold change in H+ concentration, so 3 units = 10^3 = 1000-fold difference.

**B. 30 times:** A 30-fold difference is not the result of a three-unit pH change. Multiply by 10 for each unit: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.

**C. 300 times:** A three-unit pH difference gives 10^3 = 1,000, not 300. The three is an exponent, not a multiplier applied to 100.

### Key Educational Points:

  - In dilute aqueous solutions, pH ≈ -log₁₀[H⁺].
  - Each one-unit fall in pH corresponds to about ten times the hydrogen ion concentration.
  - From pH 7 to pH 4, the concentration ratio is 103 = 1,000.

>> Bottom Line: A decrease of three pH units corresponds to about a 1,000-fold increase in hydrogen ion concentration in dilute aqueous solutions. Neutral water has pH 7 at 25°C.

Which type of bond is most likely to form between sodium (Na) and chlorine (Cl)?

  A) Nonpolar covalent bond
  B) Polar covalent bond
  C) Ionic bond
  D) Metallic bond

### Correct Answer: Ionic bond

Sodium chloride is an ionic solid made of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a crystal lattice. Sodium readily loses one electron, while chlorine readily gains one. Attraction between the oppositely charged ions holds the lattice together. The large electronegativity difference supports this ionic description, but numerical electronegativity cutoffs are only guides, not universal rules.

  | Bonding description | Electron distribution | Example |
  | Nonpolar covalent | Shared approximately equally | H-H |
  | Polar covalent | Shared unequally | H-Cl |
  | Ionic | Attraction between oppositely charged ions | NaCl |

Na loses 1 electron to become Na⁺, and Cl gains 1 electron to become Cl⁻. The electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged ions forms the ionic bond.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Nonpolar covalent bond:** Nonpolar covalent bonding involves approximately equal sharing, as in H₂ or Cl₂. Sodium chloride instead forms a lattice of positively and negatively charged ions.

**B. Polar covalent bond:** Polar covalent bonds involve unequal sharing within a covalent bond, as in HCl. Solid NaCl is described as an ionic lattice. Electronegativity difference alone is not an absolute test separating all ionic and covalent compounds.

**D. Metallic bond:** Metallic bonds occur between metal atoms in a metallic lattice, where valence electrons are delocalized and shared across many nuclei (the "electron sea" model). This gives metals their characteristic conductivity, malleability, and luster. Since NaCl involves a metal and a nonmetal, the bond is ionic, not metallic.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Sodium chloride contains Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions arranged in an ionic lattice.
  - Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  - Electronegativity differences guide bonding predictions, but numerical cutoffs have exceptions.

>> Bottom Line: NaCl is held together by attraction between Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Electronegativity differences help predict bonding, but bonding lies on a continuum and numerical cutoffs have exceptions.

What is the molar mass of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)? (Ca = 40 g/mol, C = 12 g/mol, O = 16 g/mol)

  A) 68 g/mol
  B) 84 g/mol
  C) 100 g/mol
  D) 116 g/mol

### Correct Answer: 100 g/mol

Molar mass is calculated by adding the atomic masses of all atoms in the formula:

CaCO₃ = 1(Ca) + 1(C) + 3(O)

= 1(40) + 1(12) + 3(16)

= 40 + 12 + 48

= **100 g/mol**

This means 1 mole of CaCO₃ (which is 6.022 x 10²³ formula units) has a mass of 100 grams.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 68 g/mol:** If you got 68, you may have calculated Ca + C + O = 40 + 12 + 16 = 68, forgetting to multiply oxygen by 3. The subscript 3 in CaCO3 means there are three oxygen atoms: 3 x 16 = 48. Always account for subscripts when calculating molar mass.

**B. 84 g/mol:** 84 g/mol results from counting only two oxygen atoms: 40 + 12 + 2(16) = 84. CaCO₃ contains three oxygen atoms, so their contribution is 48 g/mol.

**D. 116 g/mol:** 116 g/mol results from counting four oxygen atoms: 40 + 12 + 4(16) = 116. The formula has three oxygen atoms, giving 100 g/mol.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A formula's subscripts specify the numbers of atoms to include in its molar mass.
  - In CaCO₃, the oxygen contribution is 3 × 16 = 48 g/mol.
  - Adding the contributions gives 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol.

>> Bottom Line: To find molar mass, multiply the atomic mass of each element by how many times it appears in the formula, then add them all up. Always check that you've accounted for subscripts (the 3 in O₃ means 3 oxygen atoms).

**Oxidation of secondary alcohols:**

Secondary alcohol (R₂-CHOH) -> Ketone (R₂-CO)

**Tertiary alcohols:**

Resistant to these ordinary oxidation conditions (no H on the carbon bearing OH)

Under the controlled conditions described, oxidation of a primary alcohol stops at the aldehyde. Further oxidation would give a carboxylic acid, so the reagents and conditions chosen decide whether the aldehyde is isolated or oxidized further.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Ketone:** A ketone is the usual product of oxidizing a secondary alcohol. A primary alcohol produces an aldehyde when oxidation is stopped at the first carbonyl product. Ketones resist the mild oxidizing conditions used for this test; stronger conditions can cleave carbon-carbon bonds.

**C. Carboxylic acid:** A carboxylic acid forms if the aldehyde is oxidized further. The conditions in the stem are chosen to prevent that further oxidation.

**D. Ester:** An ester (R-COO-R') is not the product expected from the controlled oxidation of a primary alcohol described here. Acid-catalyzed reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol is one common esterification route, not the only possible way to make an ester. Many small esters have fruity and floral aromas.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A primary alcohol can be oxidized to an aldehyde and then to a carboxylic acid.
  - Reagents and conditions determine whether the aldehyde is isolated or oxidized further.

Which group of the periodic table contains the halogens?

  A) Group IA (1)
  B) Group IIA (2)
  C) Group VIA (16)
  D) Group VIIA (17)

### Correct Answer: Group VIIA (17)

The halogens are found in Group VIIA, which is Group 17 in modern IUPAC notation. Common halogens at room temperature:

  | Element | Elemental Form | State at Room Temp |
  | Fluorine | F₂ | Gas (pale yellow) |
  | Chlorine | Cl₂ | Gas (green-yellow) |
  | Bromine | Br₂ | Liquid (red-brown) |
  | Iodine | I₂ | Solid (purple-black) |

Group 17 also includes astatine and tennessine, which are radioactive. The common halogens have seven valence electrons and readily form -1 halide ions. Their oxidizing strength generally decreases from fluorine to iodine.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Group IA (1):** Group 1 contains hydrogen and the alkali metals. The alkali metals, such as lithium and sodium, have one outer-shell electron and commonly form +1 ions. They react vigorously with water to produce hydrogen gas and a strong base.

**B. Group IIA (2):** Group IIA (Group 2) contains the alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, and Ra. These elements have 2 valence electrons and form +2 ions. They are less reactive than alkali metals but still quite reactive, especially the heavier members.

**C. Group VIA (16):** Group VIA (Group 16) contains the chalcogens. Oxygen, sulfur, selenium, tellurium and polonium are examples, and Group 16 also includes livermorium. Several members have six valence electrons and can form -2 ions, but the group does not behave as a single uniform set of nonmetals. Oxygen and sulfur are the most important members biologically.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The halogens occupy Group 17, also called Group VIIA in older notation.
  - Familiar halogens include fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine.
  - These elements have seven valence electrons and commonly gain one electron to form halide ions.

>> Bottom Line: Group VIIA = halogens (7 valence e⁻, gain 1). Group IA (Group 1) = hydrogen plus the alkali metals; the alkali metals have 1 valence e⁻ and lose it. Group IIA = alkaline earth metals. Group VIIIA = noble gases (full valence shell).

Which of the following salts, when dissolved in water at 25°C, will produce a basic (alkaline) solution?

  A) NaCl
  B) NH₄Cl
  C) CH₃COONa (sodium acetate)
  D) KNO₃

### Correct Answer: CH₃COONa (sodium acetate)

The pH of a salt solution depends on whether the salt comes from a strong or weak acid/base:

  | Salt | Acid | Base | Solution pH |
  | NaCl | HCl (strong) | NaOH (strong) | Approximately neutral (about 7) |
  | NH₄Cl | HCl (strong) | NH₃ (weak) | Acidic (<7) |
  | CH₃COONa | CH₃COOH (weak) | NaOH (strong) | Basic (>7) |
  | KNO₃ | HNO₃ (strong) | KOH (strong) | Approximately neutral (about 7) |

Sodium acetate is the salt of a **weak acid** (acetic acid) and a **strong base** (NaOH). The acetate ion (CH₃COO⁻) acts as a base in water, accepting a proton to form CH₃COOH, producing OH⁻ ions:

CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. NaCl:** NaCl is formed from HCl (strong acid) and NaOH (strong base). When dissolved in water, neither the Na+ nor the Cl- ion undergoes hydrolysis, so the solution remains neutral at pH 7. This is the general rule for salts of strong acid + strong base.

**B. NH₄Cl:** NH4Cl is formed from HCl (strong acid) and NH3 (weak base). The NH4+ ion acts as a weak acid in water, donating a proton to form NH3 and H3O+, making the solution acidic (pH below 7). This is the opposite of what the question asks for.

**D. KNO₃:** KNO3 is formed from HNO3 (strong acid) and KOH (strong base). Like NaCl, neither ion undergoes hydrolysis in water, so the solution is neutral (pH 7). K⁺ and NO₃⁻ have negligible acid-base effects in water, so this solution is approximately neutral at 25°C.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A dissolved salt's ions can act as acids, bases or spectators in water.
  - Acetate accepts a proton from water, producing OH⁻ and making the solution basic.
  - Ammonium instead donates a proton to water, making an NH₄Cl solution acidic.

>> Bottom Line: Salt of strong acid + strong base = neutral. Salt of weak acid + strong base = basic. Salt of strong acid + weak base = acidic. Salt of weak acid + weak base = depends on Kₐ vs Kᵇ.

For the exothermic reaction: 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g)  2SO₃(g), which change will shift the equilibrium to the RIGHT (toward products)?

  A) Increasing the temperature
  B) Decreasing the pressure
  C) Removing SO₃ from the system
  D) Adding a catalyst

### Correct Answer: Removing SO₃ from the system

Le Chatelier's principle states that a system at equilibrium will shift to counteract any imposed change:

  | Change | Shift Direction | Reason |
  | Increase temperature | LEFT (toward reactants) | Exothermic reaction - heat is a "product" |
  | Decrease pressure by expanding the vessel at constant temperature | LEFT (more moles of gas) | 3 moles reactant gas vs 2 moles product gas |
  | Remove SO₃ | RIGHT | System makes more product to restore equilibrium |
  | Add catalyst | No shift | Speeds up approach to equilibrium without changing K or the equilibrium composition |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Increasing the temperature:** For an exothermic reaction, heat can be thought of as a product. Increasing temperature adds "heat" to the system, so equilibrium shifts LEFT (toward reactants) to absorb the extra heat. Only for endothermic reactions does raising temperature shift equilibrium to the right.

**B. Decreasing the pressure:** Here the pressure is decreased by expanding the vessel at constant temperature, which favors the side with more moles of gas. There are 3 moles of gas on the left (2 SO₂ + 1 O₂) but only 2 on the right (2 SO₃), so expanding the vessel shifts equilibrium LEFT. A change in total pressure caused by adding or removing an inert gas is not automatically equivalent.

**D. Adding a catalyst:** A catalyst speeds up the approach to equilibrium by providing a lower-activation-energy pathway, without changing K or the equilibrium composition at the same temperature.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Removing SO₃ from this equilibrium favors further formation of SO₃.
  - Expanding this gas mixture at constant temperature favors the side with more gas particles.
  - A catalyst changes the speed of reaching equilibrium, not its position at the same temperature.

>> Bottom Line: For this gas equilibrium, removing SO₃ favors further SO₃ formation. Expanding the vessel favors the side with more gas particles. A catalyst helps equilibrium be reached faster but does not change its position.

How many milliliters of 4M NaOH are required to completely neutralize 200 mL of 2M HCl?

  A) 50 mL
  B) 100 mL
  C) 200 mL
  D) 400 mL

### Correct Answer: 100 mL

The balanced neutralization reaction is:

NaOH + HCl -> NaCl + H₂O

(1:1 molar ratio)

Step-by-step calculation:

Moles of HCl = Molarity x Volume = 2M x 0.200L = 0.400 mol

Since the ratio is 1:1, we need 0.400 mol NaOH

Volume of NaOH = moles / molarity = 0.400 mol / 4M = 0.100 L = **100 mL**

This can also be solved using the dilution-like formula for neutralization: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (when molar ratio is 1:1)

4M x V = 2M x 200 mL -> V = 100 mL

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 50 mL:** Using M1V1 = M2V2: 4M x V = 2M x 200 mL gives V = 100 mL, not 50. You would get 50 mL if the NaOH were 8M instead of 4M, or if only 100 mL of HCl were used. Always verify your substitution into the formula.

**C. 200 mL:** 200 mL of 4M NaOH would provide 0.8 mol NaOH, which is double the 0.4 mol needed. This error might arise from using the same volume as the HCl without accounting for the different concentrations.

**D. 400 mL:** 400 mL of 4M NaOH would provide 1.6 mol NaOH, which is 4 times what is needed. This answer would be correct only if the NaOH concentration were 1M instead of 4M.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Balance a neutralization equation before comparing amounts of acid and base.
  - HCl and NaOH react in a 1:1 mole ratio, so 0.400 mol HCl needs 0.400 mol NaOH.
  - At 4 mol/L, 0.400 mol NaOH occupies 0.100 L, or 100 mL.

>> Bottom Line: For neutralization, moles of acid = moles of base (when the ratio is 1:1). Use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ as a shortcut. Always check the balanced equation for the molar ratio first.

According to Hund's rule, how are the three electrons in the 2p subshell of nitrogen distributed?

  A) All three in one 2p orbital
  B) Two in one orbital, one in another, one empty
  C) One electron in each of the three 2p orbitals, all with the same spin
  D) One electron in each of the three 2p orbitals, with alternating spins

### Correct Answer: One electron in each of the three 2p orbitals, all with the same spin

Hund's rule (also called the rule of maximum multiplicity) states that electrons fill degenerate orbitals (orbitals of equal energy) singly first, with parallel spins, before pairing up.

  | Principle | Rule |
  | Aufbau principle | Fill lowest energy orbitals first (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, ...) |
  | Pauli exclusion principle | Maximum 2 electrons per orbital, with opposite spins |
  | Hund's rule | Fill degenerate orbitals singly (parallel spins) before pairing |

**Nitrogen (Z=7): 1s² 2s² 2p³**

2p orbitals: [up][up][up] (one in each, same spin)

NOT: [up-down][up][ ] (this violates Hund's rule)

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. All three in one 2p orbital:** A single orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons (Pauli exclusion principle), so placing 3 electrons in one orbital is physically impossible. Each p subshell has 3 separate orbitals (px, py, pz), and electrons must be distributed among them.

**B. Two in one orbital, one in another, one empty:** This arrangement violates Hund's rule, which states that electrons must occupy all degenerate (equal energy) orbitals singly before any orbital gets a second electron. Pairing electrons in one orbital while leaving another empty creates unnecessary electron-electron repulsion.

**D. One electron in each of the three 2p orbitals, with alternating spins:** While this correctly distributes one electron per orbital, Hund's rule requires that unpaired electrons in degenerate orbitals all have the same spin (parallel spins). This maximizes total spin and minimizes electron-electron repulsion, making the atom more stable.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Hund's rule concerns electrons occupying orbitals of equal energy in the ground state.
  - Electrons occupy those orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing.
  - Nitrogen's 2p³ arrangement therefore has three unpaired electrons.

>> Bottom Line: In the ground state, electrons occupy equal-energy orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing. Nitrogen's three 2p electrons therefore occupy three different orbitals.

What is the Stock-system name for Fe₂O₃?

  A) Iron oxide
  B) Iron(II) oxide
  C) Iron(III) oxide
  D) Diiron trioxide

In Fe₂O₃, we need to determine the oxidation state of iron. Since oxygen is -2 and there are 3 oxygens (total charge = -6), two iron atoms must provide +6, so each iron is +3.

2(Fe) + 3(O) = 0 (neutral compound)

2(x) + 3(-2) = 0

2x = +6

x = +3, so iron is in the +3 oxidation state

  | Formula | Stock-system name | Iron Oxidation State |
  | FeO | Iron(II) oxide | +2 |
  | Fe₂O₃ | Iron(III) oxide | +3 |
  | Fe₃O₄ | Iron(II,III) oxide | Mixed +2/+3 |

The Stock system uses a Roman numeral to specify the metal's oxidation state. Fe₂O₃ therefore has the Stock name iron(III) oxide. Compositional nomenclature can instead describe atom ratios, giving diiron trioxide.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Iron oxide:** Iron oxide does not identify which oxide is meant. The Stock name distinguishes iron(II) oxide, FeO, from iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃.

**B. Iron(II) oxide:** Iron(II) oxide is the IUPAC name for FeO, where iron has a +2 oxidation state. In FeO, one iron atom balances one oxygen (-2), so iron must be +2. In Fe2O3, however, the math gives each iron a +3 oxidation state.

**D. Diiron trioxide:** Diiron trioxide is a valid compositional name describing the 2:3 atom ratio. It is not the Stock-system name requested, which specifies iron's +3 oxidation state as iron(III) oxide.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound is zero.
  - In Fe₂O₃, three oxygens contribute -6, so each of the two irons has oxidation state +3.
  - Stock nomenclature expresses that oxidation state as iron(III), giving iron(III) oxide.

>> Bottom Line: In Stock nomenclature, the Roman numeral gives oxidation state, not the number of atoms. Fe₂O₃ contains iron in oxidation state +3, so its Stock name is iron(III) oxide.

Q13  [chem-13]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
For a fixed amount of ideal gas at constant temperature, what happens to the pressure if the volume is halved?

  A) Pressure is halved
  B) Pressure doubles
  C) Pressure remains the same
  D) Pressure quadruples

### Correct Answer: Pressure doubles

This is described by Boyle's law: at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional.

**Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂** (at constant T and n)

If V₂ = V₁/2, then:

P₁ x V₁ = P₂ x (V₁/2)

P₂ = 2P₁

  | Gas Law | Relationship | Constant |
  | Boyle's law | P inversely proportional to V | T, n |
  | Charles's law | V directly proportional to T | P, n |
  | Gay-Lussac's law | P directly proportional to T | V, n |
  | Avogadro's law | V directly proportional to n | T, P |
  | Ideal gas law | PV = nRT | Combined |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Pressure is halved:** If pressure halved when volume halved, that would mean pressure is directly proportional to volume. Boyle's law states the opposite: pressure and volume are inversely proportional (P is proportional to 1/V). Halving volume means doubling pressure, since PV = constant.

**C. Pressure remains the same:** Pressure cannot remain unchanged when volume changes at constant temperature, because the gas molecules would be confined to half the space. More collisions per unit area of the container walls means higher pressure, as described by Boyle's law.

**D. Pressure quadruples:** Pressure would quadruple only if volume were reduced to one-quarter of its original value (since P is proportional to 1/V). Halving the volume doubles the pressure. Remember: the change in pressure is the inverse of the change in volume.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Boyle's law: at constant temperature and amount of gas, pressure and volume are inversely proportional, so P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
  - Halving the volume therefore doubles the pressure.
  - At the same temperature, molecular speeds are unchanged on average. Compression increases collisions with each unit area of the container wall, increasing pressure.
  - Because the relationship is inverse, a P-V graph is a curve (hyperbola), not a straight line.
  - The law holds only while temperature and amount of gas are held constant - change either and it no longer applies.

>> Bottom Line: Boyle's law (PV = constant) explains why compressing a gas increases its pressure. All individual gas laws are special cases of the ideal gas law PV = nRT, where R = 8.314 J/(mol K).

A membrane permeable to water but not to Na⁺ or Cl⁻ separates 0.5 M and 0.1 M NaCl solutions at the same temperature and initially equal hydrostatic pressure. In which direction is the initial NET movement of water?

  A) From the 0.5 M solution to the 0.1 M solution
  B) From the 0.1 M solution to the 0.5 M solution
  C) Equally in both directions
  D) Water will not move across the membrane

### Correct Answer: From the 0.1 M solution to the 0.5 M solution

Water crosses this membrane in both directions. With equal initial hydrostatic pressures, the initial net movement is from the **0.1 M solution** toward the **0.5 M solution**. The concentration comparison is valid here because the same nonpenetrating solute is present on both sides.

Initial comparison at equal hydrostatic pressure:

  | Solution Type | Relative Concentration | Water Movement |
  | Hypotonic (0.1 M) | Lower solute, higher water | Net water leaves |
  | Hypertonic (0.5 M) | Higher solute, lower water | Net water enters |
  | Isotonic | Equal | No net movement |

For an ideal dilute solution, osmotic pressure is approximated by Π = iMRT, where i is the van't Hoff factor. For ideally dissociated NaCl, i ≈ 2, because each formula unit gives Na⁺ and Cl⁻. At 0.5 M the effective value is somewhat below 2, so this is an approximation rather than an exact figure. No numerical osmotic-pressure calculation is needed here.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. From the 0.5 M solution to the 0.1 M solution:** This is opposite to the initial net movement under the stated conditions. The more concentrated NaCl solution has the greater osmotic tendency to draw water across this membrane.

**C. Equally in both directions:** Water crosses both ways, but the initial rates are unequal, so there is a net transfer toward 0.5 M NaCl. Later, a hydrostatic-pressure difference can balance the osmotic effect even if the solute concentrations remain unequal.

**D. Water will not move across the membrane:** The membrane allows water to cross. At the initially equal hydrostatic pressures, the concentration difference produces a net movement toward the more concentrated solution.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Osmosis involves water crossing a membrane selectively permeable to water.
  - With equal initial hydrostatic pressures, net water moves toward the more concentrated nonpenetrating solute.
  - An opposing pressure difference can stop net osmosis before the solute concentrations become equal.

>> Bottom Line: With a nonpenetrating solute and equal initial hydrostatic pressures, water moves net toward the more concentrated solution. Net movement stops when the opposing effects balance; the concentrations need not become equal.

Assuming volumes are additive, what volume of water must be added to 100 mL of a 2 M solution to prepare a 0.5 M solution?

  A) 100 mL
  B) 200 mL
  C) 300 mL
  D) 400 mL

### Correct Answer: 300 mL

Using the dilution formula: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

2M x 100 mL = 0.5M x V₂

V₂ = (2 x 100) / 0.5 = 400 mL (total final volume)

Volume of water to add = V₂ - V₁ = 400 - 100 = **300 mL**

  | Concentration Unit | Definition | Formula |
  | Molarity (M) | Moles of solute per liter of solution | M = n/V |
  | Mass percent (w/w%) | Mass of solute per 100 g of solution | (mₛₒₗᵤₜₑ/mₛₒₗᵤₜᵢₒₙ) x 100 |
  | Volume percent (v/v%) | Volume of solute per 100 mL of solution | (Vₛₒₗᵤₜₑ/Vₛₒₗᵤₜᵢₒₙ) x 100 |

Note: The question asks for water added, not total volume. This is a common source of error. In laboratory preparation, add water until the total solution volume reaches 400 mL; solution and water volumes are not always exactly additive.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 100 mL:** Adding 100 mL of water to 100 mL of 2M solution gives 200 mL total. By M1V1 = M2V2, the new concentration would be 2M x 100/200 = 1.0 M, not 0.5 M. You need a total volume of 400 mL to reach 0.5 M.

**B. 200 mL:** Adding 200 mL of water to 100 mL of 2M solution gives 300 mL total. The new concentration would be 2M x 100/300 = 0.67 M, which is higher than the target of 0.5 M. More water is needed.

**D. 400 mL:** Adding 400 mL would give a total volume of 500 mL, and the resulting concentration would be 2M x 100/500 = 0.4 M, which is lower than the target 0.5 M. The question asks for water added (300 mL), not the total final volume (400 mL) -- a common trap in dilution problems.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Dilution conserves the amount of dissolved solute: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.
  - Here the final solution volume must be 400 mL.
  - Under the volume-additivity assumption, the water added is 400 - 100 = 300 mL.

>> Bottom Line: The dilution equation M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ works because the number of moles of solute stays constant during dilution. Always distinguish between total final volume and volume of solvent added.

For the exothermic gas reaction 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), which change speeds up the approach to equilibrium without changing the equilibrium composition? Assume pressure changes are made by changing the vessel volume.

  A) Increasing the temperature
  B) Increasing the concentration of reactants
  C) Adding a catalyst
  D) Increasing the pressure

### Correct Answer: Adding a catalyst

A catalyst increases the reaction rate by providing an alternative pathway with a **lower activation energy**, but it does NOT change the position of equilibrium or the value of the equilibrium constant.

  | Change | Equilibrium effect for this reaction |
  | Increase temperature | Favors reactants; K changes |
  | Add a reactant at fixed temperature and volume | Favors products; K is unchanged |
  | Add a catalyst at unchanged temperature | No change in equilibrium composition or K |
  | Compress the gases at fixed temperature | Favors products, the side with fewer gas particles |

Reaction rates and equilibrium are different questions. An overall rate law is determined experimentally; its concentration exponents cannot generally be read from the balanced equation. Stoichiometric coefficients determine the rate-law powers only for an appropriate elementary reaction step.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Increasing the temperature:** Increasing temperature changes K. Because this forward reaction is exothermic, the equilibrium shifts toward reactants, so the equilibrium composition is not preserved.

**B. Increasing the concentration of reactants:** Adding a reactant changes the reaction quotient and causes this mixture to form more product as equilibrium is restored. It does not change K at fixed temperature, but it does change the equilibrium composition.

**D. Increasing the pressure:** Compressing this mixture raises its partial pressures and favors SO₃ formation because the product side has fewer gas particles. Thus the equilibrium composition changes. For other gas reactions with equal gas coefficients on both sides, compression need not shift equilibrium.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Catalysts alter the reaction pathway and help equilibrium be reached faster.
  - At the same temperature, a catalyst does not change K or the final equilibrium composition.
  - An overall reaction's rate-law powers generally require experimental determination, not just its balanced equation.

>> Bottom Line: A catalyst supplies a faster reaction pathway while leaving the equilibrium constant and final equilibrium composition unchanged at the same temperature.

In the reaction: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) -> ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s), which species is the reducing agent?

  A) Zn
  B) Cu²⁺
  C) SO₄²⁻
  D) Cu

### Correct Answer: Zn

Zinc is **oxidized** (loses electrons: Zn -> Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻). The species that is oxidized is the **reducing agent** because it reduces the other species by donating electrons.

  | Species | Process | Oxidation Number Change | Role |
  | Zn | Zn -> Zn²⁺ | 0 -> +2 (oxidized) | Reducing agent |
  | Cu²⁺ | Cu²⁺ -> Cu | +2 -> 0 (reduced) | Oxidizing agent |
  | SO₄²⁻ | No change | Spectator ion | Neither |

**Memory aid (OIL RIG):**

Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons)

Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

The reducing agent IS oxidized; the oxidizing agent IS reduced.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Cu²⁺:** Cu2+ is actually the oxidizing agent in this reaction, not the reducing agent. Cu2+ gains 2 electrons (is reduced) to become Cu metal. Remember: the oxidizing agent gets reduced itself, while the reducing agent gets oxidized.

**C. SO₄²⁻:** The sulfate ion (SO4 2-) is a spectator ion in this reaction -- it does not participate in the electron transfer. It remains unchanged in solution throughout the reaction. Spectator ions appear unchanged on both sides of the complete ionic equation and are cancelled when writing the net ionic equation.

**D. Cu:** Metallic copper (Cu) is the product of reduction, not a reactant. It forms when Cu2+ gains electrons from zinc. The reducing agent must be a reactant that donates electrons, which is Zn in this case (Zn loses 2 electrons to become Zn2+).

### Key Educational Points:

  - Oxidation is electron loss; reduction is electron gain.
  - The reducing agent donates electrons and is itself oxidized.
  - Here Zn loses two electrons, while Cu²⁺ gains them; sulfate is a spectator ion.

>> Bottom Line: The activity series predicts which metals will displace others: Zn is more active than Cu, so Zn can reduce Cu²⁺. In electrochemistry, this forms the basis of galvanic (voltaic) cells.

A buffer solution is prepared by mixing acetic acid (CH₃COOH) with sodium acetate (CH₃COONa). This buffer is most effective at resisting pH changes near which pH value? (pKₐ of acetic acid = 4.76)

  A) 2.0
  B) 4.76
  C) 7.0
  D) 9.24

### Correct Answer: 4.76

A buffer is most effective when pH = pKₐ of the weak acid component. This is derived from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:

**Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:**

pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA])

When [A⁻] = [HA], log(1) = 0, so pH = pKₐ

  | Buffer Property | Detail |
  | Effective range | pKₐ +/- 1 (i.e., pH 3.76 to 5.76 for acetic acid buffer) |
  | Greatest resistance to small additions | For a given total buffer concentration, resistance to small acid/base additions is greatest near equal acid and conjugate-base amounts (pH = pKₐ) |
  | Components needed | Weak acid + its conjugate base (or weak base + its conjugate acid) |

When a small amount of strong acid is added, the conjugate base (acetate) neutralizes it. When strong base is added, the weak acid (acetic acid) neutralizes it.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 2.0:** A pH of 2.0 would indicate a very strongly acidic solution. When a weak acid and its conjugate base are present in equal concentrations, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa + 0 = pKa. For acetic acid, pKa = 4.76.

**C. 7.0:** Buffers can have pH 7 if an appropriate acid-base pair is chosen. This particular pair works best near acetic acid's pKa of 4.76; pH 7 is well outside its usual effective range.

**D. 9.24:** 9.24 is the pKb of acetic acid's conjugate base (acetate ion), since pKa + pKb = 14 for a conjugate acid-base pair at 25°C (14 - 4.76 = 9.24). A buffer made from equal concentrations of a weak acid and its conjugate base has pH = pKa, not pKb. Equal concentrations were not specified in this stem; they describe the optimal ratio, not the only mixture that can act as a buffer.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A weak acid and its conjugate base can buffer added acid or base.
  - For acetate, buffering is most effective near pKₐ = 4.76, with a useful range roughly one pH unit either side.
  - Buffer capacity depends on the amounts of both components, not just the pH.

>> Bottom Line: Choose a weak acid/conjugate-base pair with a pKₐ near the target pH. For acetate, the useful range is roughly pH 3.76-5.76, and greater total buffer concentration generally gives greater capacity.

At room temperature and away from UV light, which reagent provides a visible test to distinguish a simple alkene from an alkane?

  A) Sodium hydroxide solution
  B) Bromine water (Br₂/H₂O)
  C) Hydrochloric acid
  D) Distilled water

### Correct Answer: Bromine water (Br₂/H₂O)

Bromine water is a classic test for unsaturation (double or triple bonds). Alkenes react with bromine, decolorizing the orange-brown solution, while alkanes do not react under these conditions.

  | Hydrocarbon | General Formula | Reaction with Br₂ water | Result |
  | Acyclic alkane | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | No rapid reaction under these test conditions | Stays orange-brown |
  | Acyclic monoalkene | CₙH₂ₙ | Addition reaction | Decolorizes |
  | Acyclic monoalkyne | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | Addition reaction | Decolorizes |

**What the test observes:**

The alkene consumes bromine by addition at its C=C bond, removing the orange-brown color. In water, bromohydrin products can form; in a nonaqueous, non-nucleophilic solvent, a vicinal dibromide is the usual product. The test here depends on decolorization, not identification of one specific product.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Sodium hydroxide solution:** Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is a strong base used in reactions with acids, esters, and amides, but it does not react with simple hydrocarbons like alkanes or alkenes. It would not help distinguish between them since neither undergoes a visible reaction with NaOH.

### Key Educational Points:

  - An alkene's C=C bond can react by addition with bromine.
  - Consumption of bromine removes its orange-brown color.
  - A simple alkane does not rapidly decolorize bromine water under the same conditions away from UV light.

>> Bottom Line: A simple alkene rapidly decolorizes bromine water by addition at C=C. A simple alkane does not under the same conditions without radical initiation. Other reactive compounds can also decolorize bromine, so this is not a universal identification test for every unknown organic compound.

Benzene preferentially undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution rather than addition reactions. This is because:

  A) Benzene has very strong C-H bonds
  B) Substitution preserves the aromatic stability (delocalized pi electron system)
  C) Benzene is sterically hindered and cannot undergo addition
  D) Addition preserves benzene's aromatic pi system

### Correct Answer: Substitution preserves the aromatic stability (delocalized pi electron system)

Benzene's aromatic pi system stabilizes the ring. Electrophilic substitution temporarily disrupts this system, but loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity in the product. Addition across the ring would remove aromaticity from that product, so ordinary alkene-like addition is not benzene's characteristic reaction.

  | Reaction type | Aromaticity in the final product |
  | Substitution | Restored |
  | Addition | Lost |

**Common Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) reactions:**

Halogenation: C₆H₆ + Br₂ (FeBr₃) -> C₆H₅Br + HBr

Nitration: C₆H₆ + HNO₃ (H₂SO₄) -> C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O

Sulfonation: C₆H₆ + SO₃ (H₂SO₄) -> C₆H₅SO₃H

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Benzene has very strong C-H bonds:** While benzene does have relatively strong C-H bonds (due to sp2 hybridization), this is not the primary reason it prefers substitution. The key factor is the special stability of the aromatic pi system (delocalization energy of about 150 kJ/mol), which would be lost in an addition reaction but is preserved in substitution.

**C. Benzene is sterically hindered and cannot undergo addition:** Benzene is a small planar molecule, so steric crowding does not explain its characteristic reactivity. It can undergo addition, such as catalytic hydrogenation, under suitable conditions.

**D. Addition preserves benzene's aromatic pi system:** Addition to the aromatic ring disrupts its continuous pi system. Electrophilic aromatic substitution instead replaces a ring hydrogen and restores aromaticity after the intermediate forms.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Benzene has a stabilized aromatic system of six pi electrons.
  - Electrophilic aromatic substitution replaces a ring hydrogen and restores aromaticity in the product.
  - Benzene can undergo addition under suitable conditions, but ordinary alkene-like addition is not its characteristic reaction.

>> Bottom Line: Aromaticity (Huckel's rule: 4n+2 pi electrons in a planar cyclic conjugated system) provides exceptional stability. Benzene's 6 pi electrons (n=1) make it aromatic. EAS is the hallmark reaction of aromatic compounds.

Phenol (C₆H₅OH) is more acidic than ethanol (C₂H₅OH) because:

  A) Phenol has a higher molecular weight
  B) The phenoxide ion is stabilized by resonance delocalization into the aromatic ring
  C) Phenol has more hydrogen atoms available for donation
  D) The ethoxide ion is more resonance-stabilized than the phenoxide ion

### Correct Answer: The phenoxide ion is stabilized by resonance delocalization into the aromatic ring

When phenol loses a proton, the resulting phenoxide ion (C₆H₅O⁻) is stabilized because the negative charge is delocalized across the aromatic ring through resonance. Ethoxide has no such stabilization.

  | Property | Phenol | Ethanol |
  | pKₐ | ~10 | ~16 |
  | Acidity | More acidic | Less acidic |
  | Conjugate base stability | Resonance-delocalized into the ring | Not resonance-delocalized into a ring |
  | Appreciably deprotonated by dilute aqueous NaOH? | Yes (forms sodium phenoxide) | No, only a small fraction |
  | Reacts with NaHCO₃? | No (too weak an acid) | No |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Phenol has a higher molecular weight:** Molecular weight has essentially no direct effect on acidity. Acidity depends on how stable the conjugate base is after losing a proton. Phenol (MW 94) is more acidic than ethanol (MW 46) not because of mass, but because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilized.

**C. Phenol has more hydrogen atoms available for donation:** The number of hydrogen atoms is not what determines acid strength. Acidity is about how readily a single O-H proton is released and how stable the resulting anion is. Phenol has fewer total H atoms than many less acidic molecules.

**D. The ethoxide ion is more resonance-stabilized than the phenoxide ion:** The comparison is reversed. Phenoxide can delocalize its negative charge into the aromatic ring; ethoxide cannot. The greater stabilization of phenoxide helps make phenol more acidic.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Acidity is assessed by the equilibrium for proton loss in the specified environment.
  - Phenoxide is resonance-stabilized; ethoxide lacks comparable delocalization into an aromatic ring.
  - Phenol is therefore much more acidic than ethanol and is appreciably deprotonated by dilute aqueous NaOH.

>> Bottom Line: Acidity depends on the stability of the conjugate base. More stable conjugate base = stronger acid. Resonance delocalization and electron-withdrawing inductive effects can stabilize a negatively charged conjugate base. Phenol is acidic enough to react with NaOH but not with NaHCO₃ (unlike carboxylic acids, which react with both).

  B) Tollens' test (silver mirror reaction)

  D) Iodine test

Some other compounds, including reducing sugars, can also give positive results in these oxidation tests.

**Tollens' test reaction:**

R-CHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3OH⁻ → R-COO⁻ + 2Ag(s) + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O

Ethanal is oxidized to a carboxylate; Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver. Both sides of this equation carry a net charge of -1.

For the pair named here, both ethanal and propanone can give a positive iodoform result, so it would not separate them.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Silver(I) is reduced to metallic silver, producing the positive test.
  - Propanone does not react under the same conditions, although some other carbonyl compounds can give positive oxidation tests.

>> Bottom Line: Ethanal reduces Tollens' reagent to metallic silver as it is oxidized to a carboxylate. Propanone does not under the standard test conditions. This compares ease of oxidation, not 'reducibility' of the carbonyl compounds.

The loss of a protein's three-dimensional structure due to heat or extreme pH, without breaking peptide bonds, is called:

  A) Hydrolysis
  B) Denaturation
  C) Polymerization
  D) Saponification

### Key Educational Points:

In the reaction 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, if 4 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂, which is the limiting reagent?

  A) H₂
  B) O₂
  C) H₂O
  D) Neither, both are exactly consumed

### Correct Answer: O₂

The stoichiometric ratio requires 2 mol H₂ per 1 mol O₂. With 4 mol H₂, we need 2 mol O₂, but only 1 mol is available.

  | Reagent | Available | Needed | Status |
  | H₂ | 4 mol | 2 mol (for 1 mol O₂) | Excess (2 mol left over) |
  | O₂ | 1 mol | 2 mol (for 4 mol H₂) | Limiting |

Only 1 mol O₂ reacts with 2 mol H₂, producing 2 mol H₂O. The remaining 2 mol H₂ are unreacted.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. H₂:** The available oxygen reacts with only 2 mol of the 4 mol of H₂. Hydrogen therefore remains after oxygen is exhausted, so H₂ is the excess reactant, not the limiting reagent.

**C. H₂O:** H₂O is the product of this reaction, not a reactant. The limiting reagent must be one of the reactants (H₂ or O₂): it is the reactant that runs out first, limiting how much product can form.

**D. Neither, both are exactly consumed:** For both reactants to be exactly consumed, they would need to be in the 2:1 stoichiometric ratio (2 mol H₂ per 1 mol O₂). With 4 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂, the ratio is 4:1, so H₂ is in excess. Only 1 mol O₂ can react (with 2 mol H₂), leaving 2 mol H₂ unreacted. So the answer is not "both consumed"; O₂ is the limiting reagent.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The limiting reagent determines the maximum amount of product.
  - One mole of O₂ reacts with two moles of H₂, producing two moles of H₂O.
  - Starting with four moles of H₂ therefore leaves two moles of H₂ after the oxygen is consumed.

>> Bottom Line: The limiting reagent is consumed first and determines the maximum yield. To find it, compare the mole ratio of available reagents to the stoichiometric ratio.

What happens when the concentration of a dissolved product is increased at constant temperature and volume, with the other concentrations initially unchanged? Assume the mixture was initially at equilibrium.

  A) Equilibrium shifts toward more products
  B) Equilibrium shifts toward reactants (to the left)
  C) The equilibrium constant K changes
  D) Nothing happens; the system is already at equilibrium

### Correct Answer: Equilibrium shifts toward reactants (to the left)

According to Le Chatelier's principle, adding product shifts equilibrium to consume it, moving toward reactants.

For species whose concentrations appear in the equilibrium expression:

  | Change | Shift Direction |
  | Add reactant | Right (toward products) |
  | Add product | Left (toward reactants) |
  | Remove product | Right (toward products) |
  | Remove reactant | Left (toward reactants) |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Equilibrium shifts toward more products:** Adding more product causes the system to shift away from products (left), not toward them. Le Chatelier's principle states that the system counteracts the disturbance -- if product concentration increases, the reverse reaction speeds up to consume the excess.

**C. The equilibrium constant K changes:** The equilibrium constant K is determined only by temperature. Changes in concentration shift the position of equilibrium (the reaction quotient Q changes) but do not alter K itself. The system adjusts concentrations until Q equals K again.

**D. Nothing happens; the system is already at equilibrium:** Adding product disrupts the equilibrium: the system is no longer at equilibrium because Q is now greater than K. The system responds by net reverse reaction until Q = K is restored.

### Key Educational Points:

  - At equilibrium, the reaction quotient Q equals the equilibrium constant K.
  - Increasing a product concentration in the equilibrium expression raises Q, favoring net reverse reaction.
  - At fixed temperature, the concentrations adjust while K remains unchanged.

>> Bottom Line: Increasing the concentration of a product that appears in the equilibrium expression raises Q above K, so net reverse reaction occurs until equilibrium is restored. At fixed temperature, K does not change.

  - Resonance delocalization reduces nitrogen basicity in aniline and especially in amides.
  - Among the listed compounds in water, diethylamine is the strongest base.

>> Bottom Line: For these compounds in water: diethylamine > ammonia > aniline > acetamide in basicity. Resonance delocalization reduces the availability of the nitrogen lone pair; solvent effects also matter.

At pH 7.4, free glycine in aqueous solution predominantly exists as:

  A) Uncharged molecules
  B) Zwitterions (dipolar ions) with both positive and negative charges
  C) Anions only (negatively charged)
  D) Cations only (positively charged)

### Correct Answer: Zwitterions (dipolar ions) with both positive and negative charges

Glycine has an amino group and a carboxyl group, with no ionizable side chain. At pH 7.4 its predominant form is H₃N⁺-CH₂-COO⁻. The amino group is mainly protonated and the carboxyl group mainly deprotonated. This species is a zwitterion: it carries positive and negative formal charges but has zero net charge. Other amino acids may carry additional side-chain charges, so the same net-charge conclusion does not apply to all of them.

  | Condition | Predominant glycine form | Charge of that form |
  | Well below the carboxyl pKₐ (about 2.3) | H₃N⁺-CH₂-COOH | +1 |
  | Between the two pKₐ values, including pH 7.4 | H₃N⁺-CH₂-COO⁻ | 0 |
  | Well above the ammonium pKₐ (about 9.6) | H₂N-CH₂-COO⁻ | -1 |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Uncharged molecules:** The predominant species is not the completely un-ionized NH₂-CH₂-COOH form. Glycine mainly contains both NH₃⁺ and COO⁻ groups at this pH, even though the predominant species has zero net charge.

**C. Anions only (negatively charged):** The glycine species with net charge -1 becomes predominant when pH is raised above the amino group's pKₐ, about 9.6. At pH 7.4 the amino group is still mainly NH₃⁺, so the zwitterion predominates.

**D. Cations only (positively charged):** The glycine species with net charge +1 predominates at sufficiently low pH, when the carboxyl group is mainly COOH. At pH 7.4 the carboxyl group is mainly COO⁻, balancing the positive amino-group charge.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Glycine's predominant form at pH 7.4 contains both NH₃⁺ and COO⁻ groups.
  - A zwitterion has opposite formal charges within one species but zero net charge.
  - Other amino acids can carry additional side-chain charges, so their net charge must be assessed individually.

>> Bottom Line: At pH 7.4, glycine is predominantly H₃N⁺-CH₂-COO⁻. Its isoelectric point is about pH 6, where the population's average net charge is zero; that is not the same as saying zwitterions occur only at pI. For other amino acids, also consider ionizable side chains.

What makes a typical phospholipid in a human cell membrane amphipathic?

  A) They contain only saturated fatty acids
  B) They have both a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails
  C) They are soluble in both acids and bases
  D) They can be either solid or liquid at room temperature

### Correct Answer: They have both a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails

A typical two-tailed membrane phospholipid:

  | Region | Chemical Nature | Interaction with Water |
  | Phosphate head group | Polar, charged | Hydrophilic (faces water) |
  | Hydrocarbon tails (two, in a typical membrane phospholipid) | Nonpolar hydrocarbon | Hydrophobic (faces inward) |

This dual nature causes phospholipids to spontaneously form bilayers in water, with heads facing out and tails facing in, a major basis of the lipid bilayers in human cell membranes.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. They contain only saturated fatty acids:** Phospholipid tails may be saturated or unsaturated. Saturation influences packing and fluidity, but amphipathic describes the coexistence of a hydrophilic region and hydrophobic regions in one molecule.

**C. They are soluble in both acids and bases:** Amphoteric means able to react as either an acid or a base; it does not simply mean soluble in both acids and bases. Amphipathic instead describes a molecule with both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.

**D. They can be either solid or liquid at room temperature:** Physical state at room temperature depends on the degree of saturation and chain length of the fatty acids. Amphipathic specifically means having dual solubility character -- a polar head group that interacts with water and nonpolar tails that avoid water -- which is why phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Amphipathic means having both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.
  - A typical membrane phospholipid has a polar head and nonpolar hydrocarbon tails.
  - Bilayer formation places the heads next to water and shelters the tails inside.

>> Bottom Line: A typical membrane phospholipid has a polar head and nonpolar tails. In water, many two-tailed phospholipids assemble into bilayers with the tails sheltered inside. Other amphipathic molecules may form different structures, such as micelles.

Which polysaccharide serves as the primary energy storage molecule in animals?

  A) Cellulose
  B) Starch
  C) Glycogen
  D) Chitin

### Correct Answer: Glycogen

  | Polysaccharide | Monomer | Function | Found In |
  | Starch | Glucose (α) | Energy storage | Plants |
  | Glycogen | Glucose (α) | Energy storage | Animals (liver, muscle) |
  | Cellulose | Glucose (β) | Structural | Plant cell walls |
  | Chitin | N-acetylglucosamine | Structural | Arthropod exoskeletons |

Glycogen is more branched than starch, allowing faster mobilization of glucose when energy is needed.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Cellulose:** Cellulose is a structural polysaccharide in plants, forming cell walls. It consists of glucose units linked by beta-1,4 glycosidic bonds, which humans cannot digest due to lacking the enzyme cellulase. It provides structural support, not energy storage.

**B. Starch:** Starch is the energy storage polysaccharide in plants, not animals. It consists of amylose (unbranched, alpha-1,4 links) and amylopectin (branched). Glycogen is the animal equivalent -- it is structurally similar to amylopectin but much more highly branched for rapid glucose mobilization.

**D. Chitin:** Chitin is a structural polysaccharide made of N-acetylglucosamine units. It forms the exoskeletons of arthropods (insects, crustaceans) and the cell walls of fungi. It serves a structural role, not energy storage.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Glycogen is the storage polysaccharide of animals; starch performs the same role in plants.
  - It is a glucose polymer with α-1,4 chains and α-1,6 branch points roughly every 8 to 12 residues.
  - Heavy branching creates many non-reducing ends, so glucose can be released from many points at once - this is what makes mobilisation fast.
  - Glycogen is stored mainly in liver and skeletal muscle. Liver glycogen helps maintain blood glucose, whereas skeletal muscle uses its glycogen locally because it lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.
  - Cellulose is also a glucose polymer, but its β-1,4 linkages make it structural and indigestible to humans.

>> Bottom Line: Glycogen is the main animal storage polysaccharide, with alpha-1,4 chains and alpha-1,6 branches. Human enzymes can digest starch and mobilize glycogen, but humans lack cellulase to cleave cellulose's beta-1,4 glucose linkages.

In a DNA double helix, if 30% of the bases are adenine, what percentage are cytosine?

  A) 10%
  B) 20%
  C) 30%
  D) 40%

### Correct Answer: 20%

Chargaff's rules: A = T and G = C

If A = 30%, then T = 30%

A + T = 60%, so G + C = 40%

Since G = C: each = 40%/2 = **20%**

  | Base | Percentage | Pairs With |
  | Adenine (A) | 30% | Thymine (T) |
  | Thymine (T) | 30% | Adenine (A) |
  | Cytosine (C) | 20% | Guanine (G) |
  | Guanine (G) | 20% | Cytosine (C) |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 10%:** If A = 30%, then T = 30% (Chargaff's rule: A pairs with T). That accounts for 60% of bases. The remaining 40% is split equally between G and C (since G pairs with C), giving G = C = 20%, not 10%.

**C. 30%:** 30% for cytosine would mean G is also 30%, totaling A + T + G + C = 30 + 30 + 30 + 30 = 120%, which exceeds 100%. With A = T = 30% using 60%, only 40% remains for G + C, giving C = 20%.

**D. 40%:** If C were 40%, then G would also be 40% (by Chargaff's rule), totaling 80% for G + C. Added to A + T = 60%, the total would be 140%. The correct split is: A = T = 30% and G = C = 20%, totaling exactly 100%.

### Key Educational Points:

  - In standard complementary double-stranded DNA, A = T and G = C across both strands.
  - If A is 30%, T is also 30%, leaving 40% for G and C together.
  - Dividing that remainder equally gives 20% cytosine and 20% guanine.

>> Bottom Line: For standard complementary double-stranded DNA, A = T and G = C when both strands are counted together. These equalities are not required for an individual strand or a single-stranded genome. A-T pairs have two hydrogen bonds and G-C pairs have three.

Pyrrole is a five-membered aromatic heterocyclic compound containing one nitrogen atom. It is a structural component of which biologically important molecule?

  A) DNA
  B) ATP
  C) Hemoglobin (heme group)
  D) Cholesterol

### Correct Answer: Hemoglobin (heme group)

The heme group in hemoglobin contains a **porphyrin ring**, which is made of four pyrrole rings linked by methine bridges, with an iron (Fe²⁺) ion at the center.

  | Heterocycle | Ring | Biological Role |
  | Pyrrole-derived units | Five-membered, one N; reduction/substitution varies in derivatives | Heme porphyrin; related tetrapyrrole frameworks in chlorophyll and vitamin B12 |
  | Pyridine | 6-membered, 1 N | NAD⁺, vitamin B3 |
  | Pyrimidine | 6-membered, 2 N | Bases C, T, U |
  | Purine | Fused 5+6 rings | Bases A, G; caffeine |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. DNA:** DNA contains purine and pyrimidine bases, not pyrrole rings. The nucleotide bases in DNA (adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine) are six-membered pyrimidines or fused pyrimidine-imidazole purines. Pyrrole is a different five-membered heterocycle.

**B. ATP:** ATP (adenosine triphosphate) contains adenine (a purine base), ribose sugar, and three phosphate groups. While adenine has a five-membered imidazole ring fused to pyrimidine, it is not a pyrrole. Pyrrole is a five-membered ring with one nitrogen; the porphyrin framework is built from four pyrrole-derived units.

**D. Cholesterol:** Cholesterol is a steroid lipid built from four fused carbon rings (three six-membered and one five-membered cyclopentane ring). It contains no nitrogen and no pyrrole units. Cholesterol is important for membrane fluidity and is a precursor to steroid hormones.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The heme group contains iron coordinated by a porphyrin framework.
  - A porphyrin consists of four pyrrole-derived units linked into a larger ring.
  - Vitamin B12 contains a related but different corrin ring, not a porphyrin.

>> Bottom Line: Heme contains iron coordinated by a porphyrin ring. Chlorophyll a and b contain magnesium in a related chlorin-type ring, while vitamin B12 contains cobalt in a corrin ring. These are related tetrapyrrole frameworks, not three identical porphyrins.

==============================================================================
Moving from left to right across a period in the periodic table, which of the following generally increases?

  A) Atomic radius
  B) Metallic character
  C) Electronegativity
  D) Number of electron shells

### Correct Answer: Electronegativity

Across a typical main-group period, increasing effective nuclear charge strengthens attraction for bonding electrons, while electrons are added within the same outer shell.

  | Property | Across Period → | Down Group ↓ |
  | Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
  | Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
  | Ionization energy | Increases | Decreases |
  | Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Atomic radius:** Atomic radius actually decreases from left to right across a period. As protons are added to the nucleus (increasing nuclear charge) without adding new electron shells, the electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus. This is one of the most commonly tested periodic trends.

**B. Metallic character:** Metallic character decreases from left to right across a period. Elements on the left are metals that easily lose electrons; moving right, elements become progressively better at gaining electrons (more nonmetallic). This parallels the increase in electronegativity.

**D. Number of electron shells:** Across a typical main-group period, electrons are added within the same outer principal shell. Moving down a group introduces a new outer shell. All elements in period 3, for example, have 3 occupied electron shells.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Electronegativity describes an atom's attraction for shared electrons in a bond.
  - It generally increases from left to right across a main-group period.
  - Increasing effective nuclear charge helps explain this trend and the usual decrease in atomic radius.

>> Bottom Line: Electronegativity measures an atom's ability to attract shared electrons. Fluorine (3.98) is the most electronegative element. Noble gases are generally not assigned electronegativity values.

According to Dalton's law of partial pressures, a mixture contains O₂ at 0.40 atm and N₂ at 0.55 atm in a 10 L vessel. What is the total pressure?

  A) 0.95 atm
  B) 0.15 atm
  C) 1.10 atm
  D) 0.40 atm

### Correct Answer: A. 0.95 atm

Dalton's law states that the total pressure of a gas mixture equals the sum of the partial pressures of each component: Ptotal = PO2 + PN2 = 0.40 + 0.55 = 0.95 atm.

Each gas in a mixture behaves independently and exerts its own partial pressure as if it were alone in the container.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. 0.15 atm:** 0.15 atm is the difference between the two partial pressures (0.55 - 0.40), but Dalton's law requires addition of partial pressures, not subtraction. Ptotal = PO2 + PN2 = 0.40 + 0.55 = 0.95 atm.

**C. 1.10 atm:** 1.10 atm might result from doubling one of the values or adding incorrectly. The straightforward sum is 0.40 + 0.55 = 0.95 atm. Each gas contributes independently to the total pressure.

**D. 0.40 atm:** 0.40 atm is just the partial pressure of O2 alone. Dalton's law states total pressure is the sum of all partial pressures in the mixture: Ptotal = 0.40 + 0.55 = 0.95 atm. You must include the contribution of every gas present.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Dalton's law: total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures, so 0.40 + 0.55 = 0.95 atm.
  - Each component exerts its pressure independently, because ideal gas molecules are assumed not to interact.
  - The 10 L volume is not needed: both gases already occupy that same volume, so it is common to both terms.
  - Recognising which given data are irrelevant is part of the skill - not every number in a stem is required.
  - Expressed as a fraction rather than a sum, the same law gives P_i = x_i × P_total.

>> Bottom Line: Total pressure equals the sum of partial pressures of all gases in a mixture (Dalton's Law).
