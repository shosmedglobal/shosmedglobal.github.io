Q1  [chem-242]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: The hybridization of carbon atoms in ethyne (C₂H₂) is:

  A. sp³
  B. sp²
  C. sp   <= key
  D. sp³d

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. sp
In ethyne, each carbon forms one triple bond (one σ and two π bonds) and one single bond to hydrogen. This requires two hybrid orbitals, corresponding to sp hybridization. The bond angle is 180°, giving ethyne a linear geometry.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. sp³: sp3 hybridization gives a tetrahedral geometry with four σ bonds and no π bonds. In ethyne, each carbon forms only two σ bonds (one C-H and one C-C) and two π bonds, requiring only two hybrid orbitals. The two unhybridized p orbitals form the two π bonds of the triple bond.
B. sp²: sp2 hybridization gives a trigonal planar geometry with three σ bonds and one π bond, as seen in ethene (C2H4). Ethyne has a triple bond requiring two π bonds, so carbon needs two unhybridized p orbitals. Only sp hybridization (two hybrid orbitals + two unhybridized p orbitals) satisfies this.
D. sp³d: In the usual hybridization model, each carbon in ethyne uses two sp orbitals for its sigma bonds and retains two unhybridized p orbitals for the two pi bonds. An sp³d description does not fit this carbon bonding arrangement.
Key Educational Points:
  - Each carbon in ethyne has two directions of sigma bonding and is described as sp-hybridized.
  - A carbon–carbon triple bond contains one sigma bond and two pi bonds.
  - Ethyne is linear, with bond angles of 180°.

Q2  [chem-243]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which alkyne gives acetone (propan-2-one) on hydration with water, dilute sulfuric acid and a mercury(II) sulfate catalyst?

  A. Ethyne
  B. Propyne   <= key
  C. But-1-yne
  D. But-2-yne

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Propyne
Under these conditions, water adds to propyne to form an enol, CH₃C(OH)=CH₂. Proton transfer and rearrangement of the double bond then convert this enol into the more stable carbonyl compound, CH₃COCH₃, acetone. This enol-to-carbonyl change is called keto–enol tautomerism. The three-carbon skeleton is retained.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Ethyne: Ethyne gives the enol CH₂=CHOH, which changes into ethanal, CH₃CHO. Ethyne has only two carbon atoms, so hydration cannot give the three-carbon product acetone.
C. But-1-yne: But-1-yne gives butan-2-one, CH₃COCH₂CH₃, under these hydration conditions. Its four-carbon skeleton is retained, so the product is not acetone.
D. But-2-yne: But-2-yne is a symmetrical four-carbon alkyne. Hydration and tautomerism give butan-2-one, the same ketone obtained here from but-1-yne, rather than three-carbon acetone.
Key Educational Points:
  - Mercury(II)-catalyzed hydration of an alkyne first produces an enol that changes into a carbonyl compound.
  - Propyne gives acetone; count carbon atoms to check the product.
  - Ethyne gives ethanal, whereas both but-1-yne and but-2-yne give butan-2-one under these conditions.

Q3  [chem-244]  correct answer: A
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: What is the product when 2-butene reacts with ozone (O₃) followed by reductive workup with Zn/H₂O?

  A. 2 molecules of acetaldehyde   <= key
  B. 1 molecule of formaldehyde + 1 molecule of propanal
  C. 2 molecules of formaldehyde
  D. 1 molecule of butanal

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: A. 2 molecules of acetaldehyde
Ozonolysis cleaves the double bond. 2-Butene (CH₃CH=CHCH₃) has the double bond between C2 and C3. Cleavage produces two fragments: CH₃CHO (acetaldehyde) from each side. Reductive workup with Zn prevents further oxidation to carboxylic acids.
Why the other choices are wrong:
B. 1 molecule of formaldehyde + 1 molecule of propanal: 1 molecule of formaldehyde + 1 molecule of propanal would result from ozonolysis of 1-butene (CH2=CHCH2CH3), where the double bond is between C1 and C2. In 2-butene, the double bond is symmetrically placed between C2 and C3, so the cleavage produces two identical fragments (CH3CHO, acetaldehyde).
C. 2 molecules of formaldehyde: 2 molecules of formaldehyde would result from ozonolysis of ethene (CH2=CH2), where each carbon of the double bond has two hydrogens. For 2-butene (CH3CH=CHCH3), each carbon of the double bond bears a methyl group, yielding acetaldehyde (CH3CHO) rather than formaldehyde (HCHO).
D. 1 molecule of butanal: Butanal retains all four carbon atoms in one chain. Reductive ozonolysis of this acyclic alkene cleaves its central C=C bond, giving two molecules of ethanal instead. Cleavage of a cyclic alkene can leave both carbonyl groups in one molecule, so two separate products are not a universal rule.
Key Educational Points:
  - Reductive ozonolysis converts the two carbons of a C=C bond into carbonyl carbons.
  - Each double-bond carbon in but-2-ene bears a hydrogen, so the products are aldehydes.
  - One molecule of but-2-ene produces two molecules of ethanal, preserving the total of four carbon atoms.

Q4  [chem-245]  correct answer: A
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: What is the conventional order used to outline the stages of a productive free-radical chlorination chain?

  A. Initiation → Propagation → Termination   <= key
  B. Propagation → Initiation → Termination
  C. Termination → Propagation → Initiation
  D. Initiation → Termination → Propagation

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: A. Initiation → Propagation → Termination
A radical chain is conventionally described as initiation, propagation and termination. Light can initiate methane chlorination by splitting Cl₂ into two chlorine radicals. During propagation, Cl· + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃·, followed by CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·. The regenerated chlorine radical can continue the chain. Termination removes radicals, for example when two Cl· radicals combine. These processes can occur at the same time in a reaction mixture; the sequence is a way of describing a productive chain.
Why the other choices are wrong:
B. Propagation → Initiation → Termination: Propagation requires radicals to be present. In the conventional description, initiation explains their formation before the chain-carrying propagation steps are introduced.
C. Termination → Propagation → Initiation: Termination describes removal of radicals, not their initial generation. Starting an outline with termination therefore does not describe how a productive radical chain is established.
D. Initiation → Termination → Propagation: Putting termination before propagation omits the repeated steps that produce chloromethane and regenerate a radical. An individual newly formed radical can nevertheless terminate without first propagating; the listed stages are not a compulsory timeline for every radical.
Key Educational Points:
  - Initiation generates radicals, such as by light-induced homolytic cleavage of Cl₂.
  - Propagation consumes a radical and generates another radical, allowing the chain to continue.
  - Termination removes radicals; it can occur while other chains are still propagating.

Q5  [chem-246]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which factor helps explain why cyclopropane undergoes ring-opening reactions more readily than cyclohexane?

  A. It has fewer hydrogen atoms
  B. It has more π electrons
  C. Its C–C bonds have significant angle strain   <= key
  D. It is aromatic

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. Its C–C bonds have significant angle strain
The three-membered ring of cyclopropane forces its carbon framework into angles of about 60°, far from the preferred tetrahedral angle of about 109.5°. It also has torsional strain from eclipsing C–H bonds. Opening the ring can relieve this strain. Cyclohexane can adopt a chair conformation with bond angles close to tetrahedral and largely staggered bonds, so it has much less ring strain.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. It has fewer hydrogen atoms: Having fewer hydrogen atoms does not make a cycloalkane more reactive. Cyclopropane has 6 hydrogens (C3H6) compared to cyclohexane's 12 (C6H12), but the difference in reactivity comes from ring strain, not hydrogen count. The 60-degree bond angles in cyclopropane deviate enormously from the ideal sp3 angle of 109.5 degrees.
B. It has more π electrons: Cyclopropane has no pi electrons; it contains only σ bonds. Pi electrons are found in unsaturated compounds like alkenes and aromatic rings. Cyclopropane's high reactivity stems from the severe angle strain forcing its C-C bonds to bend, creating "banana bonds" that store extra energy and make the ring eager to open.
D. It is aromatic: Cyclopropane is not an ordinary aromatic π system. Its tendency to undergo ring opening is associated with ring strain, including angle and torsional strain, rather than aromatic stabilization.
Key Educational Points:
  - Cyclopropane has substantial angle strain because its ring angles are about 60°.
  - Eclipsing C–H bonds also contribute torsional strain.
  - Chair cyclohexane has much less strain; opening a strained ring can be energetically favorable.

Q6  [chem-247]  correct answer: D
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which of the following compounds has the highest normal boiling point, measured at 1 atm?

  A. Pentane
  B. 2-methylbutane
  C. 2,2-dimethylpropane
  D. Cyclopentane   <= key

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: D. Cyclopentane
Cyclopentane has the highest normal boiling point in this set, about 49 °C. The approximate values for pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane are 36, 28 and 10 °C, respectively. Among those three C₅H₁₂ isomers, greater branching is associated with weaker overall intermolecular attractions and a lower boiling point. Cyclopentane has the different formula C₅H₁₀, so that branching comparison alone does not establish its position. Molecular shape affects intermolecular interactions, but cyclic compounds do not universally boil above acyclic compounds.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Pentane: Pentane is the straight-chain C₅H₁₂ isomer and boils at about 36 °C. It boils above its two branched isomers, but below cyclopentane at about 49 °C.
B. 2-methylbutane: 2-Methylbutane is a branched C₅H₁₂ isomer with a normal boiling point of about 28 °C. It is therefore below both pentane and cyclopentane.
C. 2,2-dimethylpropane: 2,2-Dimethylpropane is the most compact of the three C₅H₁₂ isomers and boils at about 10 °C. It has the lowest, not the highest, boiling point in this set.
Key Educational Points:
  - Normal boiling points are compared at the same pressure, 1 atm.
  - Within the three C₅H₁₂ isomers here, increasing branching lowers the boiling point.
  - Cyclopentane boils at about 49 °C and has formula C₅H₁₀, not C₅H₁₂.

Q7  [chem-248]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Treatment of propyne with sodium amide (NaNH₂) in liquid ammonia followed by methyl iodide (CH₃I) gives:

  A. Butane
  B. But-1-yne
  C. But-2-yne   <= key
  D. Pent-2-yne

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. But-2-yne
NaNH₂ is a strong enough base to deprotonate the terminal alkyne hydrogen of propyne (CH₃C≡CH), forming the acetylide anion CH₃C≡C−Na⁺. This nucleophile then undergoes SN2 reaction with CH₃I to give CH₃C≡CCH₃ (but-2-yne).
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Butane: Butane (C4H10) is a fully saturated alkane with no triple bond. The reaction of a terminal alkyne with NaNH2 creates an acetylide anion (a carbon nucleophile), which then attacks methyl iodide in an SN2 reaction. This preserves the triple bond, so the product must be an alkyne, not an alkane.
B. But-1-yne: But-1-yne (CH3CH2C triple bond CH) would require adding an ethyl group to acetylene, not a methyl group to propyne. Starting from propyne (CH3C triple bond CH), the NaNH2 removes the terminal H to give CH3C triple bond C- Na+, which reacts with CH3I to give CH3C triple bond C-CH3 (but-2-yne), an internal alkyne.
D. Pent-2-yne: Pent-2-yne (CH3C triple bond CCH2CH3) has five carbons. Since propyne has 3 carbons and methyl iodide adds 1 carbon, the product can only have 4 carbons total. Carbon count is a quick way to eliminate wrong answers in synthesis problems.
Key Educational Points:
  - NaNH₂ removes the terminal hydrogen from propyne to form an acetylide ion.
  - The acetylide attacks methyl iodide by an SN2 reaction, forming a new C–C bond.
  - Adding one methyl carbon to three-carbon propyne produces four-carbon but-2-yne.

Q8  [chem-249]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: For a planar, monocyclic, fully conjugated π system in its ground state, Hückel's rule predicts aromaticity when it contains:

  A. 4n π electrons
  B. (4n + 2) π electrons   <= key
  C. 4n + 1 π electrons
  D. Any even number of π electrons

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. (4n + 2) π electrons
Hückel's rule applies to a planar ring with a continuous set of overlapping p orbitals. A closed-shell system with 4n + 2 π electrons, where n is a nonnegative integer, is aromatic. The allowed counts begin 2, 6, 10 and 14. A planar, fully conjugated ring with 4n π electrons is antiaromatic. If a ring avoids planarity or lacks continuous conjugation, it may instead be nonaromatic. Electron counting alone is not enough.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. 4n π electrons: A 4n π-electron count corresponds to antiaromaticity when the ring is also planar and fully conjugated. A nonplanar ring with that count, such as cyclooctatetraene, is nonaromatic rather than antiaromatic.
C. 4n + 1 π electrons: 4n + 1 is an odd number of pi electrons, which does not correspond to any standard aromaticity rule. Aromatic stability requires an even number of pi electrons following the (4n + 2) formula, where n = 0, 1, 2, 3... giving 2, 6, 10 and 14 pi electrons for a monocyclic ring. Benzene has 6, with n = 1.
D. Any even number of π electrons: Simply having an even number of pi electrons does not guarantee aromaticity. For example, cyclooctatetraene has 8 pi electrons (an even number), but 8 does not fit the (4n + 2) formula. Instead, 8 = 4(2), making it anti-aromatic if planar. Cyclooctatetraene avoids this by adopting a non-planar tub shape.
Key Educational Points:
  - Apply Hückel's rule to a planar, cyclic, continuously conjugated π system.
  - The aromatic electron counts are 4n + 2, with n = 0, 1, 2 and so on.
  - A 4n count is antiaromatic only when the other structural conditions are met.

Q9  [chem-250]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which substituent is an ortho/para director in electrophilic aromatic substitution?

  A. -NO₂
  B. -COOH
  C. -OH   <= key
  D. -CHO

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. -OH
The hydroxyl group (-OH) is an activating group that donates electron density to the ring through resonance (lone pairs on oxygen). This increases electron density at the ortho and para positions, directing incoming electrophiles there. -NO₂, -COOH, and -CHO are all electron-withdrawing and are meta directors.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. -NO₂: A nitro group withdraws electron density strongly through resonance and induction. It deactivates the benzene ring and favors meta substitution, unlike the activating hydroxyl group.
B. -COOH: A carboxyl group attached through its carbonyl carbon withdraws electron density from the aromatic ring. It is deactivating and meta-directing rather than activating and ortho/para-directing.
D. -CHO: The aldehyde group, –CHO, withdraws electron density through its carbonyl group. It deactivates the ring and favors meta substitution.
Key Educational Points:
  - A hydroxyl group can donate a lone pair into a benzene ring by resonance.
  - This donation activates the ring and favors substitution at the ortho and para positions.
  - –NO₂, –COOH and –CHO are deactivating meta directors in the examples here.

Q10  [chem-251]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Nitration of benzene uses a mixture of concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄. The active electrophile is:

  A. NO₂⁻
  B. NO₃⁻
  C. NO₂⁺   <= key
  D. NO⁺

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. NO₂⁺
Concentrated sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, which then loses water to generate the nitronium ion (NO₂⁺). This powerful electrophile attacks the electron-rich benzene ring in the rate-determining step of electrophilic aromatic substitution.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. NO₂⁻: NO2- (nitrite ion) is a nucleophile, not an electrophile. Electrophilic aromatic substitution requires an electron-poor species that attacks the electron-rich benzene ring. The nitronium ion (NO2+) is generated when concentrated H2SO4 protonates HNO3, causing it to lose water and form the powerful electrophile.
B. NO₃⁻: Nitrate, NO₃⁻, is the conjugate base of nitric acid. It is not the electrophile that attacks the ring in nitration. The active electrophile is the positively charged nitronium ion, NO₂⁺.
D. NO⁺: NO+ (nitrosonium ion) is an electrophile but is involved in nitrosation reactions, not nitration. Nitrosation typically occurs with nitrous acid (HNO2) and is important in diazonium salt formation from amines. The nitration of benzene specifically uses the nitronium ion NO2+, which adds -NO2 to the ring.
Key Educational Points:
  - The electrophile in standard mixed-acid nitration is NO₂⁺, the nitronium ion.
  - Sulfuric acid helps generate NO₂⁺ from nitric acid.
  - Nitration replaces a ring hydrogen with –NO₂ and restores aromaticity after the substitution.

Q11  [chem-252]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Toluene undergoes electrophilic bromination faster than benzene because the methyl group is:

  A. An electron-withdrawing group by induction
  B. An electron-donating group by hyperconjugation and induction   <= key
  C. A meta director
  D. A deactivating substituent

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. An electron-donating group by hyperconjugation and induction
The methyl group donates electron density to the benzene ring through both positive inductive effect (+I) and hyperconjugation. This increases the electron density of the ring, making it more nucleophilic and thus more reactive toward electrophiles. The methyl group is an activating, ortho/para-directing substituent.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. An electron-withdrawing group by induction: A methyl group is treated as electron-donating in this aromatic-substitution comparison. Its inductive effect and hyperconjugation help stabilize the positively charged reaction intermediate; it does not deactivate the ring by withdrawing electron density.
C. A meta director: A meta director directs incoming electrophiles preferentially to the meta position. Electron-withdrawing groups like -NO2, -CN, and -COOH are meta directors. The methyl group, being electron-donating, is an ortho/para director. It activates the ortho and para positions by increasing electron density at those sites through hyperconjugation.
D. A deactivating substituent: The methyl group activates the aromatic ring toward electrophilic bromination compared with benzene. It also favors ortho and para substitution. Activation describes the effect on reaction rate; directing describes the preferred position.
Key Educational Points:
  - A methyl substituent activates benzene toward electrophilic aromatic substitution.
  - Hyperconjugation and electron donation help stabilize the reaction intermediate.
  - Methyl is an ortho/para director; rate effects and positional effects are related but distinct ideas.

Q12  [chem-253]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: What is the initial monosubstitution product when benzene reacts with CH₃Cl in the presence of AlCl₃?

  A. Chlorobenzene
  B. Toluene   <= key
  C. Benzaldehyde
  D. Phenol

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Toluene
This Friedel-Crafts alkylation replaces a hydrogen on benzene with a methyl group, giving toluene. AlCl₃ acts as a Lewis acid and activates CH₃Cl by strongly polarizing its C–Cl bond. The ring attacks the activated methyl group; a freely existing methyl carbocation need not be invoked. Toluene is more reactive than benzene toward further alkylation, so additional substitution can occur if conditions allow it.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Chlorobenzene: Chlorobenzene results from introducing chlorine onto the ring, for example with Cl₂ and a suitable Lewis acid. CH₃Cl/AlCl₃ transfers a methyl group in this reaction; it does not chlorinate benzene.
C. Benzaldehyde: Benzaldehyde contains a formyl group, –CHO, but this reaction introduces –CH₃. A different formylation method is needed to introduce –CHO; formyl chloride is unstable and is not used like an ordinary acyl chloride.
D. Phenol: Phenol contains an –OH group attached directly to the ring. Neither CH₃Cl nor AlCl₃ supplies an oxygen-containing group in this reaction, so phenol is not the product.
Key Educational Points:
  - Friedel-Crafts alkylation forms a bond between an aromatic ring and an alkyl group.
  - AlCl₃ activates the alkyl halide; methylation does not require a free CH₃⁺ intermediate.
  - Toluene is the first product here, but its activated ring can undergo further alkylation.

Q13  [chem-254]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which compound is NOT aromatic?

  A. Pyridine
  B. Cyclooctatetraene   <= key
  C. Furan
  D. Thiophene

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Cyclooctatetraene
Cyclooctatetraene (COT) has 8 π electrons (4n where n = 2), which makes it anti-aromatic if planar. To avoid anti-aromaticity, COT adopts a non-planar tub-shaped conformation and behaves as a non-aromatic polyene. Pyridine, furan, and thiophene are all aromatic heterocycles with 6 π electrons.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Pyridine: Pyridine has six π electrons in its aromatic ring. Its nitrogen contributes one electron from a p orbital; the nitrogen lone pair lies outside the aromatic π system and is not part of that six-electron count.
C. Furan: Furan has four π electrons from its two double bonds and two from one oxygen lone pair. The resulting six-electron conjugated ring is aromatic.
D. Thiophene: Thiophene has four π electrons from its two double bonds and two from one sulfur lone pair. This gives an aromatic six-electron π system.
Key Educational Points:
  - Cyclooctatetraene adopts a nonplanar shape and is nonaromatic.
  - A planar, fully conjugated eight-π-electron ring would be antiaromatic.
  - Pyridine, furan and thiophene each have six aromatic π electrons, but their lone pairs contribute differently.

Q14  [chem-255]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: During mononitration of chlorobenzene, which product or product mixture is formed preferentially?

  A. m-nitrochlorobenzene
  B. o- and p-nitrochlorobenzene   <= key
  C. 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene
  D. Nitrobenzene

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. o- and p-nitrochlorobenzene
Chlorine is an ortho/para director despite being a ring deactivator. Although Cl withdraws electron density through its inductive effect (-I), it donates electron density through resonance (+M) from its lone pairs. The resonance effect selectively stabilizes the intermediate arenium ion at ortho and para positions. The principal products are the ortho and para isomers; this does not mean that no meta product can form.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. m-nitrochlorobenzene: m-nitrochlorobenzene would be the major product if chlorine were a meta director. However, chlorine is an ortho/para director (despite being a ring deactivator). Chlorine has lone pairs that donate electron density to the ring by resonance, favoring ortho and para positions, even though its inductive effect is electron-withdrawing.
C. 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene: 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene contains two nitro groups. The question specifies mononitration, which introduces one nitro group. Further nitration requires a separate reaction of the already deactivated nitrochlorobenzene.
D. Nitrobenzene: Nitrobenzene lacks the chlorine substituent. In the electrophilic nitration described here, a ring hydrogen is replaced by –NO₂ while the original chlorine remains attached.
Key Educational Points:
  - Chlorine deactivates a benzene ring overall through its electron-withdrawing inductive effect.
  - Lone-pair donation favors ortho and para substitution despite that deactivation.
  - Mononitration introduces one nitro group and retains the chlorine substituent.

Q15  [chem-256]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which of the following polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons has 10 π electrons?

  A. Benzene
  B. Naphthalene   <= key
  C. Anthracene
  D. Phenanthrene

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Naphthalene
Naphthalene, C₁₀H₈, has two fused six-membered rings and ten sp² carbon atoms. Each contributes one electron to its delocalized π system, giving ten π electrons in total. The shared ring atoms are counted only once. Benzene has six π electrons; anthracene and phenanthrene each have fourteen.
Why the other choices are wrong:

Correct Answer: B. (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > NH₃
In this aqueous comparison, dimethylamine is slightly more basic than methylamine, and both are more basic than ammonia. Alkyl electron donation favors proton acceptance, but water stabilizes the bases and their conjugate acids differently. The observed order therefore depends on both electronic effects and solvation, not simply on counting methyl groups.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. NH₃ > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₂NH: This sequence puts ammonia ahead of the two methyl-substituted amines. In water, methylamine and dimethylamine are stronger bases than ammonia, so that ranking is reversed.
C. CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₂NH > NH₃: Methylamine and dimethylamine are close in aqueous basicity, but dimethylamine is slightly stronger under the stated conditions. Adding an alkyl group does not provide a universal ranking for every amine in every solvent.
D. (CH₃)₃N > (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂: Trimethylamine is less basic than dimethylamine in water; the ordering shown is associated with the gas-phase comparison instead. Trimethylammonium, (CH₃)₃NH⁺, has one N–H bond and can donate a hydrogen bond. Its solvation is less favorable than that of the less substituted ammonium ions; it does not lack N–H bonds altogether.
Key Educational Points:
  - The aqueous order in this comparison is (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > NH₃.
  - Both electron donation and solvation affect amine basicity.
  - Protonating trimethylamine gives (CH₃)₃NH⁺, which has one N–H bond; gas-phase and aqueous rankings can differ.

STEM: Aniline is a weaker base than cyclohexylamine because:

The number of hydrogens on a molecule does not determine how readily the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton.   - Aniline's nitrogen lone pair can delocalize into its aromatic ring.

STEM: At its isoelectric point in aqueous solution, free glycine exists predominantly as:

  A. A cation
  B. An anion
  C. A zwitterion   <= key
  D. A molecule with no charged functional groups

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. A zwitterion
Near its isoelectric point, about pH 6, free glycine is predominantly ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻, a zwitterion. It contains both a positive and a negative charge but has zero net charge in this form. At the isoelectric point the population has zero average net charge and no net electrophoretic migration. Zero net charge does not mean that all functional groups are uncharged.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A cation: At sufficiently low pH, glycine is mainly ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COOH and has net charge +1. Near its isoelectric point, loss of the carboxyl proton makes the zwitterion predominant instead.

D. A molecule with no charged functional groups: The structure H₂N–CH₂–COOH has no charged functional groups, but it is not glycine's predominant form at its pI in water. The predominant zwitterion has charged groups whose contributions cancel.
Key Educational Points:
  - A zwitterion contains both positive and negative charges within the same molecular species.
  - Free glycine is predominantly ⁺H₃N–CH₂–COO⁻ near its pI, about pH 6.
  - The isoelectric point concerns zero average net charge, not absence of all charged groups.

Q52  [chem-293]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: The peptide bond between two amino acids is:

  A. An ester bond
  B. A glycosidic bond
  C. An amide bond   <= key
  D. A disulfide bond

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. An amide bond
A peptide bond is an amide linkage between the carboxyl-derived carbonyl carbon of one amino-acid residue and the amino nitrogen of the next. Resonance gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character, making the peptide unit approximately planar and restricting rotation. The linkage is often written –CO–NH–, but a peptide bond to proline's nitrogen has no N–H hydrogen.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. An ester bond: A carboxylic ester connects an acyl group to an oxygen-containing group, as in the fatty-acid esters of glycerol. A peptide instead contains an acyl carbon–nitrogen linkage. DNA and RNA have phosphodiester links, which are esters of phosphate and are structurally different from carboxylic esters.
B. A glycosidic bond: Glycosidic bonds involve a sugar's anomeric carbon. The common O-glycosidic links between sugars differ from the carbonyl carbon–nitrogen amide linkage in a peptide bond.
D. A disulfide bond: A disulfide bond joins two cysteine side chains through an S–S linkage and can stabilize protein structure. It does not replace the peptide bonds connecting successive residues. The backbone also contains the other covalent bonds within each amino-acid residue.
Key Educational Points:
  - Peptide bonds are amide linkages connecting amino-acid residues.
  - Resonance makes the peptide unit approximately planar and restricts rotation about its C–N bond.
  - Disulfide bonds connect cysteine side chains, whereas peptide bonds connect residues along the backbone.

The diazonium salt formed in this reaction is a versatile synthetic intermediate that can be converted to phenols, halides, nitriles (via Sandmeyer reaction), and azo dyes.

STEM: Gabriel synthesis is used to prepare:

  A. Secondary amines
  B. Tertiary amines
  C. Primary amines   <= key
  D. Quaternary ammonium salts

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. Primary amines
The Gabriel synthesis uses potassium phthalimide, which acts as a nucleophile in an SN2 reaction with a primary alkyl halide. Subsequent hydrolysis (or treatment with hydrazine - Ing-Manske procedure) releases the primary amine. This method avoids over-alkylation problems because the nitrogen in phthalimide is only monoalkylated.
Why the other choices are wrong:

The method involves potassium phthalimide reacting with a primary alkyl halide (SN2), then hydrolysis releases the primary amine. For secondary amines, other methods like reductive amination are used.
B. Tertiary amines: Tertiary amines cannot be made by Gabriel synthesis for the same reason. The phthalimide nitrogen is already bonded to two carbonyl carbons, so only one alkyl group can be introduced. Gabriel synthesis is valued precisely because it avoids the over-alkylation problem that plagues direct amine synthesis.
D. Quaternary ammonium salts: Quaternary ammonium salts contain R₄N⁺ ions, with four carbon-containing groups bonded to nitrogen; these can be alkyl or aryl groups. Gabriel synthesis instead releases a primary amine, RNH₂. A quaternary ammonium salt can be prepared by alkylating a tertiary amine.
Key Educational Points:
  - Phthalimide anion substitutes on a suitable primary alkyl halide by SN2.
  - Cleavage of the resulting N-alkyl phthalimide releases a primary amine.
  - The method avoids repeated alkylation of a free amine during the substitution step.

STEM: How many structural isomers does C₄H₁₀ have?

  B. 2   <= key
  C. 3
  D. 4

Correct Answer: B. 2
C₄H₁₀ (butane) has two structural isomers: n-butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃) with a straight chain, and isobutane or 2-methylpropane (CH(CH₃)₃) with a branched chain. These differ in the connectivity of their carbon atoms.

A. 1: One isomer would mean C4H10 has only one possible structure, but there are two: n-butane (a straight chain of four carbons, CH3CH2CH2CH3) and isobutane (2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CH, with a branched structure). Even with just four carbons, branching creates a second structural isomer.

  - Branching lowers the boiling point, because a more compact molecule presents less surface for London dispersion forces.
  - Structural isomers differ in connectivity; stereoisomers have identical connectivity and differ only in spatial arrangement.

Q59  [chem-300]  correct answer: B

STEM: For an alkene to have distinct E/Z configurations about a C=C bond, which structural requirement must be met?

  A. A triple bond
  B. Two different substituents on each carbon of the double bond   <= key
  C. Free rotation about the double bond
  D. An asymmetric carbon

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Two different substituents on each carbon of the double bond
Each carbon of the double bond must have two different substituents. Restricted rotation about C=C then allows two distinct configurations. E/Z notation compares the higher-priority group on each carbon: together gives Z, opposite gives E. In simple examples such as but-2-ene, cis/trans terminology can also be used. If either carbon has two identical groups, there is no E/Z pair at that bond.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A triple bond: An ordinary carbon–carbon triple bond is linear and has only one external substituent on each carbon. It therefore does not create the two-sided arrangement required for an E/Z pair.
C. Free rotation about the double bond: Free rotation would allow the arrangements to interconvert easily. Rotation about a C=C bond requires disruption of π overlap, which creates the barrier separating its configurations. Ordinary single-bond rotation usually gives conformations rather than a stable alkene E/Z pair.
D. An asymmetric carbon: An alkene can have E/Z configurations without any tetrahedral chiral carbon. A carbon attached to four different groups is one common source of chirality, but it is not required for all enantiomerism either.
Key Educational Points:
  - Each double-bond carbon must have two different substituents for E/Z configurations to exist.
  - Restricted rotation results from the π interaction between the double-bonded carbons.
  - E/Z notation compares substituent priorities on the two carbons, not simply their sizes.
  - Cis/trans terminology works for familiar simple cases, while E/Z notation is more general.
  - Alkene stereoisomerism does not require a tetrahedral chiral carbon; substituted rings can also show cis/trans relationships.

Q60  [chem-301]  correct answer: B

STEM: A molecule has one tetrahedral carbon bonded to four different groups and no other source of stereoisomerism. How many stereoisomers are possible?

  A. 1
  B. 2   <= key
  C. 3
  D. 4

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. 2
The four different groups around this carbon can form two configurations, designated R and S. These are nonsuperimposable mirror images, so they are enantiomers. With one such center and no other source of stereoisomerism, the count is two. The familiar 2ⁿ expression is an upper limit for n tetrahedral chiral centers when these are the only stereogenic elements; symmetry can reduce the count in molecules with multiple centers.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. 1: One would mean only one distinct configuration. Here the four different groups create two nonsuperimposable mirror-image configurations. An achiral molecule still has a mirror image; its mirror image is simply superimposable on it.
C. 3: The specified single center has only two configurations, R and S, so there is no third configuration. Some molecules with multiple centers have three stereoisomers because of symmetry and a meso form, but that does not apply to this molecule.
D. 4: Four requires additional independent stereochemical variation, such as a second chiral center or a separate E/Z unit. The stem excludes those possibilities, leaving only two configurations.
Key Educational Points:
  - A tetrahedral carbon attached to four different groups is a common type of chiral center.
  - One such center, with no other stereogenic element, gives two configurations: R and S.
  - These two configurations are nonsuperimposable mirror images, called enantiomers.
  - Pure enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light equally in opposite directions under the same conditions; a racemic mixture has no net rotation.
  - For molecules whose only stereogenic elements are n tetrahedral chiral centers, 2ⁿ is an upper limit; symmetry can reduce the actual number.
