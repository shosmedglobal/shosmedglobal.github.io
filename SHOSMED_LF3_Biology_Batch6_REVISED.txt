Which feature of phospholipids increases membrane fluidity by preventing their fatty acid tails from packing tightly together?

  A) Integral proteins
  B) Cholesterol only
  C) Unsaturated fatty acid tails in phospholipids
  D) Peripheral proteins on the extracellular surface

### Correct Answer: Unsaturated fatty acid tails in phospholipids

Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more double bonds. A cis double bond puts a kink in the hydrocarbon tail, so neighbouring tails cannot pack tightly and the membrane stays more fluid. Not every double bond has this geometry, which is why the cis arrangement matters. Cholesterol has a dual role: it reduces fluidity at high temperatures and prevents solidification at low temperatures, acting as a fluidity buffer. Fluidity also depends on temperature, tail length, and the overall lipid composition of the membrane, so no single factor acts on its own.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Integral proteins:** Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer, and many of them span it from one side to the other, although not all do. They serve important functions in transport, signaling, and cell adhesion, and they contribute to the structure described by the fluid mosaic model. They are not, however, the phospholipid feature the question asks about: the fluidity described here comes from the lipid part of the bilayer.

**B. Cholesterol only:** Cholesterol does influence membrane fluidity, but saying "cholesterol only" is incorrect because cholesterol acts as a fluidity buffer rather than the primary determinant. At high temperatures, cholesterol reduces fluidity by restricting phospholipid movement; at low temperatures, it prevents solidification by disrupting tight packing. The question asks about a feature of the phospholipids themselves, which is the unsaturation of their fatty acid tails.

**D. Peripheral proteins on the extracellular surface:** Peripheral proteins are loosely attached to the membrane surface (either extracellular or cytoplasmic side) through electrostatic interactions. They participate in cell signaling, cytoskeletal attachment, and enzymatic activity. Because they sit at the surface rather than among the fatty acid tails, they are not the phospholipid feature the question asks about. Proteins can influence how a membrane is organised locally, but the fluidity described here comes from the lipid tails.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Membrane fluidity comes mainly from the phospholipid tails, and unsaturated tails increase it.
  - Cis double bonds put kinks in the tails that stop neighbouring molecules packing closely together.
  - Cholesterol is a fluidity buffer rather than a simple increaser: it restrains movement at high temperature and prevents tight packing at low temperature.
  - Many membrane proteins move laterally within the bilayer, while some are held in place by attachments such as those to the cytoskeleton; either way, the lipids rather than the proteins give the membrane its fluidity.
  - Fluidity matters functionally, permitting protein diffusion, vesicle budding and fusion, and membrane growth during cell division.

> Bottom Line: The fluid mosaic model describes the membrane as a dynamic structure where lipid composition (saturation, chain length, cholesterol) determines fluidity.

In the Calvin cycle, which enzyme catalyzes the fixation of CO₂ by combining it with ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP)?

  A) PEP carboxylase
  B) Rubisco (RuBisCO)
  C) Phosphofructokinase
  D) Hexokinase

### Correct Answer: Rubisco (RuBisCO)

Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) is the enzyme that fixes CO₂ in the Calvin cycle by combining it with RuBP (a 5-carbon molecule) to produce two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). RuBisCO is considered the most abundant enzyme on Earth. PEP carboxylase is used in C4 and CAM plants for initial CO₂ fixation, but the Calvin cycle itself always uses RuBisCO.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. PEP carboxylase:** PEP carboxylase is used in C4 and CAM plants for initial CO₂ fixation, but the Calvin cycle itself always uses Rubisco.

**C. Phosphofructokinase:** Phosphofructokinase (PFK-1) is a key regulatory enzyme of glycolysis, not the Calvin cycle. It catalyzes the phosphorylation of fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and is the rate-limiting step of glycolysis. PFK-1 is allosterically activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.

**D. Hexokinase:** Hexokinase is the first enzyme of glycolysis, phosphorylating glucose to glucose-6-phosphate using ATP. It operates in the cytoplasm during sugar metabolism and has nothing to do with carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle. Hexokinase is inhibited by its own product (glucose-6-phosphate) as a feedback mechanism.

### Key Educational Points:

  - RuBisCO attaches CO₂ to RuBP in the Calvin cycle; the unstable product splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate.
  - The Calvin cycle takes place in the chloroplast stroma and uses ATP and NADPH supplied by the light reactions.
  - C4 and CAM plants initially capture inorganic carbon using PEP carboxylase, but still use RuBisCO in the Calvin cycle.

> Bottom Line: RuBisCO catalyzes the first step of carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle: CO₂ + RuBP → 2 molecules of 3-PGA.

Which statement about green light and photosynthesis is correct?

  A) Chlorophyll a and b absorb green light more strongly than blue and red light
  B) Green light can support photosynthesis, although chlorophyll a and b absorb it less strongly than blue and red light
  C) Green light activates only the Calvin cycle
  D) Green light directly inhibits ATP synthase

### Correct Answer: Green light can support photosynthesis, although chlorophyll a and b absorb it less strongly than blue and red light

Chlorophyll a and b absorb blue and red light more strongly than green light. This does not mean that green light is useless to a plant. A leaf absorbs some green light, and green light can reach chloroplasts deeper inside the leaf and support photosynthesis. Some green light is reflected or transmitted, contributing to the leaf's green appearance. The photosynthetic rate also depends on light intensity, leaf structure, CO₂ availability, and other conditions.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Chlorophyll a and b absorb green light more strongly than blue and red light:** The opposite is true. Chlorophyll a and b have stronger absorption in the blue and red regions than in the green region. Do not confuse a pigment's absorption spectrum with the total light captured by a whole leaf.

**C. Green light activates only the Calvin cycle:** The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH supplied by the light reactions. Green light does not selectively activate the Calvin cycle while bypassing the light reactions.

**D. Green light directly inhibits ATP synthase:** ATP synthase uses a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane to make ATP. Green light is not a direct inhibitor of this enzyme.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Chlorophyll a and b absorb blue and red light more strongly than green light.
  - Whole leaves can absorb and use green light, including in deeper chloroplasts.
  - Wavelength alone does not determine the photosynthetic rate.

> Bottom Line: Green light can support photosynthesis; weaker chlorophyll absorption does not mean that a leaf cannot use it.

What is the net yield of ATP, NADH, and pyruvate from one molecule of glucose undergoing glycolysis?

  A) 4 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate
  B) 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate
  C) 2 ATP, 2 FADH₂, 1 pyruvate
  D) 4 ATP, 4 NADH, 2 pyruvate

### Correct Answer: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate

Glycolysis converts one glucose (6C) into two pyruvate (3C) molecules. While 4 ATP are produced by substrate-level phosphorylation, 2 ATP are consumed in the energy investment phase (hexokinase and PFK-1 steps), yielding a **net** gain of 2 ATP. Additionally, 2 NAD⁺ are reduced to 2 NADH at the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 4 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate:** 4 ATP represents the gross (total) ATP produced by substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis, but it is not the net yield. Glycolysis invests 2 ATP in the preparatory phase (one at the hexokinase step and one at the phosphofructokinase step), so the net gain is 4 - 2 = 2 ATP. The question specifically asks for the net yield.

**C. 2 ATP, 2 FADH₂, 1 pyruvate:** This answer is incorrect on multiple counts. FADH2 is not produced during glycolysis; it is produced in the citric acid cycle (specifically at succinate dehydrogenase, which is also Complex II of the ETC). Also, one glucose molecule yields two pyruvate molecules, not one, because fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is split into two three-carbon molecules.

**D. 4 ATP, 4 NADH, 2 pyruvate:** 4 ATP and 4 NADH per glucose overstates both products. The net ATP yield is 2 (not 4), and exactly 2 NADH are produced (one per glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate oxidized by GAPDH). Since one glucose generates two G_3P molecules, 2 NADH total are produced, not 4.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Net glycolysis per glucose: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, and 2 pyruvate.
  - Four ATP are produced but two are invested at hexokinase and phosphofructokinase-1, leaving a net of 2.
  - NADH is generated at the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step.
  - Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and requires neither oxygen nor mitochondria, which is why red blood cells depend on it.
  - NAD⁺ must be regenerated for glycolysis to continue. This can occur through respiration or fermentation; mature human red blood cells form lactate even when oxygen is available.

> Bottom Line: Net glycolysis yield per glucose = 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 pyruvate. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen.

In the mitochondrial electron transport chain during aerobic respiration, what is the final electron acceptor?

  A) NAD⁺
  B) FAD
  C) Cytochrome c
  D) Molecular oxygen (O₂)

### Correct Answer: Molecular oxygen (O₂)

In aerobic respiration, O₂ is the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain (Complex IV - cytochrome c oxidase). Electrons are transferred from cytochrome c to O₂, which combines with H⁺ ions to form water (H₂O). Without O₂, the ETC cannot function, NADH and FADH₂ accumulate in reduced form, and oxidative phosphorylation halts.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. NAD⁺:** NAD+ is an electron carrier that accepts electrons during metabolic reactions (glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, citric acid cycle) to form NADH. NAD+ accepts electrons to become NADH. NADH, not NAD+, supplies electrons to Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) at the beginning of the chain, so neither form is the acceptor at the end of the chain.

**B. FAD:** FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) is an electron carrier derived from riboflavin (vitamin B2). FAD accepts electrons rather than donating them. In succinate dehydrogenase (Complex II) the FAD is bound to the enzyme and is reduced as succinate is oxidized; those electrons then pass through Complex II to ubiquinone, entering the chain beyond Complex I. FAD is therefore not the terminal acceptor. The final electron acceptor is molecular oxygen at Complex IV.

**C. Cytochrome c:** Cytochrome c is a mobile electron carrier that shuttles electrons from Complex III to Complex IV within the ETC. While it plays a crucial role in electron transport, it is an intermediary carrier, not the final acceptor. After cytochrome c delivers electrons to Complex IV, those electrons are ultimately accepted by molecular oxygen to form water.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor at Complex IV, where it is reduced to water.
  - Without O₂ the chain backs up, NADH and FADH₂ accumulate, and oxidative phosphorylation stops.
  - Oxygen accepts electrons at the end of the chain; it does not itself supply energy.
  - The gradient built by electron transport is what ATP synthase uses - the two processes are coupled but mechanistically distinct.
  - Without oxygen, human mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation stops. Glycolysis can still supply some ATP if NAD⁺ is regenerated.

> Bottom Line: O₂ serves as the terminal electron acceptor at Complex IV, forming H₂O. This is why aerobic organisms require oxygen for efficient ATP production.

Q6  [bio-262]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Which metabolic poison directly inhibits ATP synthase (Complex V) in the mitochondria?

  A) Rotenone
  B) Cyanide
  C) Oligomycin
  D) 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP)

### Correct Answer: Oligomycin

Oligomycin blocks the proton channel (Fₒ subunit) of ATP synthase, preventing H⁺ flow from the intermembrane space back into the matrix. This stops ATP production by ATP synthase, although substrate-level phosphorylation elsewhere in the cell is not directly affected. The proton gradient builds up, eventually slowing the ETC as well because it cannot pump against an excessive gradient.

  |  | Inhibitor |  | Target | 
  |  | Rotenone |  | Complex I | 
  |  | Antimycin A |  | Complex III | 
  |  | Cyanide/CO |  | Complex IV | 
  |  | Oligomycin |  | ATP synthase (Complex V) | 
  |  | DNP |  | Uncoupler (dissipates gradient) | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Rotenone:** Rotenone inhibits Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) of the electron transport chain, blocking electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone. It is a natural pesticide found in certain plant roots. Rotenone blocks the entry of NADH-derived electrons at Complex I. Electrons from succinate can still enter further along the chain through Complex II, so it does not close every route through the chain, and it does not act on ATP synthase.

**B. Cyanide:** Cyanide inhibits Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) by binding to the iron center, blocking the final electron transfer to oxygen. This stops the entire ETC, halts the proton gradient, and shuts down oxidative phosphorylation. Carbon monoxide (CO) inhibits the same complex. Cyanide poisoning is rapidly lethal because cells cannot produce ATP aerobically.

**D. 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP):** 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP) is an uncoupler, not an inhibitor. It is a lipophilic weak acid that shuttles protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, dissipating the proton gradient without passing through ATP synthase. The ETC continues running (and even speeds up), but the energy is released as heat instead of being used for ATP synthesis. With adequate oxygen and substrate, uncoupling can increase oxygen consumption while reducing the ATP yield.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Oligomycin blocks the Fₒ proton channel of ATP synthase, preventing protons from returning to the matrix.
  - ATP synthesis stops, and the gradient builds until it opposes further pumping, so electron transport slows as a secondary effect.
  - That secondary slowing is what distinguishes an ATP synthase inhibitor from a direct chain inhibitor.
  - Uncouplers such as DNP do the opposite: the gradient collapses, electron transport accelerates, and ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation is greatly reduced. Substrate-level phosphorylation can still make some ATP.
  - Both reduce ATP, so identify the mechanism by what happens to oxygen consumption - decreased by inhibitors, increased by uncouplers.

> Bottom Line: Oligomycin inhibits ATP synthase; DNP uncouples the proton gradient from ATP synthesis - both reduce ATP production but by different mechanisms.

During DNA replication, which enzyme relieves the torsional strain ahead of the replication fork by cutting and rejoining DNA strands?

  A) DNA ligase
  B) Helicase
  C) Topoisomerase
  D) Primase

### Correct Answer: Topoisomerase

As helicase unwinds the double helix, positive supercoils form ahead of the replication fork. Topoisomerases relieve this strain by making transient breaks in the DNA. These breaks let the strands rotate, or let one DNA segment pass through another, before the enzyme reseals them.

  |  | Enzyme |  | Role | 
  |  | Helicase |  | Unwinds double helix | 
  |  | Topoisomerase |  | Relieves supercoiling | 
  |  | Primase |  | Synthesizes RNA primer | 
  |  | DNA ligase |  | Joins Okazaki fragments | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. DNA ligase:** DNA ligase seals nicks in the sugar-phosphate backbone, joining Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand into a continuous strand. It acts behind the fork on already-synthesised DNA, and does nothing about the torsional strain building up ahead of it.

**B. Helicase:** Helicase is the enzyme that creates the problem rather than solving it: by unwinding the double helix it generates the positive supercoils ahead of the fork that topoisomerase must then relieve.

**D. Primase:** Primase synthesises the short RNA primers that DNA polymerase needs in order to begin synthesis, since polymerase cannot start a chain from nothing. It plays no part in managing DNA supercoiling.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Unwinding DNA at a replication fork creates torsional strain ahead of the fork.
  - Topoisomerases relieve this strain by temporarily breaking and resealing DNA.
  - Helicase separates the strands; ligase seals remaining nicks. These are different jobs.

> Bottom Line: Topoisomerases prevent DNA tangling during replication by cutting and resealing the backbone.

Q8  [bio-265]  Answer: B

DNA polymerase synthesizes DNA only in the 5' to 3' direction. How is the lagging strand synthesized?

  A) Continuously in the 3' to 5' direction by a special polymerase
  B) Discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments, each synthesized 5' to 3'
  C) By RNA polymerase which works in both directions
  D) By reverse transcriptase working 3' to 5'

### Correct Answer: Discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments, each synthesized 5' to 3'

DNA polymerase extends a new strand only 5' to 3'. The lagging-strand template runs 5' to 3' toward the replication fork, so the new DNA is made in short sections extending away from the fork. Primase supplies an RNA primer for each section. DNA polymerase extends each Okazaki fragment; the primers are later removed and replaced with DNA, and DNA ligase seals the remaining nicks. In *Escherichia coli* this work is divided between DNA polymerase III, which extends the fragments, and DNA polymerase I, which replaces the primers; those enzyme names do not apply to every organism.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Continuously in the 3' to 5' direction by a special polymerase:** No known DNA polymerase synthesizes DNA in the 3' to 5' direction. All DNA polymerases add nucleotides exclusively to the 3'-OH end of a growing strand, extending in the 5' to 3' direction. This is a fundamental biochemical constraint because the energy for bond formation comes from the incoming nucleoside triphosphate, which must be added at the 3' end.

**C. By RNA polymerase which works in both directions:** RNA polymerase makes RNA, not DNA. It reads its DNA template 3' to 5' while synthesizing RNA 5' to 3', so it does not work in both directions. Primase makes the short RNA primer at the start of each Okazaki fragment; it does not synthesize the DNA fragments themselves. A DNA polymerase does that, such as DNA polymerase III in *E. coli* or DNA polymerase delta in eukaryotes.

**D. By reverse transcriptase working 3' to 5':** Reverse transcriptase synthesizes DNA from an RNA template, and like other DNA-synthesizing enzymes it extends 5' to 3', not 3' to 5'. It is not the enzyme that extends ordinary Okazaki fragments. Retroviruses such as HIV carry one, and cells have a reverse transcriptase of their own in telomerase, but neither takes part in ordinary lagging-strand synthesis.

### Key Educational Points:

  - New DNA is synthesized 5′ to 3′ on both leading and lagging strands.
  - The antiparallel templates make continuous synthesis possible on one strand and discontinuous synthesis necessary on the other.
  - Okazaki fragments require primers, primer replacement with DNA, and sealing by ligase.

> Bottom Line: The lagging strand is synthesized as discontinuous Okazaki fragments, each requiring a separate RNA primer.

Q9  [bio-268]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
In bacteria, the sigma factor of RNA polymerase is essential for:

  A) Elongation of the RNA transcript
  B) Termination of transcription
  C) Recognition and binding to the promoter sequence
  D) Proofreading of newly synthesized RNA

### Correct Answer: Recognition and binding to the promoter sequence

The sigma factor lets RNA polymerase recognize promoter sequences, so that transcription starts in the right place. In the familiar *Escherichia coli* sigma-70 example these include the -10 Pribnow box (TATAAT) and the -35 region, although not every bacterial promoter follows that consensus. Sigma is needed for initiation; the core enzyme is what catalyzes RNA synthesis.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Elongation of the RNA transcript:** Elongation is carried out by the core enzyme of RNA polymerase, which moves along the template strand synthesizing RNA in the 5' to 3' direction. Sigma's job is promoter recognition at initiation. It may remain associated with the enzyme into elongation rather than leaving at a fixed point, but it is not what catalyzes chain extension.

**B. Termination of transcription:** Transcription termination in prokaryotes occurs by two mechanisms, neither involving the sigma factor. Rho-independent termination uses a GC-rich hairpin loop followed by a poly-U sequence in the mRNA. Rho-dependent termination uses the rho helicase protein, which catches up to RNA polymerase and unwinds the RNA-DNA hybrid to release the transcript.

**D. Proofreading of newly synthesized RNA:** RNA polymerase can correct some of its own errors, for example by backtracking and cleaving the transcript, so it is not true that the enzyme cannot proofread at all. Error correction is not what sigma does: its principal role is promoter recognition.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Sigma factors help bacterial RNA polymerase recognize promoters.
  - A sigma factor associates with the core enzyme to form an initiation-competent holoenzyme.
  - The core enzyme synthesizes RNA; sigma is not the enzyme’s proofreading component.

> Bottom Line: Sigma factor = promoter recognition; core enzyme = elongation.

Q10  [bio-270]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
A mutation changes a codon from UAU (tyrosine) to UAA. What type of mutation is this?

  A) Missense mutation
  B) Silent mutation
  C) Nonsense mutation
  D) Frameshift mutation

### Correct Answer: Nonsense mutation

UAA is a stop codon. Converting a sense codon to a stop codon is a nonsense mutation, causing premature termination and a truncated protein.

  |  | Type |  | Effect | 
  |  | Silent |  | Same amino acid | 
  |  | Missense |  | Different amino acid | 
  |  | Nonsense |  | Premature stop codon | 
  |  | Frameshift |  | Shifted reading frame | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Missense mutation:** A missense mutation changes one codon to code for a different amino acid (not a stop codon). For example, the sickle cell mutation changes GAG (glutamic acid) to GUG (valine). Missense mutations may have mild, severe, or no effect depending on where in the protein the substitution occurs and how chemically different the new amino acid is.

**B. Silent mutation:** A silent (synonymous) mutation changes a codon but still codes for the same amino acid, thanks to the degeneracy of the genetic code. For example, GCU and GCC both encode alanine. Silent mutations most often occur at the third (wobble) position of a codon. UAU to UAA is not silent because UAA is a stop codon, not a tyrosine codon.

**D. Frameshift mutation:** A frameshift mutation results from insertion or deletion of nucleotides (in numbers not divisible by 3), shifting the reading frame of all downstream codons. This typically produces a completely altered amino acid sequence. The UAU to UAA change is a single nucleotide substitution (U to A at the third position), which does not alter the reading frame.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A nonsense mutation changes an amino-acid codon into a stop codon.
  - UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons in the standard genetic code.
  - A premature stop can shorten the protein; a single-base substitution does not itself shift the reading frame.

> Bottom Line: UAU → UAA introduces a premature stop codon: a nonsense mutation.

Q11  [bio-271]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
In the lac operon, what occurs when BOTH lactose is present AND glucose is absent?

  A) The operon is repressed by the lac repressor
  B) Transcription occurs at a low basal level only
  C) The operon is maximally transcribed due to repressor inactivation and CAP-cAMP activation
  D) The operon is silenced by methylation

### Correct Answer: The operon is maximally transcribed due to repressor inactivation and CAP-cAMP activation

Lactose (as allolactose) inactivates the repressor (derepression). No glucose means high cAMP, forming CAP-cAMP complex that enhances RNA polymerase binding (positive regulation). Both together = maximal expression.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. The operon is repressed by the lac repressor:** The lac repressor binds the operator and blocks transcription only when lactose is absent. When lactose is present, it is converted to allolactose, which binds the repressor and causes a conformational change that releases it from the operator (derepression). Since the question states lactose IS present, the repressor would be inactivated, not actively repressing.

**B. Transcription occurs at a low basal level only:** A low basal transcription level occurs when lactose is present but glucose is also present. In this scenario, the repressor is inactivated (allowing some transcription) but cAMP levels are low (because glucose inhibits adenylyl cyclase), so the CAP-cAMP activator complex cannot form. Without CAP-cAMP enhancement, RNA polymerase binds weakly and transcription is minimal.

**D. The operon is silenced by methylation:** DNA methylation is an epigenetic gene silencing mechanism found in eukaryotes (not the primary regulatory mechanism for the bacterial lac operon). The lac operon is regulated by protein-DNA interactions: the repressor (negative control) and the CAP-cAMP complex (positive control), not by covalent DNA modification.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The lac operon is under dual control: negative by the LacI repressor and positive by CAP-cAMP.
  - Lactose (as allolactose) inactivates the repressor, removing the block - this is derepression, not activation.
  - Absent glucose means high cAMP, so CAP-cAMP binds and recruits RNA polymerase.
  - Maximal transcription therefore requires both conditions: lactose present and glucose absent.
  - This is catabolite repression - the cell preferentially consumes glucose and only invests in lactose enzymes when it must.

> Bottom Line: Lac operon dual control: negative (repressor) and positive (CAP-cAMP).

Q12  [bio-272]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which epigenetic modification commonly reduces transcription when it occurs at CpG sites in a gene’s promoter?

  A) Histone acetylation
  B) DNA methylation
  C) Alternative splicing
  D) Polyubiquitination

### Correct Answer: DNA methylation

DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) add methyl groups to cytosine in CpG dinucleotides. Methylation of a gene's promoter CpG island is generally associated with reduced transcription, because it recruits proteins that pack the chromatin more tightly. Methylation elsewhere in a gene is not automatically a silencing switch.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Histone acetylation:** Histone acetylation generally has the opposite effect: it promotes gene expression. Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) add acetyl groups to lysine residues on histone tails, neutralizing their positive charge and loosening the interaction with negatively charged DNA. This generally opens the chromatin (euchromatin) and promotes transcription, while removal of the acetyl groups by HDACs generally has the opposite effect.

**C. Alternative splicing:** Alternative splicing is a post-transcriptional mechanism that generates multiple mRNA variants from a single gene by including or excluding different exons. It increases protein diversity and can affect gene expression, for example by producing a transcript that is not translated into functional protein. What it is not is methylation of promoter DNA, which is what the question describes, and a gene must first be transcribed before its RNA can be spliced.

**D. Polyubiquitination:** Polyubiquitination attaches chains of the small protein ubiquitin to a target protein. This commonly marks the protein for degradation by the 26S proteasome, although not every ubiquitin modification does so; some instead change a protein's location or activity. Either way it modifies proteins and does not add methyl groups to cytosine, which is what the question asks about.

### Key Educational Points:

  - DNA methyltransferases add methyl groups to cytosine in CpG dinucleotides.
  - Methylation of a promoter CpG island is generally associated with reduced transcription of that gene, because it recruits proteins that pack the chromatin more tightly.
  - The DNA sequence itself is unchanged, which is what makes this epigenetic rather than mutational.
  - Pair the two rules: DNA methylation generally silences, histone acetylation generally activates by loosening chromatin.
  - Methylation patterns are heritable through cell division and are commonly disturbed in cancer, where tumour suppressor promoters become hypermethylated.

> Bottom Line: Promoter CpG methylation is generally associated with reduced transcription; histone acetylation is generally associated with increased transcription. Both are epigenetic modifications.

Q13  [bio-273]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which level of protein structure is stabilized primarily by hydrogen bonds between backbone carbonyl C=O groups and amide N-H groups, forming alpha helices and beta sheets?

  A) Primary structure
  B) Secondary structure
  C) Tertiary structure
  D) Quaternary structure

### Correct Answer: Secondary structure

Secondary structure = local folding patterns stabilized by H-bonds between backbone C=O and N-H groups: alpha-helices (coiled, H-bonds every 4 residues) and β-pleated sheets.

  |  | Level |  | Stabilizing Forces | 
  |  | Primary |  | Peptide bonds (covalent) | 
  |  | Secondary |  | Backbone H-bonds | 
  |  | Tertiary |  | R-group interactions (H-bonds, disulfide, hydrophobic, ionic) | 
  |  | Quaternary |  | Subunit interactions | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Primary structure:** Primary structure is simply the linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, held together by covalent peptide bonds. It is determined directly by the gene sequence and contains no folding. The primary structure dictates all higher levels of structure, but it does not involve hydrogen bonds between backbone groups forming helices or sheets.

**C. Tertiary structure:** Tertiary structure refers to the overall three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptide chain, stabilized by interactions between R-groups (side chains): hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds between side chains, disulfide bridges between cysteine residues, and van der Waals forces. It is the full 3D folding, not just local patterns like helices.

**D. Quaternary structure:** Quaternary structure exists only in proteins composed of multiple polypeptide subunits (e.g., hemoglobin has four subunits: 2 alpha and 2 beta). It describes how the subunits assemble and interact, stabilized by the same types of non-covalent forces as tertiary structure and sometimes by disulfide bonds between chains. Not all proteins have quaternary structure.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Secondary structure is local folding stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N-H groups.
  - Alpha helices and beta pleated sheets are the two principal motifs.
  - The characteristic hydrogen bonds are between backbone groups rather than side chains, although the side chains still influence which secondary structures form where.
  - Tertiary structure is where R-group interactions act: hydrophobic packing, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide bridges.
  - Quaternary structure describes the assembly of separate polypeptide subunits, as in the four chains of haemoglobin.

> Bottom Line: Secondary = backbone H-bonds; Tertiary = R-group interactions.

An enzyme follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics with a Kₘ of 2 mM and a Vₘₐₓ of 100 µmol/min. At a substrate concentration of 2 mM, what is the reaction velocity?

  A) 25 µmol/min
  B) 50 µmol/min
  C) 75 µmol/min
  D) 100 µmol/min

  |  | Pure noncompetitive |  | Unchanged |  | Decreased |

**C. Decrease Vₘₐₓ but NOT change Kₘ:** Decreased Vmax with unchanged Km describes PURE noncompetitive inhibition. > Bottom Line: Competitive inhibition is overcome by excess substrate.

Which of the following would VIOLATE Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium assumptions?

  A) Random mating in a large population
  B) No natural selection acting on the trait
  C) Significant immigration of individuals with different allele frequencies
  D) No mutations occurring at the locus

### Correct Answer: Significant immigration of individuals with different allele frequencies

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium requires: (1) large population, (2) random mating, (3) no selection, (4) no mutation, (5) no migration (gene flow). Immigration introduces alleles at different frequencies, violating assumption 5 and shifting allele frequencies.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Random mating in a large population:** Random mating in a large population is actually one of the conditions REQUIRED for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, not a violation of it. If mating is random and the population is large, allele frequencies remain stable when the other Hardy-Weinberg assumptions also hold. Violations include non-random mating (like assortative mating), small population size, selection, mutation, and gene flow.

**B. No natural selection acting on the trait:** The absence of natural selection is a condition that SUPPORTS Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. If no selection acts on the trait, all genotypes have equal fitness and allele frequencies do not shift, again when the other Hardy-Weinberg assumptions also hold. Natural selection occurring would violate equilibrium by preferentially increasing or decreasing certain allele frequencies.

**D. No mutations occurring at the locus:** No mutations occurring at the locus is another condition that MAINTAINS Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Mutations introduce new alleles into a population, changing allele frequencies. The absence of mutation helps keep the allele pool stable when the other Hardy-Weinberg assumptions also hold. Remember: H-W equilibrium requires no mutation, no selection, random mating, large population size, and no migration.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is a null model: it describes what allele frequencies do when nothing is acting to change them.
  - It requires five conditions - a large population, random mating, no selection, no mutation, and no gene flow (migration).
  - Immigration introduces alleles at different frequencies, so it violates the no-gene-flow condition and shifts the population's allele frequencies.
  - The other options each restate a condition that is being met, so none of them can be a violation.
  - Real populations rarely meet all five conditions. A departure from Hardy-Weinberg proportions tells us to examine which assumption is failing; it does not identify the cause. Nonrandom mating, for instance, can change genotype frequencies without changing allele frequencies.

> Bottom Line: The five conditions for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: large population, random mating, no selection, no mutation, no gene flow.

During which phase of mitosis do chromosomes align along the metaphase plate, attached to spindle fibers at their kinetochores?

  A) Prophase
  B) Metaphase
  C) Anaphase
  D) Telophase

### Correct Answer: Metaphase

In metaphase, all chromosomes are aligned at the cell's equator (metaphase plate). Each chromosome is attached to spindle microtubules from opposite poles via kinetochores on the centromere. The spindle assembly checkpoint helps ensure that chromosomes are correctly attached before anaphase proceeds.

  |  | Phase |  | Key Event | 
  |  | Prophase |  | Chromatin condenses, nuclear envelope begins to break down | 
  |  | Metaphase |  | Chromosomes align at metaphase plate | 
  |  | Anaphase |  | Sister chromatids separate, move to poles | 
  |  | Telophase |  | Nuclear envelopes reform, chromosomes decondense | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Prophase:** During prophase of mitosis, chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear envelope begins to break down, and the mitotic spindle forms. However, chromosomes have not yet aligned at the metaphase plate - that alignment is the defining feature of metaphase.

**C. Anaphase:** Anaphase occurs *after* metaphase. During anaphase, the centromeres split and sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles by shortening kinetochore microtubules. The metaphase plate alignment has already occurred before anaphase begins.

  - The spindle assembly checkpoint holds the cell in metaphase until the kinetochores are correctly attached, which reduces the risk of missegregation; like any checkpoint, it is not infallible.
  - Keep the sequence straight: prophase (condensation), metaphase (alignment), anaphase (separation), telophase (nuclear reassembly).

> Bottom Line: The spindle assembly checkpoint at metaphase delays anaphase until the kinetochores are properly attached, which reduces the risk of missegregation.

  B) 46 chromosomes
  C) 92 chromosomes

Mitosis is an equational division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells. Each daughter cell has the same chromosome number as the parent cell (2n = 46 in humans). DNA is replicated in S phase (46 chromosomes, each with 2 chromatids = 92 chromatids), and mitosis separates sister chromatids so each daughter cell gets 46 chromosomes with 1 chromatid each.

These are partitioned into two daughter cells of 46 each, and the question asks about each daughter cell after division.

**D. 12 chromosomes:** Mitosis is an equational division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same chromosome number as the parent cell (2n = 46 in humans).

### Key Educational Points:

  - Mitosis is an equational division: each daughter cell ends with the same chromosome number as the parent, 46 in humans.
  - DNA is doubled beforehand in S phase, giving 46 chromosomes of two sister chromatids each.
  - Mitosis separates those sister chromatids, so each daughter receives 46 chromosomes of one chromatid each.

  B) Random orientation of homologous pairs at metaphase I
  C) Separation of sister chromatids in anaphase II
  D) DNA replication in S phase

### Correct Answer: Random orientation of homologous pairs at metaphase I

During metaphase I, each bivalent (pair of homologous chromosomes) aligns independently at the metaphase plate. The orientation of maternal vs. paternal chromosomes toward each pole is random. With 23 pairs of chromosomes, there are 2²³ = ~8.4 million possible combinations of maternal/paternal chromosomes in human gametes.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Crossing over in prophase I:** Crossing over during prophase I exchanges segments between homologous chromosomes at chiasmata, creating new allele combinations within a chromosome. It is a real source of genetic variation, but a separate one from the independent orientation of different homologous pairs, which is what this question asks about.

**C. Separation of sister chromatids in anaphase II:** Anaphase II separates sister chromatids; it does not create the copies. Those were made in S phase, and crossing over can leave the two sister chromatids differing from each other. This question concerns the independent arrangement of homologous pairs in meiosis I, which is established at metaphase I, with those homologs segregating at anaphase I.

**D. DNA replication in S phase:** DNA replication in S phase copies each chromosome to give sister chromatids. It is not the mechanism responsible for independent assortment, which comes from the random orientation of homologous pairs at metaphase I. Replication is high-fidelity, with proofreading by DNA polymerase, although replication errors can still introduce mutations.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Independent assortment arises from the random orientation of each bivalent at metaphase I.

  - Crossing over is a separate source of variation: it recombines alleles within a chromosome, whereas independent assortment shuffles whole chromosomes.
  - The law applies to genes on different chromosomes; genes lying close together on the same chromosome are linked and do not assort independently.

> Bottom Line: Independent assortment = random metaphase I orientation. Crossing over provides additional recombination beyond independent assortment.

  C) Proto-oncogenes cause cancer while oncogenes suppress tumors

### Correct Answer: Proto-oncogenes normally regulate cell growth; oncogenes are abnormally activated or overexpressed versions of these genes

Proto-oncogenes encode proteins that promote normal cell growth and division (growth factors, receptors, signal transducers, transcription factors). Changes that make such a gene abnormally active convert it into an oncogene, which drives uncontrolled proliferation. The change can be in the gene's sequence, its copy number, or the way it is regulated; point mutation, gene amplification, and translocation are the common examples. One activated allele can exert a dominant growth-promoting effect at the level of a single cell, although one alteration alone is generally not enough to produce a cancer. Examples: RAS, MYC, HER2.

**A. Proto-oncogenes are always silent and never expressed:** Proto-oncogenes are NOT always silent. They are normal cellular genes that are actively expressed and encode proteins essential for cell growth, division, and differentiation. Growth factors, growth factor receptors, signal transduction proteins, and transcription factors are all products of proto-oncogenes. They become problematic when they are abnormally activated or overexpressed as oncogenes.

**C. Proto-oncogenes cause cancer while oncogenes suppress tumors:** This answer reverses the relationship. Proto-oncogenes are normal genes that PROMOTE cell growth when appropriately regulated. Oncogenes are mutated or overexpressed versions of proto-oncogenes that drive uncontrolled cell proliferation. Tumor suppressors (like p53 and Rb) are the genes that suppress tumor formation, not oncogenes.

  - Standard examples are RAS, MYC, and HER2 for oncogenes, versus TP53, RB1, and BRCA1/2 for tumour suppressors.

> Bottom Line: Oncogenes are abnormally activated proto-oncogenes, acting dominantly within the cell. Tumor suppressors lose function, and many follow a two-hit pattern in which both alleles are inactivated (Knudson's hypothesis).

A bacterium acquires a new gene by taking up naked DNA from its environment. This process is called:

  A) Conjugation
  B) Transduction
  C) Transformation
  D) Binary fission

Transformation is the uptake and incorporation of free (naked) DNA from the environment by a competent bacterium. This was demonstrated in Griffith's experiment (1928) with *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and later confirmed by Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty (1944) as evidence that DNA is the genetic material.

  |  | Process |  | Mechanism | 
  |  | Transformation |  | Uptake of free DNA | 
  |  | Transduction |  | DNA transfer via bacteriophage | 
  |  | Conjugation |  | Contact-dependent transfer between cells |

The F (fertility) plasmid, which encodes a sex pilus and the transfer machinery, is the familiar example, but not every conjugative system uses that same machinery. **B. Transduction:** Transduction is the transfer of bacterial DNA mediated by a bacteriophage (bacterial virus). In generalized transduction, random bacterial DNA is accidentally packaged into phage particles. In specialized transduction, specific bacterial genes adjacent to the prophage are excised and transferred. Both types require a phage intermediary, unlike transformation.

**D. Binary fission:** Binary fission is the normal mode of bacterial reproduction, where a single cell divides into two identical daughter cells. It is not a mechanism of horizontal gene transfer. DNA is replicated and partitioned equally to each daughter cell. Binary fission increases bacterial numbers but does not introduce new genetic material from outside sources.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Transformation is the uptake of free, naked DNA from the environment by a competent bacterium.

  - All three are horizontal gene transfer, moving genes between contemporaries rather than from parent to offspring.
  - Horizontal transfer is a major route by which antibiotic resistance genes spread between bacteria.

> Bottom Line: Transformation = naked DNA uptake. Griffith's experiment first demonstrated this principle.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Viral genomes always contain both DNA and RNA:** A viral genome is classified as DNA or RNA, not both. DNA viruses (e.g., herpes, adenovirus, poxvirus) have DNA genomes, while RNA viruses (e.g., influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2) have RNA genomes, and the type of nucleic acid determines the replication strategy. This does not mean that the other nucleic acid can never appear as a packaged molecule or a replication intermediate: a retrovirus, for example, makes a DNA copy of its RNA genome inside the cell.

**B. Viruses can reproduce independently on nutrient media:** Viruses require living cells in order to multiply, so they cannot be grown on cell-free nutrient media; they are cultured in cell cultures, embryonated eggs, or laboratory animals. Many bacteria do grow on suitable cell-free media, although some bacteria also require host cells.

**D. Viruses are always larger than bacteria:** Most viruses are smaller than typical bacteria: many are about 20-300 nm, while bacteria are typically 0.2-10 micrometres. The two size ranges overlap, however, and giant viruses are large enough to be seen by light microscopy, so neither always smaller nor invisible to every light microscope holds as a universal rule.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Viruses are acellular infectious agents with a DNA or RNA genome and a protein capsid; some also have an envelope.
  - Viruses lack ribosomes and use host cells to make viral proteins.
  - Viral enzymes can help replication, but do not make a virus independently self-sufficient.

> Bottom Line: Viruses lack their own ribosomes and rely on host-cell protein synthesis to produce new virus particles.

HIV is a retrovirus. Which enzyme does HIV use to synthesize DNA from its RNA genome after entering a host cell?

  A) DNA polymerase III
  B) RNA polymerase II
  C) Reverse transcriptase
  D) Helicase

### Correct Answer: Reverse transcriptase

Retroviruses like HIV carry reverse transcriptase, which synthesizes complementary DNA (cDNA) from the viral RNA genome. This cDNA is then converted to double-stranded DNA and integrated into the host chromosome by the viral integrase enzyme. HIV targets CD4⁺ T helper cells, leading to immunodeficiency.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. DNA polymerase III:** DNA polymerase III is the main replicative enzyme in bacteria such as *Escherichia coli*. It synthesizes new DNA strands using a DNA template during normal cell division. It cannot use RNA as a template. HIV requires reverse transcriptase specifically because its genome is RNA and must be converted to DNA before integration into the host chromosome.

**B. RNA polymerase II:** RNA polymerase II is a eukaryotic enzyme that transcribes protein-coding genes from DNA into mRNA. It synthesizes RNA from a DNA template (DNA to RNA), which is the normal direction of genetic information flow. Reverse transcriptase does the opposite (RNA to DNA), which is why it was named "reverse" transcriptase.

**D. Helicase:** Helicase unwinds double-stranded DNA by breaking hydrogen bonds between base pairs, creating single-stranded templates for replication or transcription. While helicase is important in many DNA processes, it does not synthesize new nucleic acid strands. HIV requires reverse transcriptase to create a DNA copy of its RNA genome after entering the host cell.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Reverse transcriptase synthesises DNA from an RNA template - the defining enzyme of a retrovirus.
  - The route is RNA to cDNA (reverse transcriptase), then double-stranded DNA, then integration by integrase. The integrated viral DNA can then be transcribed into RNA; viral proteins are translated, and new virus particles are assembled and released.
  - Integration into the host genome as a provirus contributes to long-lived HIV reservoirs, which standard treatment does not eliminate.
  - HIV enters CD4⁺ T helper cells via CD4 plus a chemokine co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4); their depletion produces immunodeficiency.
  - Reverse transcriptase lacks proofreading, so the high mutation rate drives drug resistance and forces combination therapy.

> Bottom Line: Retrovirus replication: RNA → cDNA (reverse transcriptase) → dsDNA → integration (integrase) → transcription of viral RNA → translation of viral proteins → assembly and release of new virus particles.

Bacterial binary fission differs from eukaryotic mitosis in that:

  A) Binary fission produces four daughter cells
  B) Binary fission does not involve a mitotic spindle apparatus
  C) Binary fission includes a prolonged G2 phase
  D) Binary fission results in genetically different daughter cells

### Correct Answer: Binary fission does not involve a mitotic spindle apparatus

Bacteria lack the microtubule-based spindle apparatus used in eukaryotic mitosis. DNA replication is followed by organized partitioning of the chromosome copies to opposite ends of the cell. Division machinery then forms a septum at midcell, separating the cell into two daughters. In a typical bacterial model such as *Escherichia coli* there is a single circular chromosome and the septum is coordinated by the FtsZ protein ring, although not every bacterium matches that description. The two daughter cells are usually genetically identical apart from mutations.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Binary fission produces four daughter cells:** Binary fission produces two daughter cells, not four. Each daughter cell receives a copy of the single circular chromosome. The process yielding four cells from one starting cell is meiosis (in eukaryotes), which involves two sequential divisions. Binary fission is a single division event.

**C. Binary fission includes a prolonged G2 phase:** Binary fission does not include a prolonged G2 phase. In fact, bacterial cell cycles can be extremely rapid (E. coli can divide every 20 minutes under optimal conditions) and do not have clearly defined G1, S, and G2 phases like the eukaryotic cell cycle. DNA replication and cell division can even overlap in fast-growing bacteria.

**D. Binary fission results in genetically different daughter cells:** Binary fission produces daughter cells that are usually genetically identical, not deliberately different ones. Replication is high-fidelity, so each daughter normally receives an identical copy of the chromosome, although mutations do occur. Most genetic variation in bacteria arises from mutation and horizontal gene transfer (conjugation, transformation, transduction) rather than from binary fission itself.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Binary fission produces two daughter cells after DNA replication and chromosome partitioning.
  - Bacterial division uses its own cellular machinery, rather than a eukaryotic mitotic spindle.
  - Daughter cells are usually clones, but mutations can introduce differences.

> Bottom Line: Binary fission: no spindle and no nuclear envelope breakdown, with an FtsZ ring forming the septum in typical bacteria. Result: two daughter cells, usually identical apart from mutations.

  C) Organisms must intentionally adapt to their environment
  D) Mutations must be directed toward beneficial traits

### Correct Answer: Heritable variation exists among individuals, and some variants have higher fitness

Darwin's natural selection requires: (1) Variation among individuals in a population, (2) The variation must be heritable (genetic basis), (3) Differential reproductive success (some variants leave more offspring). Over time, alleles associated with higher fitness increase in frequency. Mutations are random, not directed, and organisms do not intentionally adapt.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. All individuals in a population must be genetically identical:** If all individuals were genetically identical, there would be no variation for natural selection to act upon. Natural selection is nonrandom with respect to reproductive success, but it has no intention or foresight.

  - Natural selection requires variation, heritability, and differences in reproductive success.
  - Evolutionary fitness means contribution to future generations, not simply physical strength.

A small population of birds is blown by a storm to a remote island where that species was not previously present. The allele frequencies in this founder population differ from the original mainland population. This is an example of:

  A) Natural selection
  B) Gene flow
  C) Founder effect (genetic drift)
  D) Stabilizing selection

### Correct Answer: Founder effect (genetic drift)

The founder effect is a type of genetic drift that occurs when a small group establishes a new population. The allele frequencies in the founders may not represent the original population due to sampling error. This can lead to increased frequency of rare alleles and reduced genetic diversity. The bottleneck effect is another form of genetic drift caused by drastic population reduction.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Natural selection:** Natural selection is a non-random process favoring individuals with higher fitness in a given environment. The scenario described (random event of a storm blowing birds to an island) is governed by chance, not fitness advantages. The allele frequency differences between the founder population and the original population are due to random sampling, which defines genetic drift.

**B. Gene flow:** Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations, and a single successful migration event can transfer alleles, so it does not require continuous or repeated migration. What this scenario describes, though, is the founding of a new population where the species was not previously present. The point of interest is that the founders carry an unrepresentative sample of the source population's alleles, which is the founder effect, a type of genetic drift.

**D. Stabilizing selection:** Stabilizing selection favors intermediate phenotypes and reduces variation, maintaining the population mean. This is a form of natural selection that acts on existing populations over time. The scenario describes the initial establishment of a new population with altered allele frequencies due to chance sampling from the source population, which is the founder effect.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The founder effect is genetic drift acting when a small group establishes a new population.
  - Allele frequencies in the founders differ from the source population purely by sampling error, not selection.

  - Both often reduce genetic diversity and can raise the frequency of otherwise rare alleles, which is the basis of certain disorders in geographically isolated populations.

  A) 100%
  B) 50%
  C) 10%
  D) 1%

### Correct Answer: 10%

The 10% rule is a rough average: roughly 10% of the production at one trophic level passes to the next. It is an approximate guide rather than a fixed law, and measured efficiencies vary between systems. Much of the rest is dissipated through respiration, while uneaten or unassimilated material enters detrital food webs. Less energy at higher levels helps explain why food chains are usually short, often around four or five levels, and why a pyramid of energy narrows upward.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 100%:** 100% energy transfer between trophic levels is thermodynamically impossible. The second law of thermodynamics dictates that energy is lost as heat during every metabolic process. Organisms at each trophic level use most of the energy they consume for respiration, movement, and maintenance. Energy stored in new biomass is a different case: it can be transferred when that biomass is eaten. On average only about 10% of the production passes to the next level.

**B. 50%:** 50% is not the figure used by the 10% model, which is the approximation this question is testing. Measured transfer efficiencies vary between ecosystems, but a value as high as 50% would be well above the usual range and would support far longer food chains than are generally observed.

**D. 1%:** 1% is well below the approximate 10% figure this question is testing. Transfer efficiencies do vary between systems, so a value other than 10% is not automatically impossible; it is simply not the approximation the 10% rule describes.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The 10% rule is an approximate guide to transfer of production between trophic levels.
  - Respiration dissipates energy as heat, and uneaten or unassimilated material can enter detrital pathways.
  - Less energy is available at higher trophic levels, helping limit food-chain length.

> Bottom Line: Roughly 10% of production passes between trophic levels, as an approximation. Energy flow is unidirectional; nutrients cycle.

  D) Formation of the blastocoel cavity

### Correct Answer: Elimination of tissue between developing fingers and toes

Apoptosis sculpts tissues during development. The webbing between fingers and toes is removed by programmed cell death during about weeks 6 to 8 after fertilization. Other examples include: elimination of the tail in human embryos, selection of neurons that make proper connections, and removal of autoreactive lymphocytes. Failure to remove the normal interdigital tissue can contribute to syndactyly (webbed fingers or toes), although not every case of syndactyly has that single mechanism.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Unlimited proliferation of neural crest cells:** Unlimited proliferation of neural crest cells would be pathological, not a normal developmental process. Uncontrolled cell proliferation is a hallmark of cancer, such as neuroblastoma (a neural crest-derived tumor). Normal development requires a precise balance between cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.

**C. Increase in cell size during muscle growth:** Increase in cell size during muscle growth is called hypertrophy, not apoptosis. Hypertrophy occurs when existing muscle fibers enlarge (increase in protein content and myofibril number) in response to exercise or hormonal stimulation. This is a growth process, fundamentally opposite to apoptosis (programmed cell death).

**D. Formation of the blastocoel cavity:** Formation of the blastocoel cavity occurs during early embryogenesis as cells of the morula secrete fluid inward to create a fluid-filled space. This is cavitation: ion transport creates an osmotic gradient and water follows by osmosis. It is not apoptosis. The blastocoel forms the cavity of the blastocyst and helps establish the inner cell mass and trophoblast.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Apoptosis sculpts developing structures, and the loss of interdigital webbing is the standard example.
  - In human embryos this occurs around weeks 6 to 8 after fertilization, and failure to remove the interdigital tissue can contribute to syndactyly.
  - Other developmental roles include regression of the embryonic tail, pruning of neurons that fail to make connections, and deletion of autoreactive lymphocytes.
  - Apoptosis is orderly and typically does not provoke the inflammatory response associated with uncontrolled cell injury: cells shrink, fragment, and are phagocytosed intact.
  - This contrasts with necrosis, where cells swell and rupture, spilling contents and provoking inflammation.

> Bottom Line: Apoptosis is essential for normal development, sculpting organ shape and eliminating unnecessary or potentially harmful cells.

In nervous tissue, which type of glial cell forms the myelin sheath around axons in the central nervous system?

  A) Schwann cells
  B) Oligodendrocytes
  C) Astrocytes
  D) Microglia

### Correct Answer: Oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocytes produce myelin in the CNS (brain and spinal cord). One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of multiple axons. In the PNS, Schwann cells produce myelin, with each Schwann cell wrapping around a segment of one axon only.

  |  | Glial Cell |  | Function | 
  |  | Oligodendrocytes |  | CNS myelination | 
  |  | Schwann cells |  | PNS myelination | 
  |  | Astrocytes |  | Supports blood-brain barrier function, nutrient support, neurotransmitter uptake | 
  |  | Microglia |  | Immune defense (phagocytosis) in CNS | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Schwann cells:** Schwann cells myelinate axons, but in the peripheral nervous system rather than the CNS. Each Schwann cell also wraps a single internode of one axon, whereas one oligodendrocyte myelinates segments of many axons at once.

**C. Astrocytes:** Astrocytes are abundant CNS glia that provide metabolic support, buffer extracellular potassium, and take up neurotransmitters. At the blood-brain barrier the principal physical barrier is formed by tight junctions between endothelial cells, with astrocyte end-feet supporting its function. Either way, astrocytes produce no myelin.

**D. Microglia:** Microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS and arise from embryonic yolk-sac myeloid precursors rather than from neuroectoderm. They phagocytose debris and dying cells and mediate neuroinflammation. They do not form myelin sheaths themselves, although they can influence the process.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Oligodendrocytes form CNS myelin, and one cell can myelinate segments of several axons.
  - A myelinating Schwann cell forms one internode on one PNS axon.
  - Myelin insulates axons and speeds conduction between nodes of Ranvier.

> Bottom Line: CNS myelin = oligodendrocytes (multiple axons each). PNS myelin = Schwann cells (one axon segment each).

Which of the following correctly describes the mechanism of competitive enzyme inhibition?

  A) The inhibitor binds to the active site and increases V<sub>max</sub>
  B) The inhibitor binds to the active site and increases the apparent K<sub>m</sub> without changing V<sub>max</sub>
  C) The inhibitor binds to an allosteric site and decreases V<sub>max</sub>
  D) The inhibitor binds to the enzyme-substrate complex and decreases both K<sub>m</sub> and V<sub>max</sub>

### Correct Answer: B. The inhibitor binds to the active site and increases the apparent Kₘ without changing Vₘₐₓ

In competitive inhibition, the inhibitor competes with the substrate for binding at the active site. Such inhibitors often resemble the substrate structurally, which makes the active-site picture a useful introductory model, but structural resemblance is not required. The competition increases the apparent Kₘ, meaning that a higher substrate concentration is needed to reach half-maximal rate. However, at sufficiently high substrate concentrations, the substrate can outcompete the inhibitor, so Vₘₐₓ remains unchanged.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. The inhibitor binds to the active site and increases V_max:** A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and increases the apparent Km, but it does NOT increase Vmax. Vmax remains unchanged because at sufficiently high substrate concentrations, the substrate can outcompete the inhibitor for the active site. Increasing Vmax would mean the inhibitor somehow makes the enzyme work faster, which contradicts the concept of inhibition.

**C. The inhibitor binds to an allosteric site and decreases V_max:** Binding away from the active site can produce mixed or PURE noncompetitive inhibition. In the pure noncompetitive case, inhibitor binding to free enzyme and to the enzyme-substrate complex is equally strong, so Vmax falls while Km is unchanged; when those two affinities differ, the inhibition is mixed and the apparent Km changes as well. In competitive inhibition the inhibitor competes directly for the active site.

**D. The inhibitor binds to the enzyme-substrate complex and decreases both K_m and V_max:** An inhibitor that binds the enzyme-substrate complex and decreases both Km and Vmax describes uncompetitive inhibition. Uncompetitive inhibitors bind only after the substrate has bound, reducing both the apparent Km and Vmax by the same factor. This produces parallel lines on a Lineweaver-Burk plot, a distinctive feature that distinguishes it from competitive and noncompetitive inhibition.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for the active site on the free enzyme; it commonly resembles the substrate, but that resemblance is not required.
  - Apparent Kₘ rises, meaning a higher substrate concentration is needed to reach half-maximal rate, while Vₘₐₓ is unchanged because excess substrate can outcompete the inhibitor.
  - On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, competitive inhibition changes the slope and x-intercept, but the lines share a y-intercept of 1/Vₘₐₓ.
  - Contrast with pure non-competitive inhibition (Vₘₐₓ falls, Kₘ unchanged) and uncompetitive inhibition (both fall together).
  - Because the inhibition is surmountable, raising substrate concentration reverses a competitive inhibitor's effect.

Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is a coenzyme derived from which vitamin?

  B) Vitamin B₁ (thiamine)
  C) Vitamin B₆ (pyridoxine)
  D) Vitamin B₁₂ (cobalamin)

### Correct Answer: B. Vitamin B₁ (thiamine)

Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is the biologically active form of vitamin B₁ (thiamine). It serves as an essential coenzyme for pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and transketolase. Deficiency of thiamine leads to beriberi and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Vitamin B₂ (riboflavin):** Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) is the precursor for the coenzymes FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) and FMN (flavin mononucleotide), not TPP. FAD and FMN are electron carriers in oxidation-reduction reactions, notably in the electron transport chain (Complex I uses FMN, Complex II uses FAD) and in fatty acid β-oxidation.

**C. Vitamin B₆ (pyridoxine):** Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) is converted to pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the most versatile coenzyme in amino acid metabolism.   - Deficiency causes beriberi (wet: cardiac; dry: neuropathic) and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, classically in chronic alcohol use.
  - Map each B vitamin to its coenzyme: B₁ to TPP, B₂ to FAD, B₃ to NAD⁺, B₅ to coenzyme A, B₆ to PLP, B₇ to biotin, B₉ to THF.

Q57  [bio-338]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
In a Lineweaver-Burk plot, uncompetitive inhibition is characterized by:

  A) Lines that intersect on the y-axis
  B) Lines that intersect on the x-axis
  C) Parallel lines with the same slope but different y-intercepts
  D) Lines that intersect in the second quadrant

### Correct Answer: C. Parallel lines with the same slope but different y-intercepts

Uncompetitive inhibition produces parallel lines on a Lineweaver-Burk plot because both Kₘ and Vₘₐₓ are decreased by the same factor. The inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex (not free enzyme), so the ratio Kₘ/Vₘₐₓ (the slope) remains constant while both the y-intercept (1/Vₘₐₓ) and x-intercept (−1/Kₘ) change.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Lines that intersect on the y-axis:** Lines intersecting on the y-axis is the pattern for competitive inhibition on a Lineweaver-Burk plot. Both lines share the same y-intercept (1/Vmax is unchanged) but have different slopes and x-intercepts (apparent Km changes). This makes sense because competitive inhibition does not alter Vmax but increases the apparent Km.

**B. Lines that intersect on the x-axis:** Lines intersecting on the x-axis is the pattern for PURE noncompetitive inhibition. Both lines share the same x-intercept (-1/Km is unchanged) while the y-intercepts and slopes differ, because Vmax decreases and the apparent Km stays the same.

**D. Lines that intersect in the second quadrant:** A quadrant-II intersection is one pattern of mixed inhibition rather than a universal feature of it. In the standard model, preferential binding to the free enzyme raises the apparent Km and gives a quadrant-II intersection, while preferential binding to the enzyme-substrate complex lowers the apparent Km and gives a quadrant-III intersection; equal affinities give the pure noncompetitive case, intersecting on the x-axis. Uncompetitive inhibition instead produces parallel lines.

### Key Educational Points:

  - An uncompetitive inhibitor binds only the enzyme-substrate complex, never the free enzyme.
  - Both Kₘ and Vₘₐₓ decrease by the same factor, so their ratio - the Lineweaver-Burk slope - is unchanged.
  - An unchanged slope with shifted intercepts is what produces the characteristic parallel lines.
  - Reading the plot: slope = Kₘ/Vₘₐₓ, y-intercept = 1/Vₘₐₓ, x-intercept = -1/Kₘ.
  - Because the inhibitor requires substrate to be bound, adding more substrate does not relieve uncompetitive inhibition.

Q58  [bio-339]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which enzyme class catalyzes the transfer of a functional group from one molecule to another?

  A) Oxidoreductases
  B) Transferases
  C) Hydrolases
  D) Lyases

### Correct Answer: B. Transferases

Transferases (EC class 2) catalyze the transfer of functional groups such as methyl, amino, phosphoryl, and glycosyl groups from a donor molecule to an acceptor. Examples include kinases (transfer phosphate groups) and aminotransferases (transfer amino groups). Oxidoreductases catalyze redox reactions, hydrolases cleave bonds using water, and lyases carry out nonhydrolytic cleavage or the reverse addition across a double bond.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Oxidoreductases:** Oxidoreductases (EC class 1) catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions, transferring electrons between molecules. Examples include dehydrogenases (like lactate dehydrogenase, which uses NAD+ as electron acceptor), oxidases (like cytochrome c oxidase), and peroxidases. Many use cofactors such as NAD+, FAD, or metal ions to facilitate electron transfer, although not every oxidoreductase requires one.

**C. Hydrolases:** Hydrolases (EC class 3) catalyze the cleavage of bonds using water (hydrolysis). Examples include proteases (break peptide bonds), lipases (break ester bonds in lipids), nucleases (break phosphodiester bonds in nucleic acids), and glycosidases (break glycosidic bonds in carbohydrates). Digestive enzymes like pepsin, trypsin, and amylase are all hydrolases.

**D. Lyases:** Lyases (EC class 4) catalyze nonhydrolytic cleavage of a bond, often forming a double bond or a ring, as well as the reverse reaction of adding a group across a double bond. Examples include decarboxylases (remove CO2), aldolase (splits fructose-1,6-bisphosphate in glycolysis), and carbonic anhydrase (EC 4.2.1.1, which adds water across the CO2 double bond; water taking part does not make it a hydrolase). They do not transfer groups between molecules.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Transferases (EC class 2) move a functional group from a donor molecule to an acceptor.
  - Kinases (phosphoryl transfer) and aminotransferases (amino transfer) are the two most commonly tested examples.
  - The six classical EC classes: oxidoreductases (redox), transferases (group transfer), hydrolases (cleavage using water), lyases (non-hydrolytic cleavage, or addition across a double bond), isomerases (rearrangement), ligases (energy-dependent joining of molecules, usually using ATP). The current EC classification adds a seventh class, the translocases (EC 7).
  - Classify an enzyme by the reaction it performs, not by the substrate it acts on.
  - Ligases are distinguished from lyases because they couple bond formation to an energy source, usually ATP hydrolysis; NAD+-dependent DNA ligase shows that a nucleoside triphosphate is not an absolute requirement.

Q60  [bio-341]  Answer: B

An enzyme follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics and has a Kₘ of 5 mM for substrate A and a Kₘ of 0.1 mM for substrate B. Which conclusion follows from these values alone?

  A) The enzyme has higher affinity for substrate A
  B) Half-maximal reaction rate is reached at a lower concentration of substrate B than of substrate A
  C) The enzyme reaches Vₘₐₓ more quickly with substrate A
  D) Kₘ values cannot be compared between different substrates

### Correct Answer: B. Half-maximal reaction rate is reached at a lower concentration of substrate B than of substrate A

Kₘ is the substrate concentration at which the reaction rate is half of that enzyme-substrate pair's Vₘₐₓ. The reaction with A reaches half of its maximum rate at 5 mM, while the reaction with B reaches half of its maximum rate at 0.1 mM, so B requires a 50-fold lower concentration to reach its own half-maximal rate. Kₘ alone does not establish which substrate binds more tightly or which has the higher maximum rate, because Kₘ reflects both binding and catalytic steps.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. The enzyme has higher affinity for substrate A:** Binding affinity cannot be ranked from these Kₘ values alone. Substrate A does have the higher concentration requirement for half-maximal rate, but that is not the same as directly measuring binding strength.

**C. The enzyme reaches Vₘₐₓ more quickly with substrate A:** Kₘ is a concentration, not a time needed to reach a maximum rate. These values also tell us nothing about the catalytic rate constant, or about whether the two Vₘₐₓ values are equal.

**D. Kₘ values cannot be compared between different substrates:** Kₘ values can be compared, to determine the substrate concentrations associated with half-maximal rates. What they do not establish by themselves is binding affinity or overall substrate preference.

### Key Educational Points:

  - For Michaelis-Menten kinetics, Kₘ is the substrate concentration giving half Vₘₐₓ.
  - Here, substrate B reaches its own half-maximal rate at a 50-fold lower concentration than substrate A.
  - Kₘ is not generally identical to the substrate-binding dissociation constant K_d.
  - For fixed conditions and a given enzyme-substrate pair, Kₘ does not depend on enzyme concentration, whereas Vₘₐₓ does.
  - Comparing substrate use may require catalytic efficiency (k_cat/Kₘ) and substrate concentrations, rather than Kₘ alone.

> Bottom Line: Lower Kₘ means half-maximal rate at a lower substrate concentration; it does not by itself prove tighter binding.
