At the same temperature and pressure, O₂ (M = 32 g/mol) and He (M = 4 g/mol) effuse separately through identical tiny openings into a vacuum. Which gas effuses faster, and by what factor?

  A) O₂ effuses 2.83 times faster
  B) They effuse at equal rates
  C) He effuses 8 times faster
  D) He effuses 2.83 times faster

### Correct Answer: D. He effuses 2.83 times faster

Graham's law states rate1/rate2 = sqrt(M2/M1). Thus rateHe/rateO2 = sqrt(32/4) = sqrt(8) = 2.83.

Lighter molecules have higher average speeds at the same temperature, so they effuse more rapidly through a small opening.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. O₂ effuses 2.83 times faster:** This reverses the relationship. Lighter gases effuse faster, not heavier ones. O2 (M=32) is heavier than He (M=4), so O2 effuses slower. By Graham's law, rateHe/rateO2 = sqrt(32/4) = sqrt(8) = 2.83, meaning He is faster.

**B. They effuse at equal rates:** Effusion rate depends inversely on the square root of molar mass (Graham's law). Since O2 and He have very different molar masses (32 vs. 4 g/mol), they effuse at very different rates. Under the same temperature, pressure and opening conditions, equal effusion rates would require equal molar masses.

**C. He effuses 8 times faster:** The factor 8 is the ratio of molar masses (32/4 = 8), but Graham's law uses the square root of this ratio: rate1/rate2 = sqrt(M2/M1) = sqrt(8) = 2.83. A common error is forgetting to take the square root.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Graham's law: the rate of effusion is inversely proportional to the square root of molar mass.
  - The ratio is √(M₂/M₁) = √(32/4) = √8 ≈ 2.83, so helium effuses about 2.83 times faster than O₂.
  - At a given temperature, ideal gas particles have the same average translational kinetic energy, so lighter particles move faster on average.
  - Because the dependence is on a square root, a molar mass eight times smaller gives a speed only about 2.83 times greater - not eight times.
  - Effusion is escape through a tiny opening; diffusion is spreading through space or another gas. Diffusion also depends on collisions and concentration gradients, so the simple effusion formula is not a universal diffusion-rate equation.

>> Bottom Line: Graham's law - the rate of effusion is inversely proportional to the square root of molar mass.

At the triple point of water on a phase diagram, which phases coexist in equilibrium?

  A) Solid and liquid only
  B) Liquid and gas only
  C) Solid, liquid, and gas
  D) Solid and gas only

### Correct Answer: C. Solid, liquid, and gas

The triple point is the unique temperature and pressure at which all three phases of a substance coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium. For water, this occurs at approximately 273.16 K and 611 Pa.

The water triple point historically defined the kelvin and remains useful for temperature calibration. Since 2019, the kelvin has been defined using a fixed value of the Boltzmann constant.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Solid and liquid only:** Solid and liquid coexisting describes any point along the solid-liquid boundary line (melting/freezing curve) on the phase diagram. The triple point is unique because it is the only condition where all three phases coexist simultaneously.

**B. Liquid and gas only:** Liquid and gas coexisting describes any point along the liquid-gas boundary line (vaporization curve), from the triple point up to the critical point. The triple point additionally includes the solid phase.

**D. Solid and gas only:** Solid and gas coexisting without liquid describes points along the solid-gas boundary line (sublimation curve). At the triple point, liquid is also present -- it is the intersection of all three boundary lines.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The triple point is the single temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid, and gas coexist in equilibrium.
  - It is where all three phase-boundary lines meet on a phase diagram - a point, not a line or region.
  - For water it lies at 0.01 °C and about 611 Pa, far below atmospheric pressure.
  - Do not confuse it with the critical point, which is where the liquid-gas boundary ends and those two phases become indistinguishable.
  - Because it is fixed and reproducible for a pure substance, the triple point of water was long used to define the kelvin.

>> Bottom Line: The triple point is where solid, liquid, and gas phases coexist simultaneously in equilibrium.

A fixed amount of ideal gas occupies 5.0 L at 0 °C and 1 atm. Approximately what volume will it occupy at 2.0 atm and 273 °C?

  A) 5.0 L
  B) 2.5 L
  C) 10.0 L
  D) 20.0 L

### Correct Answer: A. 5.0 L

Use P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2 with temperatures in kelvin. T1 = 273.15 K and T2 = 546.15 K, so V2 = (1 × 5.0 × 546.15)/(2.0 × 273.15) ≈ 5.0 L. The absolute temperature approximately doubles while the pressure doubles, so the effects nearly cancel.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. 2.5 L:** Halving the volume to 2.5 L would account for doubling pressure while ignoring the temperature rise. The absolute temperature also approximately doubles, so the final volume is about 5.0 L.

**C. 10.0 L:** Getting 10.0 L might result from only accounting for the temperature increase (doubling T doubles V) while ignoring the pressure increase. Both changes must be considered: the absolute temperature approximately doubles, which tends to double V, while doubling P tends to halve V, so the two effects nearly cancel and V stays at about 5.0 L.

**D. 20.0 L:** 20.0 L would result from doubling the volume twice (once for temperature, once for pressure in the wrong direction). Increasing pressure compresses gas (reduces volume), it does not expand it. The correct answer accounts for both effects simultaneously; because the absolute temperature only approximately doubles, the volume returns to about 5.0 L.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The combined gas law P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ handles simultaneous changes in pressure and temperature.
  - Use kelvin: 0 °C = 273.15 K and 273 °C = 546.15 K.
  - The absolute temperature approximately doubles and the pressure doubles, so their opposing effects nearly cancel; the answer rounds to 5.0 L.
  - Spotting that competing changes cancel is faster and safer than grinding through the arithmetic.
  - Celsius temperatures cannot be used in gas-law ratios. Here the initial 0 °C would even cause division by zero.

>> Bottom Line: The combined gas law relates pressure, volume, and temperature changes for a fixed amount of gas.

For an ideal gas, what happens to the average translational kinetic energy per particle when the absolute temperature is doubled?

  A) It stays the same
  B) It doubles
  C) It quadruples
  D) It increases by a factor of 1.41

### Correct Answer: B. It doubles

The average translational kinetic energy per particle is given by KEavg = (3/2)kT, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T is absolute temperature. Since this energy is directly proportional to T, doubling T doubles it.

This relationship for the average translational kinetic energy holds for all ideal gases regardless of their identity or molar mass.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. It stays the same:** Average translational kinetic energy is directly proportional to absolute temperature (KEavg = 3/2 kT). If temperature changes, this energy must change proportionally. It stays the same only if temperature is unchanged.

**C. It quadruples:** Translational kinetic energy is directly (linearly) proportional to absolute temperature, not to the square of temperature. Doubling absolute temperature doubles this energy. Quadrupling would require quadrupling the absolute temperature.

**D. It increases by a factor of 1.41:** 1.41 (which is the square root of 2) relates to molecular speed, not to translational kinetic energy. The root-mean-square speed is proportional to the square root of temperature (vrms = sqrt(3RT/M)), so doubling T increases speed by a factor of sqrt(2) = 1.41. But the average translational kinetic energy, being proportional to v², doubles.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The average translational kinetic energy of an ideal gas depends only on absolute temperature: KE = (3/2)kT per molecule.
  - Doubling the absolute temperature therefore doubles the average kinetic energy.
  - The mean translational kinetic energy depends on temperature, not on particle identity or molar mass.
  - Since KE = ½mv², equal energy means heavier molecules move more slowly: speed depends on mass, but energy does not.
  - The temperature must be absolute - going from 10 °C to 20 °C is nowhere near a doubling.

>> Bottom Line: The average translational kinetic energy per ideal-gas particle is (3/2)kT, so doubling kelvin temperature doubles this energy.

Real gases deviate most from ideal behavior under which conditions?

  A) High temperature and low pressure
  B) Low temperature and high pressure
  C) High temperature and high pressure
  D) Low temperature and low pressure

### Correct Answer: B. Low temperature and high pressure

At low temperatures, gas molecules move slowly and intermolecular attractive forces become significant. At high pressures, molecules are close together and their finite volume becomes a significant fraction of the container volume.

Both of these factors cause deviations from the ideal gas law assumptions of negligible intermolecular forces and negligible molecular volume.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. High temperature and low pressure:** High temperature and low pressure are actually the conditions where real gases behave most ideally. At high T, molecules have so much kinetic energy that intermolecular attractions become negligible. At low P, molecules are far apart, making their volume insignificant.

**C. High temperature and high pressure:** High temperature reduces the relative importance of attractive forces, but high pressure can still produce substantial deviations because particles occupy space. The size of the deviation depends on the gas and the actual conditions.

**D. Low temperature and low pressure:** Low temperature increases the relative importance of attractions, but low pressure keeps particles farther apart. The combination of low temperature and high pressure more strongly challenges the ideal model; no fixed degree of deviation follows from these labels alone.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Real gases deviate most from ideal behaviour at low temperature and high pressure.
  - The ideal model makes two assumptions - that molecules have negligible volume and exert no attractions - and both fail under these conditions.
  - At lower temperature, attractive interactions are more important relative to translational kinetic energy. Attractions can lower pressure below the ideal prediction, while short-range repulsion and excluded volume can dominate under strong compression.
  - At high pressure the molecules' own volume becomes a significant fraction of the container, so the free volume is less than assumed.
  - Gases behave most ideally at high temperature and low pressure, where molecules are fast-moving and far apart.

>> Bottom Line: Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at low temperatures and high pressures.

In a mixture of gases at 2.0 atm total pressure, nitrogen has a mole fraction of 0.70. What is the partial pressure of nitrogen?

  A) 0.70 atm
  B) 0.35 atm
  C) 2.70 atm
  D) 1.40 atm

### Correct Answer: D. 1.40 atm

The partial pressure of a gas equals its mole fraction times the total pressure: PN2 = XN2 x Ptotal = 0.70 x 2.0 = 1.40 atm.

Mole fraction is a convenient way to express composition and directly relates to partial pressure via Dalton's law.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 0.70 atm:** 0.70 is the dimensionless mole fraction; it is not itself a pressure of 0.70 atm. Partial pressure = mole fraction x total pressure = 0.70 x 2.0 = 1.40 atm. A mole fraction is always between 0 and 1 and has no units; it must be multiplied by total pressure to get partial pressure.

**B. 0.35 atm:** 0.35 would result from dividing the mole fraction by the total pressure (0.70/2.0) instead of multiplying. The correct formula is Pi = Xi x Ptotal = 0.70 x 2.0 = 1.40 atm.

**C. 2.70 atm:** 2.70 atm would result from adding mole fraction and total pressure (0.70 + 2.0), which is not a valid operation. Partial pressure is found by multiplying: PN2 = XN2 x Ptotal = 0.70 x 2.0 = 1.40 atm.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Partial pressure equals mole fraction times total pressure: P_i = x_i × P_total, so 0.70 × 2.0 = 1.40 atm.
  - Mole fraction is moles of one component divided by total moles, so it is dimensionless and all fractions in a mixture sum to 1.
  - Pressure tracks the number of particles rather than their identity, which is why composition by moles converts directly into pressure.
  - This is Dalton's law expressed as a fraction rather than as a sum - the same physical idea.
  - Mole fraction is not the same as mass fraction; converting between them requires the molar masses.

>> Bottom Line: Partial pressure = mole fraction x total pressure.

### Key Educational Points:

  - SnH₄ is the heaviest, so it has the strongest dispersion forces and the highest boiling point.

Q11  [chem-73]  Answer: A   (UNCHANGED per the review)
==============================================================================
Moving along the liquid-gas boundary line on a phase diagram toward higher temperatures, the line terminates at the:

  A) Critical point
  B) Triple point
  C) Normal boiling point
  D) Eutectic point

### Correct Answer: A. Critical point

The liquid-gas boundary line on a phase diagram ends at the critical point. Beyond this temperature and pressure, the distinction between liquid and gas disappears and the substance exists as a supercritical fluid.

At the critical point, the densities of the liquid and gas phase become equal and the surface tension vanishes.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Triple point:** The triple point is at the lower end of the liquid-gas boundary, where it meets the solid-liquid and solid-gas boundaries. Moving along the liquid-gas line toward higher temperatures leads away from the triple point, toward the critical point.

**C. Normal boiling point:** The normal boiling point is a specific point on the liquid-gas boundary line where the pressure equals 1 atm. It is not the endpoint of the line. The boundary continues beyond the normal boiling point to higher temperatures and pressures until it reaches the critical point.

**D. Eutectic point:** A eutectic point appears in phase diagrams of mixtures (like alloys or salt solutions), not in single-component phase diagrams. It represents the lowest melting point of a mixture. The endpoint of the liquid-gas line for a pure substance is the critical point.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The liquid-gas boundary line ends at the critical point; beyond it, liquid and gas are no longer distinguishable.
  - At that point the densities of the two phases become equal and the meniscus between them disappears.
  - Above the critical temperature and pressure the substance is a supercritical fluid, diffusing like a gas but dissolving like a liquid.
  - Do not confuse it with the triple point, where all three phases coexist - the critical point terminates a single boundary line.
  - A gas above its critical temperature cannot be liquefied by pressure alone, no matter how much is applied.

>> Bottom Line: The critical point marks the end of the liquid-gas coexistence curve; beyond it exists a supercritical fluid.

**D. 24 mmHg:** 24 mmHg is just the water vapor pressure at 25 degrees C, not the pressure of the collected gas. The dry gas pressure is much larger: Pgas = Ptotal - Pwater = 765 - 24 = 741 mmHg.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Gas collected over water is a mixture of the gas and water vapour, so Dalton's law applies.
  - P_dry gas = P_total − P_water vapour = 765 − 24 = 741 mmHg.
