Q1  [chem-302]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which molecule can exhibit optical activity?

  A. Propan-2-ol
  B. Butan-2-ol   <= key
  C. 2-methylpropan-2-ol
  D. Methanol

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Butan-2-ol
Butan-2-ol (CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃) has a chiral center at C2, bonded to four different groups: –H, –OH, –CH₃, and –CH₂CH₃. A sample containing more of one enantiomer than the other can rotate plane-polarized light; an equal mixture of the two enantiomers has no net rotation. The other molecules either lack four different substituents on any carbon (no chirality center) or are too symmetric.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Propan-2-ol: Propan-2-ol (CH₃CH(OH)CH₃) has a central carbon bonded to –OH, –H, and two identical –CH₃ groups. Since two substituents are the same, this carbon is not a chiral center, so propan-2-ol cannot rotate plane-polarized light.
C. 2-methylpropan-2-ol: 2-Methylpropan-2-ol ((CH₃)₃COH) is a tertiary alcohol where the central carbon bears three identical methyl groups plus one –OH. With three of four substituents identical, there is no chiral center and no optical activity.
D. Methanol: Methanol (CH₃OH) is the simplest alcohol. Its carbon is bonded to three hydrogen atoms and one –OH group, giving it no chiral center. For a tetrahedral carbon to be a chirality center, its four attached groups must be different.
Key Educational Points:
  - Chirality means that a molecule is not superimposable on its mirror image. A pure enantiomer can show optical activity, whereas a racemate has no net rotation.
  - In butan-2-ol, C2 carries H, OH, CH₃, and C₂H₅ - four different groups, so it is a stereocentre.
  - For these alcohols, inspect each tetrahedral carbon for four different attached groups.
  - Check the whole structure as well: a molecule with stereocenters can still be achiral, as in a meso compound.
  - A racemic mixture of equal enantiomers also shows no net rotation, even though each molecule in it is chiral.

Q2  [chem-303]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Meso-tartaric acid has two stereocenters but is optically inactive. Which feature explains its overall achirality?

  A. Have no chiral centers at all
  B. Possess an internal plane of symmetry   <= key
  C. Are racemic mixtures
  D. Contain only one chiral center

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Possess an internal plane of symmetry
Meso-tartaric acid has an internal mirror plane in an appropriate conformation, making the molecule superimposable on its mirror image. It is therefore achiral despite having two stereocenters. A meso compound is a single achiral substance, whereas a racemate is an equal mixture of two different enantiomers.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Have no chiral centers at all: Meso-tartaric acid does have two stereocenters. The presence of stereocenters alone does not establish that the whole molecule is chiral; its overall symmetry also matters.
C. Are racemic mixtures: A racemate contains equal amounts of two enantiomers whose optical rotations cancel in the sample. Meso-tartaric acid is one achiral compound, so its lack of optical rotation does not require an enantiomer mixture.
D. Contain only one chiral center: Meso-tartaric acid has two stereocenters, not one. Its achirality follows from the relationship between the two halves of the molecule, rather than from an absence of stereocenters.
Key Educational Points:
  - A meso compound has stereocenters but is achiral as a whole.
  - Meso-tartaric acid illustrates how an internal mirror plane can make a structure superimposable on its mirror image.
  - A meso substance and a racemate are different: one is achiral, while the other contains two enantiomers in equal amounts.
  - Separating a racemate can give its individual enantiomers; a meso compound cannot be separated into an enantiomeric pair.
  - For a structure whose only stereogenic features are n tetrahedral centers, 2ⁿ is an upper bound; symmetry can reduce the number of distinct stereoisomers.

Q3  [chem-304]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: When the atoms directly attached to a stereocenter are different elements, what determines their initial priority in the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) system?

  A. Molecular weight of the entire group
  B. Atomic number of the atom directly bonded to the chiral center   <= key
  C. Electronegativity of the substituent
  D. Number of bonds in the substituent

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Atomic number of the atom directly bonded to the chiral center
Compare the directly attached atoms first: the element with the higher atomic number has higher priority. For isotopes of the same element, the higher mass number takes priority, such as ²H over ¹H. If the directly attached atoms tie, compare the next sets of attached atoms in descending priority order and use the first difference.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Molecular weight of the entire group: The total molecular mass of a substituent is not the criterion. Compare atoms in sequence: for –CH₂OH and –CH₂CH₃, the first atoms are both carbon, but the next comparison includes oxygen versus carbon, so –CH₂OH ranks higher.
C. Electronegativity of the substituent: Electronegativity describes attraction for bonding electrons. It is not the ranking rule here: the initial comparison of different elements uses atomic number.
D. Number of bonds in the substituent: Simply counting bonds does not assign priority. Multiple bonds are represented using duplicate atoms when applying the comparison rules. Double and triple bonds are handled by the phantom atom rule: a C=O double bond is treated as if carbon is bonded to two oxygen atoms and oxygen to two carbon atoms.
Key Educational Points:
  - For directly attached atoms of different elements, higher atomic number means higher priority.
  - When directly attached atoms tie, compare the next attached atoms in descending priority order and stop at the first difference.
  - A double bond is treated as though the atom were duplicated.
  - Isotopes are an important exception to a simple atomic-number comparison: the isotope with the higher mass number ranks higher.
  - With the lowest-priority group pointing away from you, 1 to 2 to 3 clockwise is R and anticlockwise is S.

Q4  [chem-305]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: A compound with the configuration (R)-2-bromobutane and its mirror image (S)-2-bromobutane are:

  A. Identical
  B. Enantiomers   <= key
  C. Diastereomers
  D. Constitutional isomers

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Enantiomers
Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images that differ in the configuration at every chiral center. (R)-2-bromobutane and (S)-2-bromobutane are mirror images with opposite configurations at the single stereocenter. Enantiomers have the same ordinary physical properties in achiral environments, but opposite optical rotations under the same measurement conditions; they can interact differently with chiral substances.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Identical: The two structures have the same connectivity, but they cannot be superimposed: the four groups around C2 have opposite three-dimensional arrangements. They are different stereoisomers, rather than two drawings of the same configuration.
C. Diastereomers: Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. Examples include E/Z alkene pairs and compounds differing at some, but not all, of several stereocenters. These two bromobutanes are instead a non-superimposable mirror-image pair.
D. Constitutional isomers: Constitutional (structural) isomers have the same molecular formula but different atom connectivity - for example, butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol. (R)- and (S)-2-bromobutane have identical connectivity; they differ only in the 3D arrangement of atoms around the chiral center, making them stereoisomers (specifically enantiomers).
Key Educational Points:
  - Two molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images are enantiomers.
  - With a single stereocentre, inverting it produces the mirror image, so R and S forms of 2-bromobutane are an enantiomeric pair.
  - In achiral environments, enantiomers share ordinary physical properties; they can behave differently toward chiral molecules.
  - Diastereomers are not mirror images and generally have different physical properties, which can help separate them.
  - R/S is an assigned configurational label, not a measurement - it does not tell you which way the compound rotates light, which must be determined experimentally.

Q5  [chem-306]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which statement about diastereomers is correct?

  A. They are mirror images of each other
  B. They have identical physical properties
  C. They are stereoisomers that are NOT mirror images   <= key
  D. They always have opposite optical rotations

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. They are stereoisomers that are NOT mirror images
Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. They may differ at some of several stereocenters, or they may be E/Z alkene isomers. Their physical properties generally differ, so methods such as crystallization or chromatography can often separate them.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. They are mirror images of each other: Stereoisomers that are mirror images are called enantiomers, not diastereomers. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are specifically NOT mirror images. For example, (2R,3R)-tartaric acid and (2R,3S)-tartaric acid (meso) are diastereomers because they differ at one stereocenter but not all.
B. They have identical physical properties: Diastereomers are not required to have identical physical properties. Differences in properties such as melting point or solubility often allow separation by ordinary methods; this does not mean that every property must differ.
D. They always have opposite optical rotations: Pure enantiomers have equal and opposite optical rotations when measured under the same conditions. Diastereomers have no such predictable relationship - their optical rotations can differ in both magnitude and sign. There is no rule requiring them to be opposite.
Key Educational Points:
  - Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of one another.
  - Examples include structures differing at some of several stereocenters and E/Z alkene pairs.
  - Differences in physical properties often make diastereomers separable by ordinary laboratory methods.
  - Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images and have the same ordinary physical properties in achiral environments.
  - Cis-trans isomers are themselves a kind of diastereomer: stereoisomers that are not mirror images.

Q6  [chem-307]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: The E/Z system for naming geometric isomers uses which priority rule?

  A. Alphabetical order of substituents
  B. Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules   <= key
  C. Molecular weight of substituents
  D. Size of substituents

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules
The E/Z system assigns priorities to the two substituents on each carbon of the double bond using CIP rules, including atomic-number comparisons and the mass-number rule for isotopes. If the higher-priority groups are on the same side, the isomer is Z (zusammen, German for 'together'). If on opposite sides, it is E (entgegen, 'opposite'). This system is more general than cis/trans.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Alphabetical order of substituents: Alphabetical order does not determine E/Z priority. Apply the CIP rules separately at each alkene carbon, then compare the positions of the two higher-priority groups.
C. Molecular weight of substituents: The total molecular mass of a substituent does not determine E/Z priority. CIP compares atoms in sequence; when comparing isotopes of the same element, however, the higher mass number does give higher priority.
D. Size of substituents: Bulk alone does not set priority. For example, bromine outranks a group directly attached through carbon because Br has the higher atomic number, even if that carbon-containing group is much larger.
Key Educational Points:
  - The E/Z system assigns priority on each alkene carbon using CIP rules, exactly as R/S does at a stereocentre.
  - Z (zusammen, together) means the two higher-priority groups are on the same side; E (entgegen, opposite) means they are on opposite sides.
  - E/Z exists because cis/trans becomes ambiguous once the two carbons carry four different substituents.
  - Z is not simply a synonym for cis: the labels coincide only when the priority groups happen to be the ones cis/trans referred to.
  - CIP first compares atomic numbers, uses mass numbers to distinguish isotopes, and works outward to resolve remaining ties.

Q7  [chem-308]  correct answer: B   (kept unchanged)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: How many stereoisomers exist for 2,3-dibromobutane?

  A. 2
  B. 3   <= key
  C. 4
  D. 5

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. 3
2,3-Dibromobutane has two chiral centers (C2 and C3). Normally, 2² = 4 stereoisomers would be expected. However, because the molecule has identical substituents on C2 and C3 (–CH₃ and –Br), one pair of configurations creates a meso compound (with an internal mirror plane). This reduces the total to 3 stereoisomers: (R,R), (S,S), and meso (R,S).
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. 2: Two chiral centers normally give 2² = 4 stereoisomers. However, 2,3-dibromobutane is a special case because the two halves of the molecule are identical. This creates a meso form (with an internal mirror plane) that reduces the count from 4 to 3: one (R,R), one (S,S), and one meso (R,S).
C. 4: The formula 2ⁿ = 2² = 4 gives the maximum possible stereoisomers for two chiral centers. However, because 2,3-dibromobutane has identical substituent patterns on C2 and C3, one pair of expected stereoisomers collapses into a single meso compound. This reduces the count from 4 to 3.
D. 5: Five stereoisomers is not possible from two chiral centers, since the maximum from 2ⁿ is 4, and the actual count can only decrease (due to meso forms), never increase beyond 2ⁿ. The correct answer is 3: a pair of enantiomers and one meso form.
Key Educational Points:
  - The 2ⁿ rule predicts 4 stereoisomers for two stereocentres, but 2,3-dibromobutane has only 3.
  - The two halves of the molecule are identical, so one combination possesses an internal mirror plane and is a meso compound.
  - The three are therefore one pair of enantiomers plus a single achiral meso form.
  - Always check for internal symmetry before applying 2ⁿ - it gives an upper limit, not a guaranteed count.
  - The meso form is a diastereomer of each enantiomer, so it differs in physical properties and can be separated from them.

Q8  [chem-309]  correct answer: D
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Glucose is classified as:

  A. A ketopentose
  B. An aldopentose
  C. A ketohexose
  D. An aldohexose   <= key

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: D. An aldohexose
Glucose is an aldohexose: it has six carbon atoms, and its open-chain form has an aldehyde at C1. In water it exists mainly as cyclic hemiacetals, but its classification still refers to the open-chain structure. Fructose is a ketohexose because its open-chain carbonyl is a ketone.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A ketopentose: A ketopentose is a 5-carbon sugar with a ketone (C=O) functional group. Ribulose is an example. Open-chain glucose has 6 carbons and an aldehyde group, so it is neither a pentose nor a ketose. Remember: “aldo-” = aldehyde at C1, “keto-” = ketone (usually at C2), “-pentose” = 5 carbons, “-hexose” = 6 carbons.
B. An aldopentose: An aldopentose is a 5-carbon sugar with an aldehyde group. Ribose (found in RNA) is a classic example. The open-chain form of glucose has an aldehyde but has 6 carbons, not 5, making it an aldohexose.
C. A ketohexose: A ketohexose has six carbons and a ketone in its open-chain structure, as in fructose. Open-chain glucose instead has an aldehyde at C1, so glucose is an aldohexose.
Key Educational Points:
  - Monosaccharides are classified by carbon count and by the carbonyl group in their open-chain form.
  - Open-chain glucose has six carbons and an aldehyde at C1, giving the name aldohexose.
  - Open-chain fructose has six carbons and a ketone at C2, giving the name ketohexose.
  - Aldoses are reducing sugars because the aldehyde can be oxidised; ketoses such as fructose still test positive, because alkaline test conditions isomerise them to aldoses.
  - The D in D-glucose refers to the configuration at the highest-numbered stereocentre (C5) relative to D-glyceraldehyde. It is a structural label and says nothing about which way the sugar rotates polarised light.

Q9  [chem-310]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: The α and β anomers of D-glucose differ in the configuration at:

  A. C-2
  B. C-4
  C. C-1 (the anomeric carbon)   <= key
  D. C-6

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. C-1 (the anomeric carbon)
When glucose cyclizes to form the pyranose ring, the C1 aldehyde carbon becomes a new stereocenter called the anomeric carbon. In the usual Haworth drawing of D-glucopyranose, the α-OH is down and trans to the C6 CH₂OH group; the β-OH is up and cis to it. In the preferred ⁴C₁ chair, these hydroxyls are axial and equatorial, respectively; a chair flip exchanges axial and equatorial positions without changing α into β. This difference underlies mutarotation.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. C-2: C-2 is the carbon whose configuration distinguishes epimers like glucose and mannose (they differ only at C-2). The anomeric carbon in glucose is C-1, which is the carbon that forms a new stereocenter when the open-chain form cyclizes into the ring form.
B. C-4: C-4 configuration distinguishes glucose from galactose (they are C-4 epimers). Anomers specifically differ at the anomeric carbon (C-1 in aldoses like glucose), which is the hemiacetal carbon formed during ring closure.
D. C-6: C-6 is the terminal carbon bearing a –CH₂OH group in glucose; it is not a stereocenter in the ring form and plays no role in anomer formation. The anomers differ at C1: their OH groups are trans or cis to the C6 CH₂OH group, respectively, in α- and β-D-glucopyranose.
Key Educational Points:
  - Cyclisation turns the C1 carbonyl carbon into a new stereocentre, known as the anomeric carbon.
  - α and β anomers differ only in the configuration at that one carbon.
  - In the preferred ⁴C₁ chair of D-glucopyranose, the β-anomer's OH is equatorial; axial/equatorial is a conformation label, not the definition of α/β.
  - Anomers are diastereomers rather than enantiomers, because they differ at only one of several stereocentres.
  - Humans digest starch's α-linked glucose chains but lack cellulase for the β(1→4) linkages in cellulose.

Q10  [chem-311]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Cellulose differs from starch primarily in:

  A. The monosaccharide units used
  B. The type of glycosidic linkage (α vs. β)   <= key
  C. The number of carbon atoms per unit
  D. The molecular weight

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. The type of glycosidic linkage (α vs. β)
Both cellulose and starch are polymers of D-glucose, but starch uses α-1,4 glycosidic bonds (and α-1,6 for branches), while cellulose uses β-1,4 glycosidic bonds. Human amylases hydrolyze starch's α(1→4) linkages, and other digestive enzymes finish digestion, including at branch points. Humans lack cellulase for cellulose's β(1→4) linkages.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. The monosaccharide units used: Both cellulose and starch are polymers of the same monosaccharide: D-glucose. The difference lies in how the glucose units are connected. Starch uses α(1→4) glycosidic bonds (easily digested by human amylase), while cellulose uses β(1→4) bonds (humans lack cellulase to break these, which is why we cannot digest cellulose/fiber).
C. The number of carbon atoms per unit: Both cellulose and starch are built from glucose, which has 6 carbon atoms per unit. The critical difference is the stereochemistry of the glycosidic bond: α in starch versus β in cellulose. This small geometric difference has enormous biological consequences for digestibility and structural properties.
D. The molecular weight: Both polymers have variable chain lengths and molecular masses. Their defining difference is linkage: starch contains α-linked glucose, whereas cellulose contains β(1→4)-linked glucose. The linkage changes chain shape and how enzymes recognize the polymer.
Key Educational Points:
  - Starch and cellulose are both polymers of D-glucose - they differ in the linkage, not in the monomer.
  - Starch uses α-1,4 bonds, with α-1,6 branches in amylopectin; cellulose uses β-1,4 bonds.
  - The β linkage flips alternate glucose units, producing straight chains that hydrogen-bond side by side into rigid fibres.
  - Human amylase acts on α(1→4) linkages in starch; cellulose requires cellulase, which humans do not produce.
  - Ruminants obtain cellulose digestion from gut microbes. Termites are not a valid example of animals universally lacking cellulase: they can use both their own enzymes and microbial enzymes.

Q11  [chem-312]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Lactose is a reducing sugar because:

  A. It contains fructose
  B. It has a free anomeric carbon on the galactose unit
  C. It has a free anomeric carbon on the glucose unit   <= key
  D. Both anomeric carbons are free

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. It has a free anomeric carbon on the glucose unit
Lactose consists of β-D-galactose linked β-1,4 to D-glucose. The glycosidic bond uses the C1 anomeric carbon of galactose, but the C1 of glucose remains free. This free anomeric carbon can open to form an aldehyde, which can reduce Tollens' or Fehling's reagent, making lactose a reducing sugar.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. It contains fructose: Lactose contains galactose and glucose, not fructose. Sucrose is the disaccharide that contains fructose (bonded to glucose). In lactose, galactose is linked through its C-1 anomeric carbon to C-4 of glucose, leaving glucose’s C-1 anomeric carbon free to open and act as the reducing end.
B. It has a free anomeric carbon on the galactose unit: In lactose, the galactose unit’s anomeric carbon (C-1) is involved in the β(1→4) glycosidic bond to glucose, so it is NOT free. It is the glucose unit that retains a free anomeric carbon capable of ring-opening to expose its aldehyde, which can then reduce Benedict’s or Fehling’s reagent.
D. Both anomeric carbons are free: Both anomeric carbons are not free in lactose. Galactose C1 participates in the β(1→4) linkage, while glucose C1 retains a free hemiacetal group and can open to the aldehyde form. That glucose end accounts for lactose's reducing behavior.
Key Educational Points:
  - Lactose is reducing because its glucose unit retains a free hemiacetal group that can open to an aldehyde.
  - In lactose the β-1,4 bond uses the anomeric carbon of galactose, leaving glucose's C1 free - hence it is reducing.
  - Sucrose is the contrasting case: its linkage ties up both anomeric carbons, so it is non-reducing.
  - Common free monosaccharides test as reducing sugars; fructose also reacts because alkaline conditions allow conversion to aldoses.
  - Benedict's and Fehling's tests detect reduction of copper(II); they are not specific tests for glucose or for an exposed aldehyde in the starting structure.

Q12  [chem-313]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: D-glucose and D-mannose are:

  A. Enantiomers
  B. Epimers differing at C-2   <= key
  C. Anomers
  D. Identical compounds

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Epimers differing at C-2
Epimers are diastereomers that differ in configuration at only one stereocenter. D-glucose and D-mannose have the same configuration at C3, C4, and C5, but opposite configuration at C2. D-glucose and D-galactose are C-4 epimers. Enantiomers would differ at every stereocenter (D-glucose vs. L-glucose).
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Enantiomers: D-glucose and L-glucose are enantiomers: their corresponding stereocenters all have opposite configurations. D-glucose and D-mannose instead differ only at C2 and are not mirror images, so they are epimers.
C. Anomers: Anomers are cyclic sugar forms that differ specifically at the anomeric carbon (C-1 in aldoses) - the α and β forms of the same sugar. D-glucose and D-mannose differ at C-2 in their open-chain forms, so they are epimers, not anomers.
D. Identical compounds: D-glucose and D-mannose have the same molecular formula (C₆H₁₂O₆) but different configurations at C-2. They are clearly different compounds with distinct physical properties (different melting points, specific rotations). The term for sugars differing at exactly one stereocenter is epimers.
Key Educational Points:
  - Epimers are diastereomers that differ in configuration at exactly one stereocentre.
  - D-glucose and D-mannose differ only at C2; D-glucose and D-galactose differ only at C4.
  - Enantiomers, by contrast, require every stereocentre to be inverted - that is D-glucose versus L-glucose.
  - Epimers are different compounds, and enzymes can distinguish their different configurations.
  - Anomers are a special case of epimers: their configuration differs specifically at the anomeric carbon.

Q13  [chem-314]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Mutarotation is the process by which:

  A. A sugar undergoes hydrolysis
  B. The optical rotation of a freshly dissolved sugar changes over time until equilibrium between anomers is reached   <= key
  C. A sugar loses water
  D. A polysaccharide breaks down

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. The optical rotation of a freshly dissolved sugar changes over time until equilibrium between anomers is reached
When a pure anomer of glucose (either α or β) is dissolved in water, the ring can open and reclose to form both anomers. This gradual interconversion causes the specific rotation to change over time until an equilibrium mixture is reached (approximately 36% α and 64% β for D-glucose at 25°C).
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A sugar undergoes hydrolysis: Hydrolysis is the cleavage of a bond by addition of water, as when a disaccharide is split into monosaccharides. C. A sugar loses water: Losing water describes a dehydration or condensation reaction (like forming a glycosidic bond). Mutarotation involves the ring opening and reclosing of a single sugar molecule in aqueous solution, allowing the α and β anomers to equilibrate. D. A polysaccharide breaks down: Polysaccharide breakdown (hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds by enzymes like amylase) is a completely different process. Mutarotation refers specifically to the spontaneous change in optical rotation observed when a pure anomer of a sugar is dissolved in water, as it equilibrates between its α and β forms.
Key Educational Points:
  - Mutarotation is the change in observed optical rotation as a freshly dissolved sugar equilibrates between its α and β anomers.
  - It occurs because the ring opens transiently to the free carbonyl form and can reclose either way.
  - At equilibrium D-glucose is roughly 64% β and 36% α, with only a trace present as the open chain.
  - In the preferred chair of D-glucopyranose, the β-anomer has an equatorial anomeric hydroxyl, helping explain its greater abundance in water.

  C. Caprolactam
  D. Styrene and butadiene

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid
Nylon-6,6 is a polyamide formed by the condensation of hexamethylenediamine (H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂) and adipic acid (HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH), with water eliminated at each step. Nylon-6,6 requires a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid, both with 6 carbons: hexamethylenediamine (H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂) and adipic acid (HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH).
C. Caprolactam: Caprolactam is the monomer for nylon-6, which is made by ring-opening polymerization of this cyclic amide. Nylon-6,6 is a different polymer made by condensation of two distinct monomers (a diamine and a diacid), each contributing 6 carbons - hence the “6,6” in the name.
D. Styrene and butadiene: Styrene and butadiene are monomers for styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), a synthetic rubber made by copolymerization. These are vinyl monomers that undergo addition polymerization. Nylon-6,6 is an entirely different class - a polyamide formed by condensation of hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid.
Key Educational Points:
  - Nylon-6,6 is formed from a six-carbon diamine and a six-carbon dicarboxylic acid.
  - Amine and carboxyl groups form amide linkages, making nylon-6,6 a polyamide.
  - Nylon-6 is a different polymer associated with caprolactam; do not confuse its name with nylon-6,6.

STEM: In an SN2 reaction, the rate depends on the concentration of:

  A. Only the substrate
  B. Only the nucleophile
  C. Both the substrate and the nucleophile   <= key
  D. Neither - it is zero order

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. Both the substrate and the nucleophile
SN2 (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution) follows second-order kinetics: rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]. The reaction occurs in a single concerted step where the nucleophile attacks the substrate from the back side while the leaving group departs simultaneously, resulting in inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Only the substrate: Rate depending only on the substrate describes SN1, not SN2. SN1 is first-order (rate = k[substrate]) because the rate-determining step is unimolecular ionization to form a carbocation. SN2 is second-order (rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]) because the nucleophile attacks in the same step as the leaving group departs.
B. Only the nucleophile: The rate of SN2 depends on both the nucleophile and the substrate, not just the nucleophile. The mechanism is a concerted, one-step process: the nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbon from the back side while the leaving group simultaneously departs. Both species must be present in the transition state.
D. Neither - it is zero order: A zero-order reaction has a rate independent of all reactant concentrations, which does not apply to SN2. The SN2 reaction is second-order overall: first-order in substrate and first-order in nucleophile. This means doubling either concentration doubles the rate.
Key Educational Points:
  - The elementary SN2 rate law is rate = k[substrate][nucleophile].
  - Doubling either concentration doubles the rate; doubling both gives a fourfold increase at the same temperature.
  - Backside attack and leaving-group departure occur together; at a stereogenic reacting carbon this inverts the arrangement of its bonds.

STEM: For ordinary saturated alkyl halides without allylic or benzylic stabilization, which combination generally favors an SN1 pathway?

  A. Primary substrates and strong nucleophiles
  B. Tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents   <= key
  C. Primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents
  D. Methyl substrates and weak nucleophiles

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents
SN1 involves ionization to a carbocation. Among comparable ordinary alkyl substrates, tertiary carbocations are more stable than secondary or primary ones. Polar protic solvents can stabilize the developing ions and favor this pathway. In the simple SN1 rate law, the slow ionization step depends on substrate concentration rather than nucleophile concentration.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Primary substrates and strong nucleophiles: Ordinary primary alkyl halides do not readily form primary carbocations. An unhindered primary substrate with a suitable strong nucleophile usually favors direct SN2 attack instead.
C. Primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents: An unhindered primary substrate and a polar aprotic solvent often favor SN2. Such solvents generally hydrogen-bond to anionic nucleophiles less strongly than protic solvents; they do not leave the anions completely unsolvated.
D. Methyl substrates and weak nucleophiles: Methyl substrates cannot form stable carbocations at all (a methyl cation would be extremely unstable). Methyl halides react almost exclusively by SN2. The combination of weak nucleophiles with methyl substrates would simply result in very slow reaction, not SN1.
Key Educational Points:
  - SN1 begins with ionization to a carbocation, so carbocation stability matters.
  - Tertiary substrates are favored over ordinary primary substrates under suitable ionizing conditions.
  - Polar protic solvents commonly favor SN1, but solvent labels alone do not determine a mechanism.

Correct Answer: B. Anti-periplanar
The anti-periplanar arrangement places the β-hydrogen and leaving group about 180° apart. For a cyclohexane chair, the usual anti geometry corresponds to a trans-diaxial hydrogen and leaving group.

STEM: E1 elimination shares which feature with SN1?

  A. Both require a strong base
  B. Both proceed via a carbocation intermediate   <= key
  C. Both give inversion of configuration
  D. Both follow second-order kinetics

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Both proceed via a carbocation intermediate
Both E1 and SN1 reactions begin with the same rate-determining step: ionization of the substrate to form a carbocation. In E1, a base then removes a β-proton to form a double bond. In SN1, a nucleophile attacks the carbocation. Because they share the same first step, E1 and SN1 often compete with each other, especially at higher temperatures where elimination is favored.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Both require a strong base: Neither E1 nor SN1 requires a strong base. Both are unimolecular reactions whose rate-determining step is spontaneous ionization of the substrate to form a carbocation. A strong base would promote E2 elimination instead. E1 and SN1 are typically favored by weak nucleophiles/bases and polar protic solvents.
C. Both give inversion of configuration: Backside inversion is characteristic of SN2. An SN1 carbocation can be attacked from either face, giving retention and inversion products, but they need not occur in an exactly 1:1 ratio. E1 instead forms an alkene.
D. Both follow second-order kinetics: Both E1 and SN1 follow first-order kinetics (rate = k[substrate]), not second-order. The rate-determining step for both is the same: unimolecular departure of the leaving group to form a carbocation. This shared carbocation intermediate is why E1 and SN1 often compete when tertiary substrates are used.
Key Educational Points:
  - E1 and SN1 share a carbocation intermediate and often compete.
  - SN1 captures the carbocation with a nucleophile; E1 removes a β-proton to form an alkene.
  - In their simple rate laws, the unimolecular ionization step gives rate = k[substrate].

STEM: Which of the following is a copolymer?

  A. Polyethylene
  B. Polystyrene
  C. Styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR)   <= key
  D. Polyvinyl chloride

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. Styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR)
A copolymer is formed from two or more different monomers. SBR is made by copolymerization of styrene and 1,3-butadiene. Polyethylene, polystyrene, and PVC are all homopolymers - each is derived from a single type of monomer. Copolymers allow tailoring of properties by combining characteristics of different monomers.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Polyethylene: Polyethylene is a homopolymer made from only one type of monomer (ethylene, CH₂=CH₂). A copolymer requires two or more different monomers. SBR (styrene-butadiene rubber) is a copolymer because it combines styrene and butadiene units in the same chain.
B. Polystyrene: Polystyrene is also a homopolymer, made from only styrene monomers. While styrene is one of the monomers in SBR, polystyrene by itself contains no butadiene. A copolymer must incorporate two or more chemically distinct monomer types within its polymer chain.
D. Polyvinyl chloride: Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is a homopolymer made from vinyl chloride (CH₂=CHCl) monomers only. It is one of the most widely produced plastics. To be a copolymer, the polymer chain must contain repeating units derived from at least two different monomers, as in SBR (styrene + butadiene) or ABS (acrylonitrile + butadiene + styrene).
Key Educational Points:
  - A copolymer contains units derived from more than one monomer type.
  - Styrene-butadiene rubber combines styrene and 1,3-butadiene units.
  - Compare this with the simple homopolymers polystyrene from styrene and PVC from vinyl chloride.

STEM: The conversion of a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid involves:

  A. Reduction
  B. Oxidation   <= key
  C. Hydrolysis
  D. Dehydration

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Oxidation
Oxidation of a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid proceeds through the aldehyde oxidation level: RCH₂OH → RCHO → RCOOH. With R representing an alkyl group, the oxidation state of the reacting carbon rises from −1 to +1 to +3. Suitable strong oxidants under aqueous conditions can carry out both stages in one reaction vessel.

Key Educational Points:
  - Primary alcohol oxidation can proceed through an aldehyde to a carboxylic acid.
  - More C–O bonds and fewer C–H bonds generally mean oxidation of the carbon involved.

STEM: Conversion of a carboxylic acid to an acid chloride is best accomplished using:

  A. HCl
  B. NaCl
  C. SOCl₂ (thionyl chloride)   <= key

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: C. SOCl₂ (thionyl chloride)
Thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) reacts with carboxylic acids to give acid chlorides, SO₂, and HCl - both gaseous by-products that leave the reaction mixture, driving the reaction to completion: RCOOH + SOCl₂ → RCOCl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑. PCl₃ and PCl₅ can also be used but produce less convenient by-products.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. HCl: HCl is a simple acid that cannot convert a carboxylic acid to an acid chloride - the reaction is thermodynamically unfavorable. SOCl₂ (thionyl chloride) drives the reaction forward because its byproducts (SO₂ and HCl) are gases that escape the reaction, shifting equilibrium toward products.
B. NaCl: NaCl is a stable ionic salt that cannot donate chloride in a way that displaces –OH from a carboxylic acid. Acid chloride synthesis requires an activating reagent like SOCl₂, PCl₃, or PCl₅ that makes the hydroxyl a better leaving group while providing reactive chloride.
D. Cl₂/UV light: Cl₂/UV light causes free radical halogenation of C–H bonds (as in the chlorination of alkanes), not conversion of –COOH to –COCl. Acid chloride formation requires nucleophilic acyl substitution with reagents like thionyl chloride (SOCl₂), which also conveniently produces gaseous byproducts.
Key Educational Points:
  - SOCl₂ converts a carboxylic acid into an acid chloride.
  - The reaction replaces the acid's –OH with –Cl and produces SO₂ and HCl.
  - Activation of the carboxyl group is needed; ordinary chloride salt alone is not the equivalent of thionyl chloride.

STEM: Which functional group transformation occurs when an alkene reacts with H₂ over a Pd/C catalyst?

  A. Oxidation to an epoxide
  B. Reduction to an alkane   <= key
  C. Isomerization to a cycloalkane
  D. Halogenation

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Reduction to an alkane
Catalytic hydrogenation (H₂/Pd, Pt, or Ni) adds H₂ across the double bond in a syn fashion (both H atoms add to the same face), converting the alkene to an alkane. This is a heterogeneous catalytic reaction where both H₂ and the alkene adsorb onto the metal surface before H atoms are delivered.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Oxidation to an epoxide: Alkene epoxidation commonly uses a peracid such as mCPBA; some peroxide methods require an appropriate catalyst or other suitable conditions. Catalytic hydrogenation (H₂/Pd) adds two hydrogen atoms across the double bond, reducing the alkene to an alkane in a syn addition.
C. Isomerization to a cycloalkane: Isomerization to a cycloalkane would require forming a new C–C bond to close a ring, which catalytic hydrogenation does not do. H₂/Pd simply adds hydrogen atoms across the π bond of the alkene. The carbon skeleton remains unchanged - only the degree of saturation changes.
D. Halogenation: Halogenation (adding Br₂ or Cl₂) is a completely different reaction from hydrogenation. H₂/Pd is a classic heterogeneous catalytic hydrogenation: the alkene and H₂ adsorb onto the palladium surface, and both hydrogen atoms add to the same face of the double bond (syn addition), producing an alkane.
Key Educational Points:
  - H₂ with Pd/C reduces a simple C=C bond to C–C by adding hydrogen.
  - Catalytic hydrogenation gives syn delivery of hydrogen at the metal surface.
  - Hydrogenation and epoxidation use different reagents and make different products.

STEM: A Wittig reaction converts a ketone or aldehyde into:

  A. An alcohol
  B. An alkene   <= key
  C. An amine
  D. An ester

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. An alkene
The Wittig reaction joins the carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde or ketone to the carbon of a phosphorus ylide, forming a C=C bond. With a triphenylphosphorus ylide such as Ph₃P=CHR, the other product is triphenylphosphine oxide, Ph₃P=O. This controls where the new double bond forms, although its E/Z outcome depends on the reactants and conditions and may be a mixture.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. An alcohol: Reagents such as NaBH₄ reduce aldehydes and ketones to alcohols. A Wittig ylide instead supplies a carbon that becomes double-bonded to the original carbonyl carbon, producing an alkene.
C. An amine: Amines are produced by reductive amination of ketones/aldehydes (reaction with an amine followed by reduction). The Wittig reaction specifically converts a C=O to a C=C bond. It is one of the most important methods for making alkenes with precise control over double bond placement.
D. An ester: The Wittig reaction makes an alkene, not an ester. An ester retains a carbonyl and has an –OR group attached to that carbonyl carbon; Wittig replaces the carbonyl oxygen with the ylide-derived carbon in a new C=C bond.
Key Educational Points:
  - A phosphorus ylide converts an aldehyde or ketone C=O into a new C=C bond.
  - One alkene carbon comes from the carbonyl compound and the other from the ylide.
  - Control of double-bond position does not guarantee a single E/Z stereoisomer.

Q60  [chem-361]  correct answer: A

STEM: The classic Beckmann rearrangement of a ketoxime produces which functional group?

  A. An amide   <= key
  B. A nitrile
  C. An amine
  D. An imine

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: A. An amide
In the Beckmann rearrangement, a ketoxime is converted into an amide after activation of its –OH group. The group anti to the activated leaving group migrates from carbon to nitrogen as the N–O bond breaks; reaction with water then gives the amide. A cyclic ketoxime can undergo ring expansion to a lactam, as in cyclohexanone oxime forming caprolactam for nylon-6 production.
Why the other choices are wrong:
B. A nitrile: Nitriles (R–C≡N) can be formed by dehydration of primary amides or aldoximes, but this is not the Beckmann rearrangement. The Beckmann rearrangement specifically converts a ketoxime to a substituted amide through migration of the group anti to the hydroxyl, with ring expansion if the oxime is cyclic.
C. An amine: Amines can be obtained by reduction of suitable nitrogen-containing compounds, but that is not this rearrangement. The Beckmann product has a carbonyl bonded to nitrogen, which identifies it as an amide.
D. An imine: An oxime contains C=N–OH. Its Beckmann product instead contains the amide linkage C(=O)–N; the transformation does not simply produce an ordinary imine.
Key Educational Points:
  - The classic ketoxime Beckmann rearrangement gives an amide.
  - The group anti to the activated leaving group migrates to nitrogen during rearrangement.
  - Ring expansion of cyclohexanone oxime gives caprolactam, used to produce nylon-6.
