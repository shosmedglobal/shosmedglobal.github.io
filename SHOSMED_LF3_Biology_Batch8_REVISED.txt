Toxoplasma gondii has which animal as its definitive host?

  A) Dog
  B) Cat
  C) Rat
  D) Pig

### Correct Answer: B. Cat

The domestic cat (and other felids) is the only definitive host for Toxoplasma gondii, meaning sexual reproduction of the parasite occurs exclusively in the feline intestinal epithelium. Cats shed oocysts in their feces, which sporulate in the environment and become infectious. Humans and other warm-blooded animals serve as intermediate hosts, harboring tissue cysts (bradyzoites). Infection is particularly dangerous during pregnancy (congenital toxoplasmosis) and in immunocompromised patients.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Dog:** Dogs are not the definitive host for Toxoplasma gondii. Dogs can serve as intermediate hosts and may become infected, but they do not support the sexual reproduction of the parasite. Dogs are, however, the definitive host for other parasites like Echinococcus granulosus and Toxocara canis.

**C. Rat:** Rats are intermediate hosts. They can contain tissue cysts, and a cat can become infected by eating an infected rat. Sexual reproduction of the parasite occurs in felids, not rats.

**D. Pig:** Pigs are intermediate hosts for T. gondii and are actually an important source of human infection through consumption of undercooked pork. Tissue cysts (bradyzoites) in pork can transmit the infection to humans. Prevention includes cooking meat thoroughly and avoiding accidental ingestion of material contaminated by cat faeces.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Cats and other felids are the only definitive hosts of Toxoplasma gondii, because sexual reproduction occurs solely in feline intestinal epithelium.
  - Cats shed oocysts in faeces; these sporulate in the environment and become infectious.
  - Humans are intermediate hosts, harbouring tissue cysts containing bradyzoites.
  - Humans can acquire infection from tissue cysts in undercooked meat or from oocysts in food, water or soil contaminated by cat faeces.
  - The dangerous settings are pregnancy (congenital toxoplasmosis) and immunosuppression, where reactivation causes cerebral toxoplasmosis.

Q2  [bio-407]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which parasitic infection is transmitted by the tsetse fly (Glossina)?

  A) Malaria
  B) African sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis)
  C) Leishmaniasis
  D) Chagas disease

### Correct Answer: B. African sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis)

African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) is caused by Trypanosoma brucei and transmitted by the bite of the tsetse fly (Glossina species), found in sub-Saharan Africa. T. b. gambiense occurs in West and Central Africa and causes a chronic illness, while T. b. rhodesiense occurs in eastern and southern Africa and causes a more acute illness. The parasite evades the immune system through antigenic variation of its variant surface glycoprotein (VSG). Malaria is transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes, leishmaniasis by sandflies, and Chagas disease by triatomine bugs.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Malaria:** Malaria is caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, not by the tsetse fly.

**C. Leishmaniasis:** Leishmaniasis is transmitted by sandflies - Phlebotomus in the Old World and Lutzomyia in the New World.

**D. Chagas disease:** Chagas disease is caused by Trypanosoma cruzi. In its classic insect-borne route, parasites in the faeces of a triatomine bug enter through broken skin or mucous membranes. African sleeping sickness is transmitted through the bite of a tsetse fly.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The tsetse fly (Glossina) transmits African trypanosomiasis, caused by Trypanosoma brucei.
  - T. b. gambiense occurs in West and Central Africa and causes the chronic form; T. b. rhodesiense occurs in eastern and southern Africa and causes the acute form.
  - The parasite evades immunity by antigenic variation of its variant surface glycoprotein, producing recurring waves of parasitaemia.
  - The parasite can change its surface antigens, helping it evade antibodies and making vaccine development difficult.
  - Keep the vectors distinct: Anopheles mosquitoes for malaria, sandflies for leishmaniasis, triatomine bugs for Chagas disease, tsetse for sleeping sickness.

Q3  [bio-408]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Taenia solium (pork tapeworm) can cause cysticercosis when humans ingest:

  A) Larval cysts in undercooked pork
  B) Eggs from fecal contamination
  C) Cercariae in freshwater
  D) Metacercariae on aquatic plants

### Correct Answer: B. Eggs from fecal contamination

Cysticercosis (including neurocysticercosis) occurs when humans accidentally ingest T. solium eggs through fecal-oral contamination. The eggs are shed in the feces of a person carrying an adult T. solium tapeworm in the intestine. The eggs hatch in the intestine, and the oncospheres penetrate the intestinal wall and migrate through the bloodstream to tissues (brain, muscles, eyes), where they develop into cysticerci (larval cysts). This is different from intestinal taeniasis, which occurs when humans eat undercooked pork containing cysticerci. Cercariae and metacercariae are stages of trematodes (flukes).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Larval cysts in undercooked pork:** Eating pork containing T. solium larval cysts causes intestinal taeniasis, in which an adult tapeworm develops in the intestine. Cysticercosis instead follows ingestion of eggs, which can develop into larvae in human tissues.

**C. Cercariae in freshwater:** Cercariae in freshwater are the infective stage of Schistosoma species (blood flukes), not Taenia. Schistosome cercariae are released from freshwater snails and actively penetrate human skin during contact with contaminated water. This is an entirely different parasite with a different life cycle.

**D. Metacercariae on aquatic plants:** Metacercariae on aquatic plants are the infective stage of Fasciola hepatica (liver fluke). Humans become infected by eating raw watercress or other aquatic vegetation contaminated with encysted metacercariae. This is not relevant to Taenia solium, which is a cestode (tapeworm), not a trematode (fluke).

### Key Educational Points:

  - Cysticercosis follows ingestion of Taenia solium eggs by the faecal-oral route - the human becomes an accidental intermediate host.
  - Oncospheres hatch, cross the intestinal wall, and lodge in brain, muscle, and eye as cysticerci.
  - Eating undercooked pork containing cysticerci instead causes intestinal taeniasis - an adult worm, not tissue cysts.
  - The distinction is central: eggs cause tissue disease, larval cysts in pork cause intestinal infection.
  - Neurocysticercosis is a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic regions.

Q4  [bio-409]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Schistosoma mansoni eggs characteristically have:

  A) A terminal spine
  B) A lateral spine
  C) No spine (round and smooth)
  D) An operculum (lid)

### Correct Answer: B. A lateral spine

Schistosoma mansoni eggs have a prominent lateral spine. S. haematobium eggs have a terminal spine, while the rounder eggs of S. japonicum have a small, often inconspicuous lateral knob. Egg morphology helps distinguish these parasites microscopically. Many eggs pass out of the body, but eggs retained in tissues provoke inflammation and fibrosis.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. A terminal spine:** A terminal spine is characteristic of S. haematobium eggs, which are usually detected in urine. S. mansoni eggs have a lateral spine and are usually detected in stool.

**C. No spine (round and smooth):** S. mansoni eggs have a conspicuous lateral spine. S. japonicum eggs are rounder, but they still have a small lateral knob that may be difficult to see; they should not be taught as completely spine-free.

**D. An operculum (lid):** An operculum is a lid-like opening found in the eggs of several other flukes and some tapeworms. Schistosoma eggs do not have an operculum.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Schistosoma mansoni eggs bear a prominent lateral spine.
  - S. haematobium eggs have a terminal spine; S. japonicum eggs are rounder, with a small lateral knob that is often inconspicuous.
  - Egg morphology on microscopy is the practical means of species identification.
  - Eggs passed into the gut or urinary tract can leave the body; eggs retained in tissues cause inflammation.
  - The major pathology results from inflammation around retained eggs, giving periportal fibrosis in mansoni and bladder disease in haematobium.

Q5  [bio-410]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Giardia lamblia primarily infects which part of the human body?

  A) Large intestine (colon)
  B) Duodenum and jejunum (small intestine)
  C) Liver
  D) Lungs

### Correct Answer: B. Duodenum and jejunum (small intestine)

Giardia lamblia (G. intestinalis) is a flagellated protozoan that colonizes the duodenum and proximal jejunum of the small intestine. The trophozoites attach to the intestinal epithelium using a ventral adhesive disc, interfering with nutrient absorption and causing malabsorptive diarrhea, bloating, and flatulence. Transmission occurs through ingestion of cysts from contaminated water or food (fecal-oral route). Giardiasis is a common intestinal protozoan infection.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Large intestine (colon):** The large intestine (colon) is the primary site of infection for Entamoeba histolytica (causing amoebic dysentery) and Balantidium coli, not Giardia. Giardia lamblia specifically colonizes the upper small intestine (duodenum and jejunum), where it attaches to enterocytes using its ventral adhesive disc and interferes with nutrient absorption.

**C. Liver:** The liver can be affected by Entamoeba histolytica (amoebic liver abscess) and liver flukes (Fasciola, Clonorchis), but Giardia does not invade the liver. Giardia usually remains on the surface of the small-intestinal mucosa rather than invading it. It causes malabsorption, bloating, foul-smelling diarrhea, and steatorrhea (fatty stools).

**D. Lungs:** The lungs can be infected by parasites like Paragonimus (lung fluke) or during larval migration of helminths like Ascaris and hookworm (Loeffler syndrome), but Giardia does not infect the lungs. Giardia has a simple fecal-oral life cycle with no tissue invasion or extraintestinal migration.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Giardia lamblia colonises the duodenum and proximal jejunum.
  - Trophozoites attach with a ventral adhesive disc rather than invading the mucosa.
  - Interference with the absorptive surface produces malabsorptive, greasy diarrhoea with bloating and flatulence.
  - Giardia usually remains on the surface of the small-intestinal mucosa rather than invading it. It typically causes nonbloody diarrhoea, bloating and malabsorption; fever can occur.
  - Transmission is faecal-oral via cysts in contaminated water. Giardia cysts are relatively chlorine-tolerant, so disinfection requires suitable conditions and contact time. Boiling or appropriate filtration can also remove this waterborne risk.

Q6  [bio-411]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Which helminth enters the human body by larval penetration through the skin, often through bare feet?

  A) Ascaris lumbricoides
  B) Enterobius vermicularis
  C) Necator americanus (hookworm)
  D) Trichuris trichiura

### Correct Answer: C. Necator americanus (hookworm)

Hookworm larvae (Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale) in contaminated soil penetrate the skin (usually the feet) and migrate through the bloodstream to the lungs, ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and mature in the small intestine. Adult hookworms attach to the intestinal mucosa and feed on blood, causing iron deficiency anemia. Ascaris and Trichuris are transmitted by ingestion of eggs. Enterobius (pinworm) is transmitted by ingestion of eggs from the perianal region.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Ascaris lumbricoides:** Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm) is transmitted by ingestion of embryonated eggs from fecal-contaminated soil, food, or water, not by skin penetration. After ingestion, larvae hatch in the intestine, migrate through the liver and lungs (causing Loeffler syndrome with eosinophilic pneumonitis), ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and mature in the small intestine.

**B. Enterobius vermicularis:** Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm) is transmitted by ingestion or inhalation of eggs, often via the fecal-oral route or contaminated fomites. Eggs are deposited around the perianal area by the female worm at night, causing intense itching. It is the most common helminth infection in developed countries, particularly among children.

**D. Trichuris trichiura:** Trichuris trichiura (whipworm) is transmitted by ingestion of embryonated eggs from contaminated soil, not skin penetration. Adult worms embed their thin anterior end into the colonic mucosa. Heavy infections can cause dysentery, rectal prolapse, and iron-deficiency anemia, particularly in children in tropical regions.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Hookworm larvae (Necator americanus, Ancylostoma duodenale) penetrate intact skin, classically the bare foot.
  - They then migrate via blood to the lungs, ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and mature in the small intestine.
  - Adults attach to intestinal mucosa and feed on blood, so the hallmark is iron deficiency anaemia.
  - Strongyloides also enters through skin; Ascaris and Trichuris are acquired by ingesting eggs, and Enterobius by perianal egg transfer.
  - Sorting helminths by route of entry - skin penetration versus ingestion - is more reliable than memorising species lists.

Q7  [bio-412]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Leishmaniasis is transmitted to humans through the bite of:

  A) Anopheles mosquito
  B) Tsetse fly
  C) Sandfly (Phlebotomus/Lutzomyia)
  D) Blackfly (Simulium)

### Correct Answer: C. Sandfly (Phlebotomus/Lutzomyia)

Leishmaniasis is caused by Leishmania species and transmitted through the bite of infected female sandflies (Phlebotomus in the Old World, Lutzomyia in the New World). The sandfly injects promastigotes (flagellated form) into the skin, which are phagocytosed by macrophages and transform into amastigotes (non-flagellated form) that multiply intracellularly. The disease presents in three main forms: cutaneous, mucocutaneous, and visceral (kala-azar).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Anopheles mosquito:** Anopheles mosquitoes transmit Plasmodium species, which cause malaria. Only female Anopheles mosquitoes bite and transmit the parasite. Anopheles mosquitoes are distinguished from other mosquito genera by their resting posture (body held at an angle to the surface) and spotted wing patterns.

**B. Tsetse fly:** Tsetse flies (Glossina species) transmit Trypanosoma brucei, which causes African sleeping sickness (African trypanosomiasis). Tsetse flies are found only in sub-Saharan Africa. Both male and female tsetse flies bite and can transmit the parasite, unlike mosquitoes where only females feed on blood.

**D. Blackfly (Simulium):** Blackflies (Simulium species) transmit Onchocerca volvulus, which causes river blindness (onchocerciasis). Blackflies breed near fast-flowing rivers, which is why the disease is most prevalent in communities near waterways. The microfilariae migrate to the eyes and skin, causing blindness and severe dermatitis.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Leishmania is transmitted by female sandflies: Phlebotomus in the Old World, Lutzomyia in the New World.
  - The fly injects flagellated promastigotes, which macrophages phagocytose and which then convert to intracellular amastigotes.
  - Surviving inside the macrophage is the central feature of its pathogenesis and the reason immunity is cell-mediated.
  - Three clinical forms exist: cutaneous, mucocutaneous, and visceral (kala-azar).
  - Visceral disease affects spleen, liver, and bone marrow and is fatal untreated, whereas cutaneous disease often self-heals.

Q8  [bio-413]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which stage of Entamoeba histolytica is usually responsible for infection after ingestion of contaminated food or water?

  A) Trophozoite
  B) Cyst
  C) Sporozoite
  D) Merozoite

### Correct Answer: B. Cyst

Entamoeba histolytica is transmitted through ingestion of mature quadrinucleate cysts in fecally contaminated food or water. The cysts are resistant to the acidic environment of the stomach and excyst in the small intestine, releasing trophozoites that colonize the large intestine. Trophozoites are the active, multiplying form; they may remain in the intestinal lumen or invade the intestinal wall, causing amoebic dysentery, and can spread to the liver (amoebic liver abscess). Sporozoites and merozoites are stages of apicomplexan parasites like Plasmodium.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Trophozoite:** Trophozoites are the active, multiplying form. They may remain in the intestinal lumen or invade tissues, but they are fragile outside the host and ordinarily do not survive passage through the stomach after ingestion. The resistant cyst is the usual form transmitted in contaminated food or water.

**C. Sporozoite:** A Plasmodium sporozoite is the stage delivered by a mosquito, and it infects liver cells. It is not a stage in the Entamoeba histolytica life cycle.

**D. Merozoite:** A Plasmodium merozoite is the stage that invades red blood cells. It is not a stage in the Entamoeba histolytica life cycle.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The cyst is the infective stage of Entamoeba histolytica, ingested in faecally contaminated food or water.
  - Mature cysts are quadrinucleate and resist gastric acid, allowing them to reach the intestine intact.
  - Excystation releases trophozoites, the active form; these may stay in the intestinal lumen or invade the wall, and they cannot survive outside the host.
  - Trophozoites invade the colonic wall causing amoebic dysentery with flask-shaped ulcers, and can seed the liver as an abscess.
  - In E. histolytica and Giardia, cysts are important for transmission and trophozoites are the active form. Not all protozoan parasites have a cyst stage.

Q9  [bio-414]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Echinococcus granulosus causes which condition in humans?

  A) Neurocysticercosis
  B) Hydatid cyst disease
  C) Elephantiasis
  D) River blindness

### Correct Answer: B. Hydatid cyst disease

Echinococcus granulosus is a small tapeworm whose definitive host is the dog. Humans are accidental intermediate hosts who become infected by ingesting eggs from dog feces. The oncospheres penetrate the intestinal wall and travel via the bloodstream primarily to the liver (70%) and lungs (20%), where they develop into large, slow-growing hydatid cysts. Rupture of a hydatid cyst can cause anaphylaxis. Neurocysticercosis is caused by Taenia solium, elephantiasis by lymphatic filarial worms such as Wuchereria bancrofti, and river blindness by Onchocerca volvulus.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Neurocysticercosis:** Neurocysticercosis is caused by the larval stage of Taenia solium, the pork tapeworm, after a person ingests its eggs. Both conditions produce tissue cysts, which makes this the closest distractor, but the parasite and its definitive host are different.

**C. Elephantiasis:** Elephantiasis can result from chronic lymphatic filariasis caused by worms such as Wuchereria bancrofti or Brugia species. It is not the cyst-forming disease caused by Echinococcus.

**D. River blindness:** River blindness is onchocerciasis, caused by Onchocerca volvulus and transmitted by blackflies breeding in fast-flowing rivers. Microfilariae migrating through the eye cause the blindness.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Echinococcus granulosus causes hydatid cyst disease, with the dog as definitive host.
  - Humans are accidental intermediate hosts, infected by ingesting eggs shed in dog faeces.
  - Oncospheres travel via the bloodstream and form slow-growing cysts, mainly in liver (about 70%) and lung (about 20%).
  - Rupture of a hydatid cyst can release parasite material, causing dissemination and potentially anaphylaxis.
  - Keep the tapeworm diseases distinct: Echinococcus gives hydatid cysts, Taenia solium gives neurocysticercosis.

Q10  [bio-415]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Approximately how many ATP molecules can a human cell with functioning mitochondria obtain from the complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose molecule?

  A) 2 ATP
  B) 4 ATP
  C) 30-32 ATP
  D) 38 ATP

### Correct Answer: C. 30-32 ATP

A commonly used estimate is about 30-32 ATP per glucose. Glycolysis contributes a net 2 ATP directly, and the citric acid cycle contributes 2 ATP equivalents as ATP or GTP. Oxidative phosphorylation supplies approximately 26-28 ATP. The range mainly reflects how the electrons from cytosolic NADH are transferred to the mitochondrial respiratory chain. NADH itself does not cross the inner mitochondrial membrane.

  | Source | Approximate ATP |
  | Eight NADH formed within mitochondria | about 20 |
  | Two succinate-derived FAD-linked electron pairs | about 3 |
  | Two cytosolic NADH | about 3 or 5, depending on the shuttle |
  | Substrate-level phosphorylation | 4 ATP equivalents |
  | Total | about 30 or 32 |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 2 ATP:** 2 ATP is the net yield from glycolysis alone (substrate-level phosphorylation). Complete aerobic oxidation of glucose includes glycolysis, pyruvate dehydrogenase, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, which together yield 30-32 ATP. The 2 ATP from glycolysis is just the starting point.

**B. 4 ATP:** 4 ATP is the gross (not net) yield from glycolysis, which consumes 2 ATP in its early steps and so nets 2. It is also the total substrate-level yield from a completely oxidised glucose: 2 from glycolysis plus 2 ATP or GTP equivalents from the citric acid cycle. Neither figure is the total aerobic yield, because most ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation.

**D. 38 ATP:** 38 ATP was the older textbook estimate that assumed maximum theoretical yield from NADH and FADH2. The current accepted value is 30-32 ATP because: (1) the P/O ratio for NADH is approximately 2.5 (not 3), and for FADH2 approximately 1.5 (not 2); and (2) some proton gradient energy is used for transporting metabolites across the mitochondrial membrane.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose yields approximately 30-32 ATP.
  - The contributions are 2 ATP from glycolysis, 2 GTP/ATP from the citric acid cycle, and the remainder from oxidative phosphorylation.
  - Current estimates use about 2.5 ATP per mitochondrial NADH and about 1.5 for an FAD-linked electron pair, rather than the older values of 3 and 2.
  - The malate-aspartate shuttle transfers reducing equivalents to mitochondrial NADH. The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle transfers them through an enzyme-bound FAD to coenzyme Q, bypassing Complex I and yielding less ATP.
  - Treat these totals as approximations - the proton-to-ATP stoichiometry is not a whole number.

Q11  [bio-416]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
In the electron transport chain, Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) accepts electrons from:

  A) FADH₂
  B) NADH
  C) Cytochrome c
  D) Ubiquinone (CoQ)

### Correct Answer: B. NADH

Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) is the largest complex of the electron transport chain. It accepts electrons from NADH, oxidizing it to NAD⁺, and transfers them to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q). During this process, Complex I pumps 4 H⁺ per pair of electrons from one NADH, from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space, contributing to the proton motive force. Complex II contains enzyme-bound FAD. During succinate oxidation, this FAD is reduced and its electrons are transferred through the complex to coenzyme Q. NADH instead donates electrons to Complex I.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. FADH₂:** Free FADH₂ is not what Complex I accepts, and it is not how FAD-linked electrons reach the chain. Complex II contains enzyme-bound FAD: during succinate oxidation that FAD is reduced, and its electrons are transferred through the complex to coenzyme Q. NADH is the donor to Complex I.

**C. Cytochrome c:** Cytochrome c is a mobile electron carrier in the intermembrane space that transfers electrons from Complex III to Complex IV, not to Complex I. It is a small heme-containing protein that shuttles one electron at a time. Cytochrome c also plays a famous role in apoptosis: when released from mitochondria, it activates caspases to trigger programmed cell death.

**D. Ubiquinone (CoQ):** Ubiquinone (Coenzyme Q, CoQ) is a mobile lipid-soluble electron carrier within the inner mitochondrial membrane. During forward electron transport, CoQ accepts electrons from Complex I and other entry routes and carries them toward Complex III. It receives electrons from Complex I rather than donating them to it. The supplement CoQ10 is the same molecule.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) accepts electrons from NADH and passes them to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q).
  - It pumps 4 H⁺ into the intermembrane space, contributing to the proton motive force.
  - Succinate-derived electrons reach coenzyme Q through Complex II and bypass Complex I, so their entry supports less proton pumping than entry from NADH.
  - Of the electron-transfer complexes I–IV, Complex II does not pump protons. It also functions as succinate dehydrogenase in the citric acid cycle.
  - Ubiquinone and cytochrome c are the mobile carriers that link the fixed complexes.

Q12  [bio-417]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
The chemiosmotic hypothesis proposes that ATP synthesis is driven by:

  A) Direct transfer of phosphate groups from substrate molecules
  B) A proton electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane
  C) Conformational changes in electron carriers
  D) Direct coupling of electron transfer to ATP synthase rotation

### Correct Answer: B. A proton electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane

Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic hypothesis (Nobel Prize, 1978) explains that the energy released during electron transport is used to pump protons (H⁺) from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (proton motive force, Δp). This gradient has two components: a chemical gradient (ΔpH) and an electrical gradient (Δψ). Protons flow back through ATP synthase (Complex V) down their gradient, and this energy drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Direct transfer of phosphate groups from substrate molecules:** Direct transfer of phosphate groups from substrate molecules describes substrate-level phosphorylation, which occurs in glycolysis and the citric acid cycle. This mechanism produces only a small fraction of total ATP. The chemiosmotic hypothesis, proposed by Peter Mitchell (Nobel Prize, 1978), specifically explains oxidative phosphorylation driven by a proton electrochemical gradient.

**C. Conformational changes in electron carriers:** While conformational changes do occur in electron carriers during electron transport, these conformational changes serve to pump protons across the membrane, not directly to synthesize ATP. It is the resulting proton gradient (proton-motive force) that then drives ATP synthase. The conformational changes are part of the mechanism for creating the gradient, not for making ATP directly.

**D. Direct coupling of electron transfer to ATP synthase rotation:** There is no direct coupling between electron transfer and ATP synthase rotation. The coupling is indirect, mediated by the proton gradient. The electron transport chain creates the gradient by pumping protons, and ATP synthase uses this gradient to spin its rotor and catalyze ATP formation. This indirect coupling via the proton-motive force is the core insight of the chemiosmotic hypothesis.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Mitchell's chemiosmotic hypothesis states that electron transport stores energy as a proton gradient, which then drives ATP synthesis.
  - The proton motive force has two components: a chemical gradient (ΔpH) and an electrical membrane potential (Δψ).
  - ATP synthesis is therefore indirect - no high-energy phosphorylated intermediate links the chain to ATP.
  - The inner mitochondrial membrane has low passive permeability to protons. Controlled proton flow through ATP synthase drives ATP production; excessive proton leakage reduces this coupling.
  - The same principle operates in chloroplast thylakoids and across bacterial plasma membranes.

Q13  [bio-418]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Which inhibitor blocks electron flow at Complex III of the electron transport chain?

  A) Rotenone
  B) Antimycin A
  C) Cyanide
  D) Oligomycin

### Correct Answer: B. Antimycin A

Antimycin A inhibits Complex III (cytochrome bc₁ complex) by blocking electron transfer from cytochrome b to ubiquinone at the Qᵢ site. This halts the Q cycle and stops electron flow through the chain.

  | Inhibitor | Target |
  | Rotenone | Complex I |
  | Antimycin A | Complex III |
  | Cyanide / CO | Complex IV |
  | Oligomycin | ATP synthase (Fₒ subunit) |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Rotenone:** Rotenone inhibits Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), blocking the transfer of electrons from NADH to ubiquinone. It is a natural plant-derived insecticide and piscicide. Rotenone exposure has been linked to Parkinson-like symptoms in animal models because it damages mitochondrial function in dopaminergic neurons.

**C. Cyanide:** Cyanide (CN-) and carbon monoxide (CO) inhibit Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), the final complex in the electron transport chain. They bind to the iron in the heme group of cytochrome a3, preventing the transfer of electrons to oxygen. This is why cyanide poisoning is rapidly lethal: it shuts down the entire electron transport chain.

**D. Oligomycin:** Oligomycin inhibits ATP synthase (Complex V), not Complex III. It blocks the proton channel (Fo subunit) of ATP synthase, preventing protons from flowing through and thus stopping ATP production. When oligomycin is present, the proton gradient builds up so much that the ETC also slows down because it cannot pump more protons against the steep gradient.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Antimycin A blocks Complex III (cytochrome bc₁) at the Qᵢ site, halting electron transfer to cytochrome c.
  - The effect of inhibition depends on the target. Blocking Complex I stops NADH entry through that complex, but electrons can still enter through routes such as Complex II.
  - Know the classic targets: rotenone at Complex I, antimycin A at Complex III, cyanide and carbon monoxide at Complex IV, oligomycin at ATP synthase.
  - In a blocked electron-transfer pathway, carriers before the block tend to become more reduced and carriers after it more oxidised.
  - Electron-transfer inhibitors block parts of the chain. Oligomycin blocks ATP synthase and can secondarily slow electron transport as the gradient builds. Uncouplers dissipate the gradient, reducing ATP production while electron transport can continue.

Q14  [bio-419]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) in brown adipose tissue generates heat by:

  A) Increasing electron transport chain activity to produce more ATP
  B) Allowing protons to leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane without ATP synthesis
  C) Inhibiting Complex IV to generate heat from electron accumulation
  D) Converting ATP directly into thermal energy

### Correct Answer: B. Allowing protons to leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane without ATP synthesis

Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, thermogenin) in brown adipose tissue creates a proton channel in the inner mitochondrial membrane, allowing H⁺ to flow from the intermembrane space back to the matrix without passing through ATP synthase. The energy of the proton gradient is dissipated as heat instead of being used for ATP synthesis. UCP1 supports nonshivering heat production, which is especially important in newborns and during arousal from hibernation.

ATP synthase (Complex V) generates ATP by which mechanism?

  A) Proton flow through Fₒ drives rotation of the c-ring, causing conformational changes in F₁ that catalyze ATP synthesis
  B) Direct phosphorylation of ADP by inorganic phosphate without any proton gradient
  C) Electron transfer from NADH directly to ADP
  D) Hydrolysis of GTP provides energy for ATP formation

### Correct Answer: A. Proton flow through Fₒ drives rotation of the c-ring, causing conformational changes in F₁ that catalyze ATP synthesis

ATP synthase operates as a molecular rotary motor. The Fₒ subunit spans the inner mitochondrial membrane and contains a ring of c subunits. Protons flowing through Fₒ down their electrochemical gradient cause the c-ring and the attached γ subunit to rotate. This rotation induces sequential conformational changes in the three β subunits of the F₁ head (binding change mechanism proposed by Paul Boyer), cycling through open, loose, and tight states to bind ADP + Pᵢ, catalyze ATP synthesis, and release ATP.

**B. Direct phosphorylation of ADP by inorganic phosphate without any proton gradient:** Combining ADP and inorganic phosphate requires an energy source. **D. Hydrolysis of GTP provides energy for ATP formation:** GTP can transfer a phosphate to ADP through a separate enzyme reaction, but that is not ATP synthase's mechanism. During oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthase uses proton flow to drive rotation and ATP production.

### Key Educational Points:

  - ATP synthase is a rotary molecular motor: Fₒ spans the membrane and F₁ projects into the matrix.

Which sugar is found in the backbone of RNA?

  A) Deoxyribose
  B) Ribose
  C) Glucose
  D) Fructose

**RNA** (ribonucleic acid) contains the pentose sugar **ribose** in its backbone. Ribose has a hydroxyl group (–OH) on the 2' carbon. In contrast, **DNA** contains **2'-deoxyribose**, which lacks the 2' hydroxyl group (it has –H instead).

The 2'–OH increases RNA's chemical reactivity, including its susceptibility to alkaline hydrolysis, and contributes to its folding and catalytic possibilities. **C. Glucose:** Glucose is a six-carbon sugar used extensively in energy metabolism. It is not the five-carbon ribose of the RNA backbone.

**D. Fructose:** Fructose is another six-carbon sugar, found for example in fruit and in sucrose. It is not the five-carbon ribose of the RNA backbone.

### Key Educational Points:

  - RNA contains ribose; DNA contains 2'-deoxyribose, which lacks the 2' hydroxyl.
  - The 2'-OH is an important chemical difference between RNA and DNA, but their biological roles also depend on their sequences, structures and associated proteins.
  - The 2'-OH makes RNA vulnerable to alkaline hydrolysis, so RNA is far less chemically stable than DNA.
  - Greater reactivity and single-strandedness let RNA fold into complex shapes and act catalytically as ribozymes.
  - DNA's greater stability suits it to long-term information storage, while RNA suits transient, working copies.

>> Bottom Line: RNA contains ribose (with 2'–OH); DNA contains 2'-deoxyribose (without 2'–OH). This difference affects stability and structure.

In the Watson-Crick model of DNA, adenine pairs with thymine through:

  A) One hydrogen bond
  B) Two hydrogen bonds
  C) Three hydrogen bonds

For otherwise comparable DNA duplexes, higher G–C content generally increases melting temperature (Tₘ). Base stacking, sequence, length and solution conditions also affect stability. Covalent bonds within DNA are found in the phosphodiester bonds of the backbone (linking sugars to phosphates) and the N-glycosidic bonds (linking bases to sugars). The non-covalent nature of base pairing is essential because the two strands must be easily separated during replication and transcription.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A-T pairs are held by two hydrogen bonds; G-C pairs by three.
  - For otherwise comparable duplexes, higher G-C content generally raises the melting temperature; base stacking and solution conditions matter too.
  - Chargaff's rules follow from this pairing: in double-stranded DNA, A equals T and G equals C.

>> Bottom Line: A–T pairs form two hydrogen bonds; G–C pairs form three. Hydrogen bonding and base stacking both contribute to DNA structure and stability.

It always adds nucleotides to the free 3'-OH group, extending the chain exclusively in the 5' to 3' direction. Because the two template strands are antiparallel, one strand (leading) is synthesized continuously while the other (lagging) must be synthesized in short fragments (Okazaki fragments) that are later joined by DNA ligase.

**D. Random direction:** DNA synthesis is not random; it follows a strict 5' to 3' directionality dictated by the enzyme's catalytic mechanism. DNA polymerase catalyzes a nucleophilic attack by the 3'-OH of the growing chain on the alpha-phosphate of an incoming dNTP, releasing pyrophosphate. This chemistry only works in the 5' to 3' direction.

  - The incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate supplies the energy for incorporation. Adding it to the growing strand releases pyrophosphate, whose subsequent hydrolysis helps drive the reaction forward.
  - This strict directionality forces lagging-strand synthesis into Okazaki fragments, each with its own RNA primer.

>> Bottom Line: DNA polymerase always synthesizes 5' → 3' by adding nucleotides to the free 3'–OH end. It reads the template 3' → 5'.

In a double-stranded DNA molecule, if 22% of the bases are adenine, what percentage are cytosine?

  A) 22%
  B) 28%
  C) 44%
  D) 56%

### Correct Answer: 28%

According to **Chargaff's rules**, in double-stranded DNA: A = T and G = C. If adenine (A) = 22%, then thymine (T) = 22%. Together, A + T = 44%.

Since total bases = 100%, G + C = 100% − 44% = 56%. Because G = C, each is 56% ÷ 2 = **28%**.

Calculation: A = 22% → T = 22% → A + T = 44% → G + C = 56% → C = 28%

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 22%:** 22% is the frequency of adenine, and by Chargaff's rules also of thymine. Cytosine belongs to the other base pair, so it does not share adenine's value.

**C. 44%:** 44% is A + T combined, not cytosine alone. Subtracting it from 100% gives G + C together, which must then be halved.

**D. 56%:** 56% is G + C combined. Since Chargaff's rules make G equal to C, cytosine alone is half of this, or 28%.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Chargaff's rules for double-stranded DNA: A equals T, and G equals C.
  - If A is 22%, then T is 22%, so A + T totals 44%.
  - G + C must therefore be 100% minus 44% = 56%, and since G equals C, each is 28%.
  - One known base percentage is enough to derive all four in double-stranded DNA.
  - These equalities follow from complementary pairing in double-stranded DNA. They are not required for a single-stranded nucleic acid.

>> Bottom Line: Chargaff's rules (A=T, G=C) allow calculation of all base percentages from a single known value in double-stranded DNA.

During DNA replication, which enzyme unwinds the double helix?

  A) DNA polymerase
  B) DNA ligase
  C) Helicase
  D) Primase

### Correct Answer: Helicase

**Helicase** uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to break the hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs and unwind the DNA double helix at the replication fork. This creates two single-stranded templates for DNA polymerase.

  | Enzyme | Function in Replication |
  | Helicase | Unwinds double helix |
  | Primase | Synthesizes RNA primers |
  | DNA polymerase | Synthesizes new DNA strand |
  | DNA ligase | Joins Okazaki fragments |
  | Topoisomerase | Relieves supercoiling tension |

Single-strand binding proteins (SSBPs) stabilize the unwound single strands, preventing them from re-annealing before replication.

**D. Primase:** Primase lays down short RNA primers to give DNA polymerase the 3'-OH it needs to begin. It provides a starting point but has no unwinding activity.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Helicase unwinds the double helix at the replication fork, breaking hydrogen bonds using ATP.

  - Unwinding creates torsional strain ahead of the fork, which topoisomerase (DNA gyrase in bacteria) must relieve.

>> Bottom Line: Helicase unwinds DNA at the replication fork by breaking hydrogen bonds between base pairs, using ATP hydrolysis.

DNA replication is described as semiconservative because:

  A) Only half of the DNA is replicated
  B) Each daughter molecule contains one original strand and one new strand
  C) The leading strand is conserved but the lagging strand is not
  D) Both strands are completely newly synthesized

### Correct Answer: Each daughter molecule contains one original strand and one new strand

**Semiconservative replication**, demonstrated by the **Meselson-Stahl experiment** (1958), means that each daughter DNA molecule retains one parental (original) strand and one newly synthesized strand.

In conservative replication (disproven), both original strands would stay together. In dispersive replication (also disproven), original and new DNA would be interspersed along each strand. The semiconservative model was predicted by Watson and Crick based on the complementary base-pairing structure of DNA.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Only half of the DNA is replicated:** All of the DNA is replicated, not just half. The term "semiconservative" refers to the distribution of parental strands in the daughter molecules, not to the extent of replication. Every nucleotide in both strands serves as a template, resulting in two complete daughter DNA molecules from one parental molecule.

**C. The leading strand is conserved but the lagging strand is not:** Both the leading and lagging strands are synthesized and conserved equally in daughter molecules. The distinction between leading and lagging strands is about the direction and continuity of synthesis, not about conservation. Each daughter DNA molecule contains one complete old strand and one complete new strand, regardless of whether those segments were synthesized as leading or lagging.

**D. Both strands are completely newly synthesized:** A double helix made entirely of new strands would be one product predicted by the conservative model, which leaves the original two strands together in the other molecule. The dispersive model instead predicts mixtures of old and new DNA segments within each strand. Actual semiconservative replication gives each daughter molecule one original strand and one new strand, as the Meselson-Stahl experiment (1958) showed using 15N density labeling.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Semiconservative replication means each daughter duplex keeps one parental strand and one new strand.
  - Meselson and Stahl demonstrated this in 1958 using nitrogen isotope density labelling.
  - The competing models were conservative (parental duplex kept intact) and dispersive (old and new interspersed); both were excluded.
  - The result follows naturally from complementary base pairing, as Watson and Crick had predicted.
  - Retaining a parental strand as template is also what makes faithful, low-error copying possible.

>> Bottom Line: Semiconservative replication means each daughter DNA helix has one old and one new strand, proven by the Meselson-Stahl experiment.

Which component distinguishes a nucleotide from a nucleoside?

  A) A nitrogenous base
  B) A pentose sugar
  C) A phosphate group
  D) A hydrogen bond

### Correct Answer: A phosphate group

A **nucleotide** = nucleoside + phosphate group. A **nucleoside** contains only a nitrogenous base and a pentose sugar. The addition of one or more phosphate groups to the sugar, commonly at the 5' carbon, converts a nucleoside into a nucleotide.

For example, adenosine itself has no phosphate, while AMP has one, ADP two and ATP three. Nucleotides are the monomeric building blocks of nucleic acids. The phosphate groups also carry the negative charges that make DNA and RNA polyanionic molecules.

**A. A nitrogenous base:** The base is present in both a nucleoside and a nucleotide, so it does not distinguish them.

They are not a structural component of a nucleotide. The three components of a nucleotide are: (1) a nitrogenous base, (2) a pentose sugar, and (3) one or more phosphate groups. Remove the phosphate(s) and you have a nucleoside.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A phosphate group is the sole difference: nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate.
  - A nucleoside is base plus sugar only.
  - Phosphate commonly attaches at the 5' carbon; adenosine carries none, while AMP, ADP and ATP carry one, two and three.
  - Nucleotides are the monomers polymerised into nucleic acids.
  - Their phosphates carry the negative charge that makes DNA and RNA polyanionic and lets histones bind them electrostatically.

Bile salts are amphipathic molecules - they have both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. In the small intestine, bile salts coat large fat globules and break them into tiny droplets (emulsification), dramatically increasing the surface area available for lipase to digest triglycerides. Bile does not contain digestive enzymes itself.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Enzymatically breaking peptide bonds:** Peptide bonds in proteins are cleaved by proteases (pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin), not bile. Bile contains no digestive enzymes.

**C. Neutralizing stomach acid:** While bile is slightly alkaline, the primary neutralization of stomach acid in the duodenum is performed by bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) secreted by the pancreas.

**D. Absorbing vitamins directly:** Bile salts also help form mixed micelles that carry fat-digestion products and fat-soluble vitamins toward the intestinal surface. Intestinal cells absorb these substances. This supports absorption, while the emulsification described in the correct answer breaks large fat globules into smaller droplets.

>> Bottom Line: Bile emulsifies fats (physical breakdown, not chemical). It is produced by the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and released into the duodenum. Without bile, fat digestion and fat-soluble vitamin absorption are severely impaired.

==============================================================================
DNA replication is described as semiconservative because each new double helix contains:

  A) Two entirely new strands
  B) Two original (parental) strands
  C) One original strand and one newly synthesized strand
  D) Random mixtures of old and new nucleotides

### Correct Answer: One original strand and one newly synthesized strand

The Meselson-Stahl experiment (1958) elegantly demonstrated that DNA replication is semiconservative. When the double helix unwinds, each parental strand serves as a template for synthesizing a complementary new strand. The result is two daughter molecules, each consisting of one old (conserved) strand paired with one newly made strand.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Two entirely new strands:** A molecule containing two newly synthesized strands would be one product of the conservative model, in which the original two strands remain together in the other molecule. In semiconservative replication, the original strands separate and each serves as a template for a new partner.

**B. Two original strands:** This would describe conservative replication, where the parental molecule remains intact and a completely new copy is made - this was ruled out by Meselson and Stahl.

**D. Random mixtures:** The dispersive model predicted old and new segments mixed within each strand. It too was ruled out by the Meselson-Stahl experiment, which used density gradient centrifugation with ¹⁵N-labeled DNA.

>> Bottom Line: Semiconservative replication means each daughter DNA has one parental + one new strand. DNA polymerase always synthesizes in the 5' to 3' direction, reading the template 3' to 5'. The leading strand is made continuously; the lagging strand is made in Okazaki fragments.

Sound waves are converted into nerve impulses in which structure of the inner ear?

  A) Tympanic membrane
  B) Semicircular canals
  C) Cochlea (organ of Corti)
  D) Eustachian tube

### Correct Answer: Cochlea (organ of Corti)

The cochlea is a spiral-shaped, fluid-filled structure in the inner ear that houses the organ of Corti - the true receptor organ for hearing. Hair cells in the organ of Corti bend in response to vibrations transmitted through the cochlear fluid, triggering the release of neurotransmitters that generate nerve impulses sent to the brain via the cochlear nerve (part of cranial nerve VIII).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Tympanic membrane:** The eardrum vibrates in response to sound waves and transmits these vibrations to the ossicles (middle ear bones), but it does not convert sound to nerve impulses.

**B. Semicircular canals:** These detect rotational head movements (angular acceleration) for balance, not sound. They contain hair cells that respond to fluid movement during head rotation.

**D. Eustachian tube:** This connects the middle ear to the nasopharynx and equalizes air pressure on both sides of the tympanic membrane; it has no sensory function.

>> Bottom Line: Sound pathway: pinna → ear canal → tympanic membrane → ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) → oval window → cochlear fluid → organ of Corti (hair cells) → cochlear nerve → auditory cortex. The cochlea performs frequency analysis - different positions along its length respond to different pitches.

Scurvy, characterized by bleeding gums and poor wound healing, results from a deficiency of:

  A) Vitamin A
  B) Vitamin B12
  C) Vitamin C
  D) Vitamin E

**D. Vitamin E:** Deficiency causes hemolytic anemia and neuromuscular dysfunction due to oxidative damage to cell membranes, not collagen-related symptoms.

>> Bottom Line: Vitamin C is required for collagen hydroxylation. Deficiency → weak collagen → scurvy (bleeding, poor healing). Vitamin C is also a powerful antioxidant and enhances iron absorption in the intestine.

Visible connections between homologous chromosomes resulting from crossing over during meiosis are called:

  A) Centromeres
  B) Telomeres
  C) Chiasmata
  D) Kinetochores

### Correct Answer: Chiasmata

During prophase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) and exchange genetic material through a process called crossing over. The physical points where non-sister chromatids remain connected after the exchange are visible as X-shaped structures called chiasmata (singular: chiasma). This recombination increases genetic diversity in the resulting gametes.

During embryonic development, the neural tube - which gives rise to the brain and spinal cord - forms during which process?

  B) Gastrulation
  C) Neurulation

HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) primarily infects and destroys which type of immune cell?

  A) B lymphocytes
  B) Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)
  C) Helper T cells (CD4+)
  D) Natural killer cells

### Correct Answer: Helper T cells (CD4+)

HIV is a retrovirus that uses the CD4 receptor (along with co-receptors CCR5 or CXCR4) to enter and infect helper T lymphocytes. Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA. Integrase then inserts this viral DNA into host-cell DNA, and the cell's machinery is used to produce new virions. >> Bottom Line: HIV targets CD4+ helper T cells, which are the coordinators of the adaptive immune response. In an adult with HIV, AIDS is diagnosed when the CD4 count falls below 200 cells/μL (normal: 500-1500) or an AIDS-defining illness develops, even if the count is higher. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses viral replication and preserves CD4+ counts.

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) increases the permeability of which part of the nephron to water?

  A) Proximal convoluted tubule
  B) Descending loop of Henle
  C) Ascending loop of Henle
  D) Collecting duct

### Correct Answer: Collecting duct

ADH (vasopressin), released from the posterior pituitary in response to high blood osmolarity or low blood volume, binds to V2 receptors on collecting duct cells. In the Calvin cycle, the enzyme that fixes CO₂ by combining it with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) is:

The RNA world hypothesis proposes that early evolving systems relied on RNA for functions now divided among DNA, RNA and proteins. Which property of RNA supports this hypothesis?

  A) RNA is more chemically stable than DNA
  B) RNA always exists as a double-stranded molecule
  C) RNA contains thymine instead of uracil, making it more versatile
  D) RNA can both store genetic information and catalyze chemical reactions

### Correct Answer: RNA can both store genetic information and catalyze chemical reactions

The RNA world hypothesis is supported by the fact that RNA molecules can serve a dual function: they carry genetic information (like DNA) and can act as enzymes called ribozymes (like proteins). This dual capability suggests that RNA could have been the first self-replicating molecule before the evolution of DNA and proteins.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. RNA is more chemically stable than DNA:** This is the opposite of reality. DNA is more stable than RNA because DNA lacks the 2'-OH group on its sugar (deoxyribose vs. ribose), making it less susceptible to hydrolysis. DNA's greater resistance to backbone hydrolysis is a proposed advantage for the later evolution of DNA-based genomes.

**B. RNA always exists as a double-stranded molecule:** RNA is typically single-stranded, though it can fold into complex three-dimensional structures through intramolecular base pairing (e.g., tRNA cloverleaf, rRNA). This folding ability is actually what allows RNA to function as a catalyst.

**C. RNA contains thymine instead of uracil:** This is reversed. Standard RNA uses uracil (U) and standard DNA uses thymine (T). The chemical difference that matters for this hypothesis is RNA's 2'-OH group rather than thymine's methyl group.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Ribozymes are RNA molecules with catalytic activity (e.g., the ribosome's peptidyl transferase activity is carried out by rRNA, not protein)
  - Self-splicing introns (Group I and II) are examples of catalytic RNA
  - The RNA world hypothesis proposes an early stage in which RNA carried information and catalyzed reactions; the exact route to modern cells remains unresolved

>> Bottom Line: RNA can carry sequence information and catalyze reactions. These properties support the plausibility of an early RNA-based system, but they do not by themselves prove the historical pathway by which life arose.
