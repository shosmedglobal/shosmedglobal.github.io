Applying

How to Apply to Charles University's Third Faculty of Medicine (LF3)

10 min read

The short answer

You apply online through the Charles University portal, pay a CZK 2,000 admission fee, sit a Biology and Chemistry entrance exam, attend a short interview, and send your school documents once you pass. There is no MCAT, no personal statement and no letters of recommendation.

Applications open in the autumn and close in the spring, and the faculty sets its exam date each year. Those dates move between cycles, so this guide gives you the route rather than a calendar. The faculty publishes the current dates here, and if you are applying through us we tell you your own exam date and registration cut-off when you register.

Goes to is.cuni.cz, the university's own portal. You will need an email address and about twenty minutes.

Step 1: choose your route, because the first step differs

LF3 accepts applicants two ways, and the difference matters on day one.

Direct to the faculty. You apply yourself on the university portal and take the faculty's own sitting in Prague. You pay the faculty's CZK 2,000 fee and nothing else, and you arrange your own travel for exam day.

Through a representative. The faculty publishes a list of representatives by country, and each one runs its own sitting of the same exam, usually in or near the applicant's own country. LF3 lists SHOS Med as a representative for Canada and the United States, and you can see that on the faculty's own representatives list. The faculty also lists another agency covering both countries, so applicants there have more than one representative to choose from.

If you are in the United States or Canada, both routes are open to you and the qualification is identical either way. Applying direct means travelling to Prague for the exam. Applying through a representative means sitting the same exam closer to home. Choose on logistics, not on prestige: the faculty sets the paper and marks it the same way whichever room you sit it in.

The faculty's CZK 2,000 fee is separate, and you can handle it either way. You can pay Charles University directly by card inside the online application, which takes two minutes and costs nothing extra. Or we can take care of it for you and send you the faculty's confirmation, which is the option to use if the portal rejects your card or an international transfer is awkward from where you are.

Step 2: submit the application and save your application ID

Create your application on the Charles University portal. The programme you want is General Medicine, taught in English, at the Third Faculty of Medicine.

When you finish, write down your application ID. Everything afterwards is keyed to it: the fee payment reference, the documents you post, and any correspondence with the Study Division. People lose it and then spend a week getting it back.

Inside the application, under Proof of education, choose the option for a foreign secondary education document to be assessed. More on that in the documents section.

Step 3: pay the CZK 2,000 admission fee

CZK 2,000, or the equivalent in Euro. Pay by card inside the online application, or by bank transfer to the faculty's account. No cheques. If you transfer, you pay the bank charges, and the payment comment must carry your surname, your application ID and the words “Admission Fee”, or the faculty cannot match the money to you.

The fee and the application share one deadline. An application with no payment against it by that date is not an application, so do not leave the payment for the last evening.

Step 4: the entrance exam, and whether you can skip it

Two sections, Biology and Chemistry, 30 questions each, 60 in total. Each question has four options and exactly one correct answer, and each is worth one point, so you are scored out of 60. There is no negative marking, so a blank costs exactly as much as a wrong answer and you should never leave one. The dean sets the pass mark each year, so it moves.

Pass it and you go to a 10-minute interview, scored on a text circle, an interview circuit and a recommendation, for a published range of 12 to 62 points.

Can you skip the exam?

Some applicants can, but the criteria are narrower than they first look. The faculty's published routes to remission are: A-levels graded A, A, B in English, Chemistry and Biology; IB Higher Level in Chemistry, Biology and English totalling at least 19 points across those three subjects; a comparable examination taken in those same three subjects, with a minimum score at the 85th percentile; for applicants schooled in Germany, a grade average better than 1.30; or an accredited foreign bachelor's degree in natural or health sciences.

American readers should read that third route carefully. It is not satisfied by a strong composite SAT score. The faculty asks for results in Biology, Chemistry and English specifically, so a single overall percentile on a general admissions test is not what is being requested, however high it is. If you are considering this route, write to the Study Division with your actual score report before you rely on it.

Most of the routes also require that the qualification is recent. If you qualify, you still submit the online application, plus a signed remission form, to the Study Division by the same spring deadline, with the date of postage deciding. At most 25 applicants are admitted this way each year, so it is competitive even when you meet the criteria. Read the faculty's remission criteria in full before relying on them, and prepare for the exam in case you are not among the 25.

How to prepare for the 60 questions

The scope is a solid secondary-school Biology and Chemistry curriculum, with some areas weighted more heavily than American or British students expect. Ecology, botany and taxonomy come up more than they do on the MCAT; organic chemistry goes deeper than most national school-leaving exams. Our topic-by-topic breakdown of the LF3 entrance exam goes through all of it.

Read the faculty's own material first, because it is the only source that is definitionally correct: the official Biology and Chemistry syllabus (PDF), and the faculty's released sample paper, published by LF3 as a sample test from a previous year (PDF), with its official answer key (PDF). One sample paper is not a preparation plan, but it will tell you quickly how far you are from the standard.

After that, what helps is volume with feedback. Try 40 free LF3 biology and chemistry questions, 20 of each, which is what a SHOS account includes the moment you create one. They are written to the official syllabus, each with a worked explanation. Full access opens the rest of the bank, over 1,000 questions, with topic-level tracking so you can see which areas are actually costing you marks.

Questions and timing are different skills, and the second is where people lose marks they already knew. Our full-length SHOS LF3 mock exams run 60 questions against a two-hour timer, scored by section, with no pausing. The first one is free with an account; the rest come with full access, along with a weak-topic report aggregated across every mock you have sat.

If you are still in your final year of school

You can apply before you have the certificate. You cannot enrol without it. The faculty's deadline for the documents is the day you enrol in September, which in practice means you apply in the spring with results pending, sit the exam, and send the certificate as soon as your school issues it. Build in time for the notary, the translation and the Apostille, because those are three separate queues in most countries and none of them is fast in August.

Vaccinations

LF3 requires vaccination against hepatitis B and measles before you start your practical training in the first study year. Not in year two. Hepatitis B in particular is a multi-dose schedule spread over months, so if you are reading this in the spring, that is the moment to start it rather than the week before term.

Visa and housing

Non-EU students need permission to stay for the length of the course. In practice that is a long-stay study visa to enter, and a long-term residence permit for the purpose of studies for the years that follow, which is the route most students move onto rather than renewing a visa repeatedly. The faculty recommends applying by June for an autumn start, because the process is slow and it is the single most common reason an admitted student arrives late. It has its own document list, and we have written it up properly in the Czech student visa guide for US and Canadian medical students. For what Prague actually costs, including rent by district, see the full cost breakdown.

The admission letter, and what has to happen before it

The formal admission letter is not issued the moment you pass the exam. LF3 issues it once both conditions are met: the entrance exam requirement is satisfied, either by sitting and passing it or by being granted remission, and your secondary education has been recognised, which is the CZK 1,050 assessment described above. An applicant who passes the exam but whose certificate is still working its way through the notary and the Apostille does not yet have an admission letter.

That matters for timing, because the admission letter is the document your embassy wants. A delay in getting your certificate recognised becomes a delay in your admission letter, which becomes a delay in your visa. This is the chain that makes people miss the start of term, and it is almost always the documents rather than the exam.

Enrolment

Admitted applicants enrol in person, in September, either at the faculty building in Prague or at the Dobronice camp for incoming students. First-year tuition is due by bank transfer or by card at the Study Division. Teaching begins in the autumn term; the faculty publishes the academic calendar with the exact first day each year.

The honest summary

The administrative side of applying to LF3 is genuinely simple compared with a US medical school application. There is no MCAT, no committee letter, no personal statement, and entering costs CZK 2,000. What that means in practice is that the exam is the part you can actually influence, and it is the part most applicants under-prepare for because the paperwork looked so easy. The applicants who get in are not the ones with the best paperwork. They are the ones who treated a secondary-school syllabus as something to be mastered rather than skimmed.
