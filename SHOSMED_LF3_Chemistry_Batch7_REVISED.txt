Q1  [chem-362]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Protection of a hydroxyl group in organic synthesis is commonly achieved by converting it to:

  A. A thiol (–SH)
  B. A silyl ether (e.g., TBS ether)   <= key
  C. An amine (–NH₂)
  D. A sulfonate ester

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. A silyl ether (e.g., TBS ether)
Silyl ethers (such as TBS - tert-butyldimethylsilyl ether, or TMS - trimethylsilyl ether) are widely used protecting groups for alcohols. They are stable under many reaction conditions but can be selectively removed with fluoride ions (e.g., TBAF). Protection/deprotection strategies are essential in multi-step synthesis when a functional group must survive reactions intended for other parts of the molecule.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A thiol (–SH): A thiol (–SH) is the sulfur analog of an alcohol. While thiol-for-hydroxyl swaps exist in organic synthesis, this is a functional group transformation, not a protecting group strategy. Protection means temporarily masking a group so it can be restored later. Silyl ethers are useful because they can mask the hydroxyl group during suitable reactions and later be removed to regenerate the alcohol.
C. An amine (–NH₂): An amine (–NH₂) is not used to protect a hydroxyl group. Converting –OH to –NH₂ would be a major functional group change, not protection. Good protecting groups are easily installed, stable during subsequent reactions, and readily removed when needed. The choice of protecting group depends on which later reactions it must tolerate and how it will be removed.
D. A sulfonate ester: Tosylates and mesylates are commonly used to turn an alcohol's hydroxyl group into a good leaving group for substitution or elimination. That is activation for further reaction; a silyl ether is the standard hydroxyl-protection choice among these options.
Key Educational Points:
  - A protecting group temporarily masks a functional group so it can be restored later.
  - Silyl ethers protect alcohols under suitable reaction conditions and can often be removed with fluoride.
  - Tosylates and mesylates are commonly used to activate alcohols as leaving groups.

Q2  [chem-363]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: In retrosynthetic analysis, the symbol ⇒ represents:

  A. A forward reaction step
  B. A backward planning step from a target molecule to proposed precursors   <= key
  C. An equilibrium
  D. A catalytic step

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. A backward planning step from a target molecule to proposed precursors
Retrosynthesis plans a synthesis by working backward from the target molecule. The arrow ⇒ means 'can be made from' and points toward proposed precursors. A disconnection breaks a bond on paper; a functional-group interconversion can also be a retrosynthetic step. Synthons are idealized fragments used in planning, whereas the reagents actually used are their synthetic equivalents.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. A forward reaction step: A forward reaction step is shown by a regular reaction arrow (→). The retrosynthetic arrow (⇒) indicates the reverse thought process: working backward from the target molecule to identify simpler precursors. Retrosynthesis is a planning strategy, not an actual reaction - it maps out how a complex molecule can be built from available starting materials.
C. An equilibrium: Chemical equilibrium is shown by opposing arrows, ⇌. The retrosynthetic arrow ⇒ instead shows a planning relationship between a target and proposed precursors, not an equilibrium between them.
D. A catalytic step: A catalytic step has no special arrow notation; it is simply shown as a reaction arrow with the catalyst written above or below. The retrosynthetic arrow (⇒) means “can be derived from” - pointing from a complex target molecule to simpler building blocks. This approach, pioneered by E. J. Corey, earned him the 1990 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
Key Educational Points:
  - Retrosynthesis works backward from a target to possible starting materials.
  - The arrow ⇒ means 'can be made from'; it does not represent a reaction occurring in reverse.
  - A proposed disconnection must ultimately correspond to a feasible forward reaction.

Q3  [chem-364]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Swern oxidation uses which reagent system to oxidize an alcohol to a carbonyl compound?

  A. CrO₃/pyridine
  B. DMSO/oxalyl chloride/base   <= key
  C. PCC/CH₂Cl₂
  D. Jones reagent

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. DMSO/oxalyl chloride/base
The Swern oxidation uses DMSO activated by oxalyl chloride ((COCl)₂) at low temperature (−78°C), followed by an amine base (usually triethylamine). It oxidizes primary alcohols to aldehydes and secondary alcohols to ketones under mild, chromium-free conditions. The mild conditions and absence of toxic heavy metals make it advantageous for sensitive substrates.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. CrO₃/pyridine: A chromium trioxide-pyridine complex is a chromium-based alcohol oxidant; its use in dichloromethane is associated with Collins oxidation. It is not the DMSO/oxalyl chloride/base system that defines Swern oxidation.
C. PCC/CH₂Cl₂: PCC in dichloromethane can oxidize primary alcohols to aldehydes and secondary alcohols to ketones. It is a different, chromium-containing reagent system. Which oxidation is most suitable depends on the substrate and its other functional groups.
D. Jones reagent: Jones reagent (CrO₃ in aqueous H₂SO₄/acetone) is a strong oxidant that converts primary alcohols all the way to carboxylic acids. The Swern oxidation, by contrast, stops at the aldehyde stage and uses no metals. It works by activating DMSO to form a reactive intermediate that oxidizes the alcohol while itself being reduced to dimethyl sulfide (DMS).
Key Educational Points:
  - Swern oxidation uses activated DMSO followed by a base.
  - Primary alcohols give aldehydes; secondary alcohols give ketones.
  - Swern oxidation avoids chromium reagents, but its suitability still depends on the substrate.

Q4  [chem-365]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: A primary amide, RCONH₂, can be converted to a primary amine with one fewer carbon atom by which named reaction?

  A. Curtius rearrangement
  B. Hofmann rearrangement   <= key
  C. Lossen rearrangement
  D. Schmidt reaction

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: B. Hofmann rearrangement
The Hofmann rearrangement (or Hofmann degradation) treats a primary amide (RCONH₂) with bromine and base (Br₂/NaOH) to give a primary amine (RNH₂) with one fewer carbon (the carbonyl carbon is removed; in the basic reaction mixture it is ultimately captured as carbonate). The mechanism involves formation of an isocyanate intermediate followed by hydrolysis. The Curtius rearrangement uses acyl azides to achieve a similar result.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Curtius rearrangement: Curtius rearrangement starts from an acyl azide, RCON₃, rather than the primary amide specified here. It can also yield a primary amine with one fewer carbon after formation and hydrolysis of an isocyanate.
C. Lossen rearrangement: Lossen rearrangement starts from an activated hydroxamic acid derivative. It can form an isocyanate and ultimately an amine, but its starting material is not the simple primary amide in the stem.
D. Schmidt reaction: In the carboxylic-acid version of the Schmidt reaction, a carboxylic acid reacts with hydrazoic acid under acidic conditions to give an amine with one fewer carbon. It is not the rearrangement of a primary amide asked about here.
Key Educational Points:
  - Hofmann rearrangement converts a primary amide into a primary amine with one fewer carbon.
  - The carbon removed is the original carbonyl carbon.
  - Related rearrangements can give similar products but begin with different functional groups.

Q5  [chem-366]  correct answer: B   (KEEP: unchanged)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which statement best describes how enzymes accelerate chemical reactions?

  A. They increase the temperature of the reaction
  B. They lower the activation energy of the reaction   <= key
  C. They shift the equilibrium toward products
  D. They increase the free energy change (ΔG) of the reaction

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: They lower the activation energy of the reaction
Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up reactions by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy barrier. They achieve this by stabilizing the transition state through precise molecular interactions in the active site - including hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, and transient covalent bonds with the substrate.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Increase temperature: Enzymes do not change temperature; they work by lowering the energy barrier at the existing temperature, which is why biological reactions proceed rapidly at body temperature.
C. Shift equilibrium: Enzymes accelerate both forward and reverse reactions equally - they do not change the position of equilibrium or the equilibrium constant (Keq).
D. Increase ΔG: Enzymes do not alter the thermodynamics (ΔG) of a reaction. They only affect kinetics (how fast equilibrium is reached), not whether or which direction is favorable.
Bottom Line: Enzymes lower activation energy without changing ΔG or equilibrium. They are specific (lock-and-key or induced-fit model), reusable, and regulated by factors like pH, temperature, inhibitors, and allosteric modulators.

Q6  [chem-367]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: An enzyme that catalyzes the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to a substrate is classified as a:

  A. Oxidoreductase
  B. Transferase   <= key
  C. Hydrolase
  D. Lyase

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Transferase
The IUBMB enzyme classification has seven main classes, based on the reactions catalyzed. Transferases catalyze the transfer of functional groups (such as methyl, amino, phosphate, or glycosyl groups) from one molecule to another. Kinases are a specific subclass of transferases that transfer phosphate groups from ATP to substrates.
| Class | Reaction Type | Example |
| Oxidoreductase | Electron transfer (redox) | Dehydrogenases, oxidases |
| Transferase | Group transfer | Kinases, transaminases |
| Hydrolase | Hydrolysis (bond cleavage by water) | Lipase, protease |
| Lyase | Bond cleavage without hydrolysis or oxidation, or the reverse addition reaction | Decarboxylase, aldolase |
| Isomerase | Structural rearrangement | Mutase, racemase |
| Ligase | Bond formation coupled to an energy-supplying reaction, commonly nucleotide cleavage | DNA ligase, synthetases |
| Translocase | Movement of ions or molecules across membranes, or their separation within membranes | Na⁺/K⁺-transporting ATPase |
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Oxidoreductase: These catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions (electron transfer), not group transfer.
C. Hydrolase: Hydrolases break bonds by adding water; they do not transfer groups between molecules.
D. Lyase: Lyases cleave bonds without hydrolysis or oxidation (e.g., removing CO₂ or H₂O), not transferring groups.
Bottom Line: The seven EC classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases and translocases. A kinase that transfers a phosphate group from ATP to a substrate is a transferase.

STEM: Which vitamin serves as the precursor for the coenzymes NAD⁺ and NADP⁺, and its deficiency causes pellagra (the disease of the 4 D's: dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, death)?

  A. Thiamine (B1)
  B. Riboflavin (B2)
  C. Niacin (B3)   <= key
  D. Pyridoxine (B6)

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Niacin (B3)
Niacin (nicotinic acid or nicotinamide) is the precursor of NAD⁺ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) and NADP⁺, which are essential coenzymes in hundreds of redox reactions in metabolism. NAD⁺ is crucial in glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Severe deficiency leads to pellagra, classically characterized by dermatitis (on sun-exposed skin), diarrhea, dementia, and if untreated, death.
| Vitamin | Coenzyme Form | Deficiency Disease |
| Thiamine (B1) | TPP (thiamine pyrophosphate) | Beriberi, Wernicke-Korsakoff |
| Riboflavin (B2) | FAD, FMN | Cheilosis, angular stomatitis |
| Niacin (B3) | NAD⁺, NADP⁺ | Pellagra (4 D's) |
| Pyridoxine (B6) | PLP (pyridoxal phosphate) | Peripheral neuropathy, anemia |
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Thiamine (B1): Forms TPP, a coenzyme for pyruvate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase; deficiency causes beriberi (wet: cardiac; dry: neurological).
B. Riboflavin (B2): Forms FAD and FMN, electron carriers in the ETC and various oxidases; deficiency causes oral/skin lesions, not pellagra.
D. Pyridoxine (B6): Forms PLP, essential for transamination and amino acid metabolism; deficiency causes neuropathy and sideroblastic anemia.
Bottom Line: Niacin supplies the nicotinamide part of NAD⁺ and NADP⁺. Severe deficiency causes pellagra. Tryptophan can also contribute to niacin production; the dietary convention is approximately 60 mg tryptophan per 1 mg niacin equivalent. A poorly varied diet dominated by untreated maize can increase risk because its niacin is poorly available and it supplies relatively little tryptophan.

Q8  [chem-369]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: In human cells, most enzymes of the citric acid cycle are located in the:

  A. Cytoplasm
  B. Mitochondrial matrix   <= key
  C. Inner mitochondrial membrane
  D. Endoplasmic reticulum

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Mitochondrial matrix
Most citric acid cycle reactions occur in the mitochondrial matrix. The important exception is succinate dehydrogenase, which is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane and also functions as Complex II of the electron transport chain. The cycle oxidizes acetyl-CoA, releases CO₂ and transfers electrons to NAD⁺ and enzyme-bound FAD. These electrons can then support oxidative phosphorylation.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Cytoplasm: Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol. The citric acid cycle is mainly in the mitochondrial matrix, where acetyl-CoA can come from carbohydrate, fatty acid or amino acid metabolism. Entry into the cycle does not always require pyruvate as the source.
C. Inner mitochondrial membrane: The inner mitochondrial membrane contains the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. It also contains succinate dehydrogenase, but most citric acid cycle enzymes are in the matrix, which is what the stem asks.
D. Endoplasmic reticulum: The ER is involved in protein folding (rough ER) and lipid synthesis (smooth ER), not in the citric acid cycle.
Bottom Line: Per acetyl-CoA, a turn of the cycle yields 3 NADH, one FAD-linked reduction, 1 GTP or ATP, and 2 CO₂. The FAD is bound to succinate dehydrogenase. Most cycle enzymes are in the matrix; succinate dehydrogenase is the membrane-bound exception.

Q9  [chem-370]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: In human cells carrying out aerobic respiration, the majority of ATP generated from glucose is produced by:

  A. Substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis
  B. Substrate-level phosphorylation in the Krebs cycle
  C. Oxidative phosphorylation (chemiosmosis) in the electron transport chain   <= key
  D. Photophosphorylation in chloroplasts

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Oxidative phosphorylation (chemiosmosis) in the electron transport chain
Oxidative phosphorylation supplies about 26-28 ATP out of the approximate 30-32 ATP obtained per glucose in the standard accounting for human cells. The other 4 ATP equivalents are the net 2 ATP from glycolysis and 2 GTP or ATP from the citric acid cycle. Electrons enter the respiratory chain at different points and ultimately reduce oxygen to water. Complexes I, III and IV pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane; ATP synthase uses their return flow to make ATP. The yield varies with the shuttle used for cytosolic NADH and with coupling efficiency.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis: Glycolysis produces only 2 net ATP per glucose via substrate-level phosphorylation - a small fraction of total yield.
B. Substrate-level phosphorylation in the Krebs cycle: The Krebs cycle produces 2 GTP (equivalent to 2 ATP) per glucose via substrate-level phosphorylation.
D. Photophosphorylation in chloroplasts: This occurs in chloroplasts during photosynthesis, not during cellular respiration in mitochondria.
Bottom Line: Most ATP from aerobic glucose oxidation comes from oxidative phosphorylation. Common estimates are about 2.5 ATP per mitochondrial NADH and about 1.5 ATP per electron pair entering the ubiquinone pool while bypassing Complex I. The latter occurs during succinate oxidation by Complex II; its FAD is enzyme-bound, not a free FADH₂ molecule entering the complex.

Q10  [chem-371]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which is one of the four standard bases in RNA but not one of the four standard bases in DNA?

  A. Adenine
  B. Guanine
  C. Uracil   <= key
  D. Cytosine

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Uracil
RNA contains the bases adenine, guanine, cytosine, and uracil. DNA contains adenine, guanine, cytosine, and thymine instead of uracil. Uracil differs from thymine by lacking a methyl group at the C-5 position. In RNA, uracil pairs with adenine through two hydrogen bonds, just as thymine does in DNA.
| Feature | DNA | RNA |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | A, T, G, C | A, U, G, C |
| Structure | Usually a double-stranded helix | Usually single-stranded |
| Stability | More resistant to alkaline hydrolysis | More susceptible because of the 2'-OH group |
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Adenine: Found in both DNA and RNA; it pairs with thymine in DNA and uracil in RNA.
B. Guanine: Found in both DNA and RNA; it pairs with cytosine in both nucleic acids.
D. Cytosine: Found in both DNA and RNA; it pairs with guanine through three hydrogen bonds in both.
Bottom Line: DNA uses thymine; RNA uses uracil. The methyl group on thymine helps DNA repair enzymes distinguish thymine from deaminated cytosine (which produces uracil), protecting DNA integrity - a likely evolutionary reason DNA uses thymine.

Q11  [chem-372]  correct answer: B
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: Which lipid class provides the basic bilayer framework of human cell membranes?

  A. Triglycerides
  B. Phospholipids   <= key
  C. Cholesterol
  D. Waxes

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Phospholipids
Typical membrane phospholipids have a polar, phosphate-containing head and two hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains. In water, these amphipathic molecules assemble into the bilayer framework of human cell membranes, with the polar heads facing water and the nonpolar chains sheltered inside. This bilayer acts as a selectively permeable barrier, allowing small nonpolar molecules to pass while restricting ions and large polar molecules.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Triglycerides: Triglycerides (triacylglycerols) are energy storage lipids found in adipose tissue; they are fully hydrophobic and do not form bilayers.
C. Cholesterol: Cholesterol is an important membrane component that modulates fluidity, but it is not the primary structural lipid - it inserts between phospholipids.
D. Waxes: Waxes are esters of long-chain fatty acids and alcohols; they serve as waterproof coatings on surfaces (leaves, feathers) rather than membrane components.
Bottom Line: Phospholipid bilayers form spontaneously due to the hydrophobic effect. The fluid mosaic model describes membranes as dynamic structures with phospholipids, cholesterol, and proteins that can move laterally. Membrane fluidity is influenced by fatty acid saturation, chain length, and cholesterol content.

Q12  [chem-373]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: At pH 7.4, which form predominates for free glycine (R = H)?

  A. Fully protonated cation (NH₃⁺-CHR-COOH)
  B. Fully deprotonated anion (NH₂-CHR-COO⁻)
  C. Zwitterion (NH₃⁺-CHR-COO⁻)   <= key
  D. Uncharged molecule (NH₂-CHR-COOH)

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Zwitterion (NH₃⁺-CHR-COO⁻)
At pH 7.4, glycine's carboxyl group is predominantly deprotonated (COO⁻) and its amino group predominantly protonated (NH₃⁺). The predominant species therefore carries one positive and one negative charge and is a zwitterion. This does not mean that pH 7.4 is glycine's isoelectric point: its pI is about 6. The pI is the pH at which the population of molecules has zero average net charge. Amino acids with ionizable side chains can have a different predominant net charge at pH 7.4.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Fully protonated cation (NH₃⁺-CHR-COOH): The fully protonated cation predominates at sufficiently low pH, when the carboxyl group is mainly COOH. At pH 7.4, glycine's carboxyl group is mainly COO⁻.
B. Fully deprotonated anion (NH₂-CHR-COO⁻): The form with NH₂ and COO⁻ predominates at sufficiently high pH, when the amino group loses its proton. At pH 7.4, that amino group is still mainly NH₃⁺.
D. Uncharged molecule (NH₂-CHR-COOH): The uncharged NH₂-CHR-COOH form is not the predominant form of glycine in water at pH 7.4. A zwitterion has charged groups even though its own charges sum to zero.
Bottom Line: Glycine is predominantly a zwitterion at pH 7.4. Predominant molecular form and isoelectric point are different concepts. Do not assume every amino acid has zero net charge at physiological pH.

Q13  [chem-374]  correct answer: C
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEM: The bond that links amino acids together in a polypeptide chain is called a:

  A. Glycosidic bond
  B. Ester bond
  C. Peptide bond   <= key
  D. Phosphodiester bond

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Peptide bond
A peptide bond is a covalent amide linkage, –C(=O)–N–, joining amino-acid residues in a polypeptide. Its net formation from free amino and carboxyl groups can be drawn as loss of water. In cells, however, ribosomes form peptide bonds using activated amino acids attached to tRNAs, rather than directly dehydrating free amino acids. Resonance gives the peptide C–N bond partial double-bond character, restricting its rotation and making the peptide group approximately planar.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. Glycosidic bond: Links monosaccharides together in carbohydrates (e.g., glucose units in starch); not found between amino acids.
B. Ester bond: Links fatty acids to glycerol in lipids (triglycerides, phospholipids); also found in some other biomolecules but not in peptide chains.
D. Phosphodiester bond: Links nucleotides together in nucleic acids (DNA and RNA backbone); not found in polypeptides.
Bottom Line: Peptide bonds link amino acids in proteins. Glycosidic bonds link sugars. Phosphodiester bonds link nucleotides. Ester bonds link fatty acids to glycerol. Each biopolymer has its characteristic linkage type.

  D. Reach a new equilibrium with a different equilibrium constant

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Shift toward products to partially consume the added reactant

Iron is a transition metal that can form ions with different charges. In FeCl₃, each chloride ion carries a −1 charge, and three chloride ions give a total negative charge of −3. STEM: According to Chargaff's rules for double-stranded DNA, if a sample contains 22% adenine, what percentage of the bases is cytosine?

  A. 22%
  B. 28%   <= key
  C. 44%
  D. 11%

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: 28%
Chargaff's rules state that in double-stranded DNA, adenine pairs with thymine (A = T) and guanine pairs with cytosine (G = C). If adenine = 22%, then thymine = 22%. Together, A + T = 44%. Since all four bases must total 100%, G + C = 100% − 44% = 56%. Because G = C, each equals 56% ÷ 2 = 28%.
Why the other choices are wrong:
A. 22%: This would be correct if cytosine paired with adenine, but it does not. Cytosine pairs with guanine. The 22% figure applies to thymine (A = T = 22%).
C. 44%: This is the combined percentage of A + T (22% + 22%). Cytosine alone cannot account for 44% when all four bases must sum to 100%.
D. 11%: This error might arise from halving the adenine percentage, but there is no chemical basis for this calculation. Chargaff's rules relate complementary bases (A with T, G with C), not halving individual base percentages.
Key Educational Points:
  - In conventional double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C because of complementary base pairing
  - These rules arise from Watson-Crick base pairing: A forms 2 hydrogen bonds with T; G forms 3 hydrogen bonds with C
  - Single-stranded nucleic acids need not satisfy these equalities. Complementary double-stranded RNA instead has A = U and G = C
Bottom Line: In dsDNA, A = T and G = C. With 22% adenine: T = 22%, leaving C = G = 28% each.

STEM: Which of the following disaccharides is a NON-reducing sugar?

  A. Maltose
  B. Lactose
  C. Cellobiose
  D. Sucrose   <= key

EXPLANATION:
Correct Answer: Sucrose
Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar because the glycosidic bond links the anomeric carbons of BOTH monosaccharide units - C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose (an α(1→2)β linkage: α at glucose C1 and β at fructose C2). B. Lactose: Lactose (galactose-β-1,4-glucose) is a reducing sugar. The glycosidic bond involves C1 of galactose, but the anomeric C1 of the glucose residue remains free and can undergo ring-opening to expose its aldehyde group.
C. Cellobiose: Cellobiose (glucose-β-1,4-glucose) is a reducing sugar for the same reason as maltose - one glucose residue retains a free anomeric carbon. The only difference from maltose is the beta configuration of the glycosidic bond.
Key Educational Points:
  - For these disaccharides, a free anomeric hemiacetal permits ring opening and reducing behavior
  - Sucrose links both anomeric centers through an α(1→2)β bond and is non-reducing before hydrolysis
  - Maltose, lactose, and cellobiose each have one free anomeric carbon → reducing sugars
Bottom Line: Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons participate in the glycosidic bond, leaving no free hemiacetal to act as a reducing agent.
