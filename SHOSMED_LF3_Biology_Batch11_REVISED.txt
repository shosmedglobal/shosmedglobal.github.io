Many cyanobacteria use accessory pigments in addition to chlorophyll a. Which pair consists of phycobiliproteins?

  A) Phycocyanin and phycoerythrin
  B) Chlorophyll b and carotenoids
  C) Xanthophyll and anthocyanin
  D) Bacteriochlorophyll and rhodopsin

### Correct Answer: Phycocyanin and phycoerythrin

Phycocyanin and phycoerythrin are light-harvesting pigment-protein complexes called **phycobiliproteins**. Phycocyanin is blue and phycoerythrin is red. In many cyanobacteria, phycobiliproteins form antenna structures called **phycobilisomes** that transfer absorbed light energy to the photosynthetic machinery.

These pigments broaden the wavelengths available for photosynthesis, including wavelengths absorbed relatively poorly by chlorophyll a. Chlorophyll a and phycocyanin together help produce the familiar blue-green appearance; organisms rich in phycoerythrin can appear redder. Pigment composition varies between cyanobacterial groups.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Chlorophyll b and carotenoids:** These are different pigment groups, not phycobiliproteins. Some cyanobacteria contain chlorophyll b, and cyanobacteria also contain carotenoids, so their presence cannot be ruled out across the whole group.

**C. Xanthophyll and anthocyanin:** Xanthophylls are oxygen-containing carotenoids, and some occur in cyanobacteria. Anthocyanins are plant pigments associated with red, purple and blue coloration. Neither belongs to the phycobiliprotein group asked about here.

**D. Bacteriochlorophyll and rhodopsin:** Bacteriochlorophylls are associated with anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria. Rhodopsins are retinal-containing light-sensitive proteins, some of which act as light-driven ion pumps. These are different systems from phycobiliprotein antennae.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Phycocyanin and phycoerythrin are phycobiliproteins that help capture light energy
  - Many cyanobacteria and red algae use phycobilisomes; these structures are not unique to cyanobacteria
  - Pigment composition varies, and some cyanobacteria adjust their pigments to the available light

>> Bottom Line: Phycocyanin and phycoerythrin are accessory phycobiliproteins. Phycocyanin is blue, phycoerythrin is red, and their light-harvesting roles should be distinguished from those of chlorophylls and carotenoids.

Sickle cell disease results from a glutamic acid to valine substitution in β-globin. This change FIRST affects which level of protein structure?

  A) Primary structure
  B) Secondary structure
  C) Tertiary structure
  D) Quaternary structure

### Correct Answer: Primary structure

**Primary structure** is the amino acid sequence of a protein. Replacing glutamate with valine at position 6 of beta-globin therefore directly changes primary structure. The substitution replaces a charged side chain with a hydrophobic one. In deoxygenated HbS, this favors abnormal contacts between hemoglobin molecules, allowing them to assemble into fibers that distort red blood cells.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Secondary structure:** Secondary structure refers to local folding patterns such as alpha helices and beta sheets. An amino acid substitution directly changes the sequence; it does not automatically disrupt these local patterns. The primary-structure change is what this question asks you to identify.

**C. Tertiary structure:** Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional shape of one polypeptide chain. The HbS substitution changes the chemistry of a surface site, but its key consequence is abnormal association between hemoglobin molecules, rather than wholesale unfolding of beta-globin. The directly altered level is the amino acid sequence.

**D. Quaternary structure:** Quaternary structure describes the association of protein subunits; normal adult hemoglobin contains two alpha and two beta chains. HbS fibers arise from abnormal association of hemoglobin tetramers. This is a consequence of the substitution, whereas the substitution itself changes primary structure.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The amino acid sequence is the primary structure, so any substitution alters primary structure first.
  - In sickle cell disease, glutamate is replaced by valine at position 6 of beta-globin (E6V).
  - The change swaps a charged residue for a hydrophobic one, creating a sticky surface patch on deoxygenated haemoglobin.
  - That patch drives HbS polymerisation into fibres, distorting the red cell into the sickle shape.
  - A single amino acid substitution can alter protein behavior and cause disease without grossly disrupting every level of folding.

>> Bottom Line: An amino acid substitution directly changes primary structure. In HbS, the glutamate-to-valine substitution promotes abnormal association of deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules into fibers.

Which of the following features is found in BOTH prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?

  A) Membrane-bound nucleus
  B) 70S ribosomes free in the cytosol
  C) A plasma membrane surrounding the cell
  D) Linear chromosomes with histone proteins

### Correct Answer: A plasma membrane surrounding the cell

Both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells have a **plasma membrane** separating their internal contents from the environment. The membrane helps control the movement of substances into and out of the cell. Its presence is the shared feature. Its lipid composition and arrangement are not identical in every organism: some archaea have membranes formed by a lipid monolayer rather than a bilayer.

  | Feature | Typical prokaryotic cell | Typical eukaryotic cell |
  | Membrane-bound nucleus | Absent | Present |
  | Ribosomes in the cytosol | 70S | 80S |
  | Chromosomes | Usually circular; DNA-packaging proteins vary | Nuclear chromosomes are usually linear and associated with histones |
  | Plasma membrane | Present | Present |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Membrane-bound nucleus:** A typical eukaryotic cell has a nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope. Prokaryotic cells lack this enclosure; their DNA occupies a nucleoid region. A membrane-bound nucleus is therefore not a shared feature.

**B. 70S ribosomes free in the cytosol:** Prokaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are 70S, whereas eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are 80S. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own ribosomes, but these are inside organelles and are not all the same size.

**D. Linear chromosomes with histone proteins:** This combination is characteristic of eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes, not a general feature shared by both cell types. Prokaryotic chromosomes are usually circular, and their packaging proteins vary. Many archaea have histones, so "prokaryotes never have histones" would be incorrect.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A plasma membrane is shared by prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  - The membrane separates the internal environment from the surroundings and regulates exchange.
  - Typical prokaryotic and eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are 70S and 80S, respectively.
  - A membrane-bound nucleus distinguishes typical eukaryotic cells from prokaryotic cells.
  - Prokaryotes include both Bacteria and Archaea; statements about bacterial membranes or DNA packaging should not automatically be applied to all archaea.

>> Bottom Line: All cells have a plasma membrane. A phospholipid bilayer, a particular chromosome shape and the absence of histones are not universal properties of all prokaryotes.

In human females, ovulation is triggered by a surge of which hormone?

  A) Estrogen
  B) Progesterone
  C) Luteinizing hormone (LH)
  D) Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)

### Correct Answer: Luteinizing hormone (LH)

In a typical 28-day cycle, ovulation occurs around day 14, although the timing varies between people and cycles. A surge of **LH** from the anterior pituitary triggers the mature ovarian follicle to rupture and release its oocyte. Sustained high estradiol levels from the dominant follicle produce positive feedback on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, leading to the LH surge.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Estrogen:** Rising estrogen from the follicle triggers the LH surge indirectly through positive feedback, but estrogen itself does not cause follicle rupture.

**B. Progesterone:** Progesterone is primarily secreted by the corpus luteum AFTER ovulation; it prepares the endometrium for implantation during the luteal phase.

**D. FSH:** FSH stimulates follicle growth and maturation in the early follicular phase but does not trigger the rupture event of ovulation.

>> Bottom Line: The menstrual cycle: FSH stimulates follicle growth → rising estrogen → LH surge → ovulation → corpus luteum forms → progesterone rises → if no implantation, corpus luteum degenerates → menstruation. Ovulation = LH surge.

Two genes are 8 cM apart on the same chromosome. An individual with chromosome arrangement AB/ab is testcrossed with ab/ab. Using the short-distance approximation and ignoring undetected multiple crossovers, approximately what percentage of offspring will be recombinant?

  A) 50%
  B) 8%
  C) 42%
  D) 16%

### Correct Answer: 8%

For a short interval, a map distance of 8 cM corresponds to approximately **8% recombinant gametes**. The ab/ab parent contributes only ab gametes, so offspring genotypes reveal which gamete came from the AB/ab parent.

The parental gametes are AB and ab; the recombinant gametes are Ab and aB. With the stated approximation and equal survival of the offspring classes:

  - Parental offspring: AaBb, approximately 46%; aabb, approximately 46%.
  - Recombinant offspring: Aabb, approximately 4%; aaBb, approximately 4%.
Thus, approximately 8% are recombinant and 92% are parental.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. 50%:** A recombination frequency near 50% means that the loci behave as unlinked in a two-locus testcross. These genes are close together, so most offspring retain parental combinations. Widely separated linked loci can also approach 50% because multiple crossovers obscure their linkage.

**C. 42%:** Subtracting 8% from 50% does not calculate the recombinant percentage. Approximately 8% are recombinant in total. The remaining 92% are parental, giving approximately 46% in each of the two parental classes under this model.

**D. 16%:** The 8% already includes both recombinant classes. Each contributes approximately 4%, so they add to 8%, not 16%. Counting the two classes does not justify doubling the map distance.

### Key Educational Points:

  - For closely linked genes, 1 cM corresponds approximately to 1% recombination.
  - Genes closer together are less likely to be separated by recombination.
  - In a testcross, the recessive parent's known gamete allows offspring genotypes to reveal the heterozygous parent's gametes.
  - The expected recombination frequency in the standard two-locus model does not exceed 50%.
  - Here, AB and ab are parental combinations because the stem explicitly gives the phase as AB/ab.
  - Multiple crossovers can go undetected in two-locus offspring counts, so genetic map distance and observed recombination percentage diverge over longer intervals.

>> Bottom Line: With AB/ab × ab/ab and the short-distance approximation, 8 cM gives approximately 8% recombinant offspring: 4% Aabb plus 4% aaBb.

A patient develops periorbital edema, myalgia, fever, and eosinophilia two weeks after eating undercooked wild boar meat at a barbecue. Muscle biopsy reveals encysted larvae within skeletal muscle fibers. Which parasite is responsible?

  A) Taenia solium
  B) Toxocara canis
  C) Trichinella spiralis
  D) Dracunculus medinensis

### Correct Answer: Trichinella spiralis

**Trichinella spiralis** is one of the species that causes **trichinellosis**, acquired by eating **undercooked meat containing infective larvae**, including pork and wild game. After ingestion, larvae are released in the small intestine, mature into adults, and produce new larvae that disseminate via the bloodstream and encyst in **striated (skeletal) muscle**.

Periorbital edema, muscle pain and eosinophilia are characteristic clues, especially after eating undercooked wild game. Demonstrating Trichinella larvae within affected muscle tissue supports the diagnosis.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Taenia solium:** Ingesting T. solium eggs can cause cysticercosis, including cysts in muscle or the nervous system. Ingesting pork containing cysticerci instead causes an intestinal tapeworm infection. The meat exposure, systemic symptoms and larvae within muscle fibers in this stem favor trichinellosis.

**B. Toxocara canis:** Toxocara larvae can migrate through organs including the liver, lungs, eyes and skeletal muscle. Infection usually follows ingestion of eggs from contaminated soil, but infected meat or offal can also transmit larvae. The characteristic Trichinella muscle-stage appearance and the overall presentation make T. spiralis the best choice here.

**D. Dracunculus medinensis:** Dracunculus (Guinea worm) is acquired by drinking water containing infected copepods (water fleas). The adult female migrates to the skin (usually the lower extremity) and creates a painful blister to release larvae. It does not encyst in skeletal muscle and is not transmitted through meat.

### Key Educational Points:

  - T. spiralis completes its life cycle within mammalian hosts; transmission occurs when an animal eats infected meat
  - Cook meat thoroughly using appropriate food-safety guidance and a food thermometer; freezing wild game is not a reliable way to kill Trichinella larvae
  - Albendazole or mebendazole may be used; severe inflammatory complications may also require corticosteroids

>> Bottom Line: Trichinella larvae acquired in undercooked meat can invade skeletal muscle, producing myalgia, periorbital edema and eosinophilia. The exposure history and muscle findings identify trichinellosis here.

Which mosquito genus–disease pairing is correct?

  A) Anopheles - dengue fever
  B) Aedes - malaria
  C) Culex - Japanese encephalitis
  D) Anopheles - yellow fever

### Correct Answer: Culex - Japanese encephalitis

**Japanese encephalitis virus** is transmitted mainly by Culex mosquitoes, including Culex tritaeniorhynchus. Transmission is often associated with rural rice-growing areas. Different Culex species can transmit other infections, including West Nile virus and, in some regions, lymphatic filariasis.

  | Mosquito genus | Important disease examples | Useful general pattern |
  | Anopheles | Malaria | Many vectors bite from dusk to dawn; breeding habitats vary |
  | Aedes | Dengue, Zika, chikungunya; urban yellow fever | Important vectors such as A. aegypti often bite during the day and breed in water containers |

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Anopheles - dengue fever:** The principal dengue vectors are Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. Female Anopheles mosquitoes are the vectors responsible for mosquito-borne human malaria.

**B. Aedes - malaria:** Aedes species transmit several important viral diseases, but they are not the mosquito vectors of human malaria. That role belongs to Anopheles.

**D. Anopheles - yellow fever:** Aedes aegypti is an important urban yellow-fever vector. Other mosquitoes transmit yellow fever in forest cycles, including Haemagogus and Sabethes in the Americas. Anopheles is not the correct pairing.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Useful pairings are Anopheles–malaria, Aedes–dengue and Culex–Japanese encephalitis

A hiker in Central Europe develops an expanding erythematous ring-shaped skin lesion (erythema migrans) at the site of a tick bite, followed weeks later by facial nerve palsy and cardiac arrhythmias. Which organism is responsible for this disease?

  A) Rickettsia rickettsii
  B) Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (species complex)
  C) Francisella tularensis
  D) Tick-borne encephalitis virus

### Correct Answer: Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (species complex)

**Lyme borreliosis** is caused by bacteria in the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato species complex. In Europe, important members include B. afzelii and B. garinii as well as B. burgdorferi sensu stricto. Ixodes ricinus is an important European vector.

**Erythema migrans** is an expanding skin lesion that may have central clearing but does not always look like a bull's-eye. Disseminated infection can affect nerves and the heart, causing facial palsy, meningitis or atrioventricular conduction block. Later manifestations can include arthritis and, in Europe, particular chronic skin changes. These manifestations do not have to occur in a fixed sequence, and not every patient develops all of them.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Rickettsia rickettsii:** This bacterium causes Rocky Mountain spotted fever, a different tick-borne illness. Fever, headache and a rash that may become petechial are typical clues. It does not explain the characteristic expanding erythema migrans lesion with subsequent Lyme-type neurological and cardiac involvement.

**C. Francisella tularensis:** Tularemia can follow tick bites or contact with infected animals. A skin ulcer with enlarged regional lymph nodes is a common presentation. That differs from the expanding erythema migrans lesion in this stem.

**D. Tick-borne encephalitis virus:** This virus is also transmitted by Ixodes ticks in Europe and can cause meningitis or encephalitis. It does not produce the characteristic erythema migrans lesion that points to Lyme borreliosis here.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A typical erythema migrans lesion in an appropriate exposure setting supports a clinical diagnosis; it need not have a classic bull's-eye appearance
  - Preventive measures include avoiding tick bites and checking for and promptly removing attached ticks
  - Ixodes ticks can transmit several pathogens; which infections occur depends on tick species and geography

>> Bottom Line: An expanding erythema migrans lesion after a tick bite, followed by facial palsy or cardiac conduction problems, suggests Lyme borreliosis. In Europe, several members of the B. burgdorferi sensu lato complex can cause it.

  A) Rat flea (Xenopsylla cheopis)
  B) Human body louse (Pediculus humanus corporis)
  C) Hard tick (Ixodes ricinus)
  D) Deer fly (Chrysops)

### Correct Answer: Human body louse (Pediculus humanus corporis)

**Epidemic typhus** is caused by Rickettsia prowazekii and transmitted by the **human body louse (Pediculus humanus corporis)**. The disease occurs in conditions of overcrowding and poor hygiene (refugee camps, prisons, wars) where body lice can proliferate. Transmission occurs when louse feces containing R. prowazekii are scratched into the skin - the louse itself dies of the infection.

Epidemic typhus presents with high fever, severe headache, and a maculopapular rash spreading from the trunk outward (centrifugally).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Rat flea (Xenopsylla cheopis):** Rat fleas are associated with plague and flea-borne murine typhus, caused by Rickettsia typhi. Epidemic typhus is caused by R. prowazekii and is classically associated with body lice in crowded conditions. These similar disease names refer to different infections and vectors.

**C. Hard tick (Ixodes ricinus):** Ixodes ticks transmit Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), TBE virus, Babesia, and Anaplasma - not epidemic typhus. Tick-borne rickettsioses (e.g., Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever from R. rickettsii via Dermacentor ticks) are caused by different Rickettsia species than epidemic typhus.

**D. Deer fly (Chrysops):** Chrysops deer flies transmit **Loa loa** (African eye worm) and Francisella tularensis (tularemia). They are not the established vector of epidemic typhus. Loa loa causes subcutaneous swellings (Calabar swellings) and the adult worm may be visible crossing the conjunctiva of the eye.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Body lice transmit three diseases: epidemic typhus (R. prowazekii), trench fever (Bartonella quintana), and relapsing fever (Borrelia recurrentis)
  - Epidemic (louse-borne) typhus vs. Endemic (flea-borne/murine) typhus - different vectors, different Rickettsia species
  - Delousing (removal of lice) is the primary public health intervention during outbreaks

>> Bottom Line: Epidemic typhus = body louse = overcrowding/poor hygiene. The louse transmits R. prowazekii through its feces, not its bite. Body lice also transmit trench fever and relapsing fever.

Some filamentous cyanobacteria form specialized thick-walled cells that fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia. What are these cells called?

  A) Heterocysts
  B) Akinetes
  C) Hormogonia
  D) Baeocytes

### Correct Answer: Heterocysts

**Heterocysts** are specialized, thick-walled cells found in certain filamentous cyanobacteria (e.g., Anabaena, Nostoc) that are dedicated to **nitrogen fixation**. They convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonia (NH₃) using the enzyme **nitrogenase**.

The key challenge is that nitrogenase is **irreversibly inactivated by oxygen**, yet cyanobacteria produce oxygen through photosynthesis. Heterocysts solve this problem by:

  - Having a thick glycolipid envelope that limits O₂ diffusion
  - Suppressing oxygen-producing Photosystem II activity while retaining Photosystem I-dependent electron flow that supports ATP production
  - Possessing high rates of cellular respiration to consume residual O₂

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Akinetes:** Akinetes are thick-walled **resting spores** (dormant cells) formed by some filamentous cyanobacteria under unfavorable environmental conditions such as cold, drought, or nutrient depletion. They serve as survival structures and can germinate when conditions improve. They are not involved in nitrogen fixation.

**C. Hormogonia:** Hormogonia are short, motile segments of a cyanobacterial filament that break off and glide away to establish new colonies at different locations. They function in **dispersal and vegetative reproduction**, not nitrogen fixation. They are typically composed of smaller cells and may have gas vesicles for buoyancy.

**D. Baeocytes:** Baeocytes are small, spherical reproductive cells produced by some unicellular cyanobacteria (e.g., Dermocarpa) through a process called multiple fission. The parent cell divides internally to produce many baeocytes that are released upon rupture of the parent cell wall. They are reproductive structures, not nitrogen-fixing cells.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Heterocysts exchange fixed nitrogen and carbon compounds with neighboring vegetative cells through connections called septal junctions
  - Nitrogen fixation converts N₂ to ammonia and requires substantial ATP and reducing power
  - Cyanobacteria are ecologically important as they contribute significantly to the global nitrogen cycle, particularly in oceanic and rice paddy ecosystems

>> Bottom Line: Heterocysts are specialized nitrogen-fixing cells in some filamentous cyanobacteria. They maintain a low-oxygen interior to protect nitrogenase while neighboring cells supply carbon compounds.

About 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen began to accumulate persistently in Earth's atmosphere, with oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria providing its biological source. What is this major transition called?

  A) The Great Oxidation Event
  B) The Cambrian Explosion
  C) The Permian-Triassic Extinction
  D) The Late Heavy Bombardment

### Correct Answer: The Great Oxidation Event

The **Great Oxidation Event** was the major rise in atmospheric oxygen beginning about **2.4 billion years ago**. Oxygenic photosynthesis evolved before this transition. Atmospheric oxygen accumulated when its production and preservation exceeded the processes consuming it, including reactions with reduced materials in the oceans and crust. This did not immediately produce today's atmospheric oxygen level.

**Stromatolites** are layered sedimentary structures associated with microbial communities, which can trap sediment and promote mineral precipitation. Many modern examples involve cyanobacteria. Ancient stromatolites provide evidence about early microbial life, but their shape alone does not identify the organisms or prove that they produced oxygen.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. The Cambrian Explosion:** This diversification of animal life occurred about 540 million years ago, much later than the Great Oxidation Event. Oxygen availability was relevant to animal evolution, but these are distinct events separated by nearly two billion years.

**C. The Permian-Triassic Extinction:** This severe mass extinction occurred about 252 million years ago and is associated with massive volcanism and major environmental disruption. It was not the initial persistent accumulation of atmospheric oxygen.

**D. The Late Heavy Bombardment:** The Late Heavy Bombardment was a hypothesized period approximately **4.1-3.8 billion years ago** when the inner solar system experienced intense asteroid and comet impacts. This predates the GOE and is unrelated to cyanobacterial activity. It may have delivered water and organic molecules to early Earth.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Oxygenic photosynthesis predates the Great Oxidation Event; oxygen production and atmospheric accumulation are different milestones
  - Increasing oxygen changed habitats and disadvantaged oxygen-sensitive organisms while supporting wider use of aerobic metabolism
  - Geological evidence for ancient oxygen includes chemical signals in rocks; stromatolite shape alone does not identify its producer or metabolism

>> Bottom Line: The Great Oxidation Event marks persistent atmospheric oxygen accumulation beginning about 2.4 billion years ago. Cyanobacterial photosynthesis supplied oxygen, but accumulation also depended on the balance between oxygen production and consumption.

A researcher wants patient-derived pluripotent stem cells for disease modeling. She introduces four specific transcription factors into the patient's skin fibroblasts to reprogram them. What are these reprogrammed cells called?

  A) Embryonic stem cells (ESCs)
  B) Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)
  C) Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)
  D) Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)

### Correct Answer: Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)

**Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)** are adult somatic cells that have been reprogrammed back to a pluripotent state by the introduction of specific transcription factors. This groundbreaking technique was developed by **Shinya Yamanaka** in 2006 (mouse cells) and 2007 (human cells), earning him the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2012** (shared with John Gurdon).

The classic **Yamanaka factors** are Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc (OSKM). Reprogramming changes gene-expression programs so that the cells can generate derivatives of all three embryonic germ layers. iPSCs retain the donor's genetic background, but genetic changes can be present or arise during their production and culture. They can be made without deriving cells from an embryo.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Embryonic stem cells (ESCs):** ESCs are derived from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst. They are pluripotent, but they are not the cells produced by reprogramming the adult fibroblasts in this stem. Their embryonic origin distinguishes them from iPSCs.

**B. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs):** These cells are generally described as multipotent stromal cells. Commonly demonstrated differentiation pathways include bone, cartilage and fat cells. They are not equivalent to the pluripotent cells produced by the reprogramming procedure described here.

**D. Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs):** HSCs are **multipotent** adult stem cells residing primarily in the bone marrow that give rise to all blood cell lineages (red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets). They are used in bone marrow transplantation for leukemia and other blood disorders. They are not produced by transcription factor reprogramming.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Totipotent cells can contribute to embryonic and supporting extraembryonic lineages; pluripotent cells can generate derivatives of all three embryonic germ layers; multipotent cells have a more restricted range
  - c-Myc can contribute to tumor risk, but genetic changes and residual undifferentiated pluripotent cells are also important safety concerns
  - iPSCs are useful for disease modeling and drug screening; cell-replacement applications require careful differentiation and safety assessment

>> Bottom Line: iPSCs are somatic cells reprogrammed to pluripotency. They provide cells with a patient's genetic background without requiring an embryo, but they still require checks for genetic changes and appropriate differentiation.

A patient with leukemia receives a bone marrow transplant from a matched donor. The transplanted stem cells that will reconstitute all blood cell lineages are classified as which type of stem cell?

  A) Totipotent
  B) Pluripotent
  C) Multipotent
  D) Unipotent

### Correct Answer: Multipotent

**Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)** in bone marrow are **multipotent**: they generate several related cell types within the blood-forming system. HSCs give rise to all blood cell types: red blood cells (erythrocytes), white blood cells (all types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils), and platelets (via megakaryocytes).

Bone marrow transplantation (hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) is a life-saving treatment for hematologic malignancies (leukemia, lymphoma), aplastic anemia, and certain genetic blood disorders (sickle cell disease, thalassemia).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Totipotent:** Totipotency is the capacity to contribute to the embryo and its supporting extraembryonic lineages. The zygote is the classic example. HSCs have a much narrower developmental range and do not form all the tissues needed for an entire organism.

**B. Pluripotent:** Pluripotent cells can form derivatives of all three embryonic germ layers, such as neurons, liver cells and blood cells, but cannot by themselves generate an entire organism with all its supporting extraembryonic lineages. ESCs and iPSCs are examples. HSCs are restricted to the blood-forming system.

**D. Unipotent:** **Unipotent** cells can produce only **one cell type** while still retaining self-renewal capacity. Examples include spermatogonial stem cells (which only produce sperm) and epidermal stem cells of the skin basal layer. HSCs produce multiple different blood cell types, so they are multipotent, not unipotent.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Potency spectrum: Totipotent > Pluripotent > Multipotent > Oligopotent > Unipotent
  - HSCs reside in the bone marrow niche and can be mobilized into peripheral blood with G-CSF for transplant collection
  - Umbilical cord blood is another established source of hematopoietic stem cells for transplantation

>> Bottom Line: Hematopoietic stem cells (bone marrow) = multipotent - they produce all blood cell types but nothing else. Remember the potency hierarchy: totipotent (zygote) → pluripotent (ESCs/iPSCs) → multipotent (HSCs, MSCs) → unipotent (one type only).

Robert Koch established a set of criteria for proving that a specific microorganism causes a specific disease. Which of the following is NOT one of Koch's postulates?

  A) The microorganism must be found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease
  B) The microorganism must be isolated from a diseased organism and grown in pure culture
  C) The cultured microorganism should cause disease when introduced into a healthy organism
  D) The microorganism must be susceptible to at least one antibiotic in vitro

### Correct Answer: The microorganism must be susceptible to at least one antibiotic in vitro

Antibiotic susceptibility is NOT one of Koch's postulates. The classical formulation of **Koch's postulates** is:

  1. The microorganism must be found in all cases of the disease but absent in healthy organisms
  2. The microorganism must be isolated from a diseased host and grown in pure culture
  3. The cultured microorganism must cause the same disease when inoculated into a healthy susceptible host
  4. The microorganism must be re-isolated from the experimentally infected host and shown to be identical to the original organism
Koch developed these postulates while working on Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Bacillus anthracis.

### Why the other choices are wrong (they are actually correct postulates):

**A. The microorganism must be found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease:** This IS Koch's first postulate - establishing an association between the microorganism and the disease. The pathogen must be consistently present in diseased individuals and absent in healthy ones.

**B. The microorganism must be isolated from a diseased organism and grown in pure culture:** This IS Koch's second postulate - the pathogen must be extractable from the host and cultivable independently. This was a methodological breakthrough that allowed systematic study of individual bacterial species.

**C. The cultured microorganism should cause disease when introduced into a healthy organism:** This IS Koch's third postulate - demonstrating causation, not just correlation. The pure culture must reproduce the disease when inoculated into a susceptible host.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The postulates have limitations: some pathogens require specialized culture systems, viruses require host cells, asymptomatic carriers can occur, and deliberately infecting healthy humans may be unethical. Treponema pallidum can be maintained in specialized in-vitro co-culture, although it does not grow in routine bacteriological culture
  - Koch received the Nobel Prize in 1905 for his work on tuberculosis
  - Modern molecular Koch's postulates (proposed by Falkow, 1988) use genetic tools to establish causation

>> Bottom Line: Koch's postulates: (1) find it in all diseased hosts, (2) isolate and culture it, (3) reproduce the disease in a new host, (4) re-isolate and confirm identity. Antibiotic susceptibility is not a postulate.

Carl Woese's three-domain system of classification divides all life into Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Which evidence was primarily used to establish these three domains?

  A) Comparison of cell wall composition
  B) Comparison of metabolic pathways
  C) Comparison of gross morphological features
  D) Comparison of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequences

### Correct Answer: Comparison of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequences

Comparisons of **small-subunit ribosomal RNA** were central to recognizing deep evolutionary relationships among cells. Woese and Fox's 1977 work helped establish Archaea as a distinct lineage. In 1990, Woese, Kandler and Wheelis formally proposed the three-domain classification.

Small-subunit rRNA is useful because the ribosome has an essential role across cellular life and its RNA contains both conserved and variable regions. Prokaryotic small-subunit rRNA is called 16S rRNA, whereas eukaryotic cytosolic small-subunit rRNA is called 18S rRNA. Comparisons can use the RNA sequence or the genes encoding it. The three-domain framework does not mean that all three groups are equally related.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Comparison of cell wall composition:** Cell-envelope chemistry provides useful supporting differences, but it was not the main molecular evidence behind this classification. Most bacterial walls contain peptidoglycan; archaeal envelopes differ, and eukaryotes vary in whether they have a wall and what it contains. These patterns alone do not establish the evolutionary relationships.

**C. Comparison of gross morphological features:** Morphological comparison is insufficient to distinguish Bacteria from Archaea because both are prokaryotic and often look similar under the microscope (rods, cocci, spirals). Morphology is useful for classifying plants and animals but breaks down at the microbial level. Molecular methods were essential because morphological convergence is common in microorganisms.

**B. Comparison of metabolic pathways:** Metabolic traits can help characterize organisms, but they vary within groups and can change through gene loss or horizontal transfer. Methanogenesis is characteristic of certain archaea, not all archaea. Comparative rRNA evidence was central to Woese's work.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The 16S and 18S rRNA molecules are homologous components of the small ribosomal subunit; their names are not names for whole ribosomes
  - Recognition of Archaea as a distinct lineage in 1977 preceded the formal three-domain proposal in 1990
  - rRNA remains useful for identifying and comparing organisms, while modern evolutionary analyses also use many genes and whole genomes

>> Bottom Line: Comparative small-subunit rRNA sequences underpinned Woese's work on deep cellular relationships. Distinguish the 1977 recognition of the archaeal lineage from the formal three-domain proposal in 1990.

  B) "Felis catus" together form the species name; both words capitalized and in bold

In **binomial nomenclature**, established by Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 Species Plantarum and 1758 Systema Naturae, every organism is given a two-part Latin name:

  | Component | Example | Rules |
  | Genus name | Felis | Capitalized, italicized (or underlined if handwritten) |
  | Species epithet (specific epithet) | catus | Lowercase, italicized (or underlined if handwritten) |

The complete binomial Felis catus identifies the species. After first use in a text, the genus can be abbreviated: F. catus.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. "Felis" is the species name; both words should be capitalized and italicized:** Two errors: (1) Felis is the **genus** name, not the species name. (2) Only the genus name is capitalized; the species epithet is always **lowercase**. Writing "Felis Catus" (both capitalized) would be incorrect formatting. The species epithet is never capitalized, even if derived from a proper noun in zoological nomenclature.

**C. "catus" is the species name; both words should be underlined and lowercase:** Two errors: (1) the genus name must always be **capitalized**. Writing "felis catus" (both lowercase) is incorrect. (2) While underlining is an acceptable alternative to italics when handwriting, having both words lowercase is never correct. Additionally, "catus" alone is technically the species epithet - the species is identified by the full binomial.

**B. "Felis catus" together form the species name; both words capitalized and in bold:** While it is correct that the two words together identify the species, capitalizing both words is wrong (the species epithet is always lowercase), and bold formatting is not the convention - **italics** (or underlining) is the standard for scientific names.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Linnaeus introduced this system to create a universal naming convention, replacing inconsistent common names
  - Names are in Latin (or Latinized) to ensure language neutrality across countries
  - The International Codes of Nomenclature (ICN for plants, ICZN for animals) govern naming rules

>> Bottom Line: Binomial nomenclature: Genus (capitalized) + species epithet (lowercase), both in italics. Example: Homo sapiens, Escherichia coli, Felis catus. The genus can be abbreviated after first use (H. sapiens).
