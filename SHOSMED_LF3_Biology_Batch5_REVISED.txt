The parietal cells of the gastric glands secrete which of the following?

  A) Pepsinogen
  B) Gastrin
  C) Mucus and bicarbonate
  D) Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor

### Correct Answer: D) Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor

Parietal (oxyntic) cells produce HCl, which maintains the gastric pH at 1.5–3.5, activates pepsinogen to pepsin, and kills ingested microorganisms. They also secrete intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, mucous cells produce the protective mucus-bicarbonate barrier, and G-cells secrete gastrin.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Pepsinogen:** Pepsinogen is the inactive zymogen form of pepsin, secreted by chief cells in the stomach. It is activated to pepsin by the low pH (1.5-2.0) of gastric acid, but pepsinogen itself is not responsible for producing the acidic environment - that role belongs to parietal cells which secrete HCl.

**B. Gastrin:** Gastrin is secreted by G cells, mainly in the gastric antrum. It stimulates gastric acid secretion, but parietal cells themselves secrete HCl and intrinsic factor.

**C. Mucus and bicarbonate:** Mucus and bicarbonate form the protective gastric mucosal barrier and are produced by mucous neck cells and surface mucous cells of the gastric glands, not by parietal cells. This mucus-bicarbonate layer is essential for protecting the stomach lining from self-digestion by its own acid and pepsin. Disruption of this barrier (e.g., by NSAIDs or H. pylori) can lead to peptic ulcers.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Parietal cells secrete HCl and intrinsic factor.
  - HCl provides an acidic environment for pepsin; intrinsic factor enables vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum.
  - Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, whereas G cells secrete gastrin.

> Bottom Line: Parietal cells produce HCl (for digestion and defense) and intrinsic factor (for vitamin B12 absorption).

Q2  [bio-192]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Which of the following enzymes is secreted by the pancreas in an inactive (zymogen) form and activated in the duodenum?

  A) Pancreatic lipase
  B) Pancreatic amylase
  C) Trypsinogen
  D) Nuclease

### Correct Answer: C) Trypsinogen

Trypsinogen is an inactive protease (zymogen) secreted by the pancreatic acinar cells. It is activated to trypsin by the brush border enzyme enterokinase (enteropeptidase) in the duodenum. Active trypsin then catalyzes the activation of other pancreatic zymogens including chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, and procarboxypeptidase. Pancreatic lipase and amylase are secreted in their active forms and do not require activation.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Pancreatic lipase:** Pancreatic lipase is secreted in an active form and does not require activation in the duodenum. It digests triglycerides, working together with bile salts and colipase.

**B. Pancreatic amylase:** Pancreatic amylase, like salivary amylase, is secreted in its active form and does not require activation. It continues the digestion of starch that began in the mouth, breaking alpha-1,4-glycosidic bonds to produce maltose, maltotriose, and alpha-limit dextrins. It is secreted in an active form, so unlike trypsinogen it needs no activation step in the duodenum.

**D. Nuclease:** Pancreatic nucleases (DNase and RNase) digest dietary nucleic acids into nucleotides. Like lipase and amylase, they are secreted in active form. Trypsinogen is the one in this list that requires activation after it reaches the duodenum.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A zymogen is an inactive enzyme precursor; this helps prevent premature digestive activity.
  - Brush-border enteropeptidase converts trypsinogen to trypsin in the small intestine.
  - Trypsin activates additional trypsinogen and several other pancreatic zymogens.

> Bottom Line: Trypsinogen is activated by enterokinase; active trypsin then activates other pancreatic zymogens in a cascade.

Which region of the large intestine is the main site of water and electrolyte absorption?

  A) Rectum
  B) Sigmoid colon
  C) Transverse colon
  D) Proximal colon, particularly the cecum and ascending colon

### Correct Answer: D) Proximal colon, particularly the cecum and ascending colon

Most water entering the digestive tract is absorbed in the small intestine. The large intestine recovers much of the remaining water and electrolytes, especially in its proximal region. Sodium absorption helps create an osmotic gradient, so water follows into the body. This progressively changes liquid intestinal contents into more solid feces.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Rectum:** The rectum mainly stores feces before defecation. It is not the main region for colonic water absorption.

**B. Sigmoid colon:** The sigmoid colon helps store and propel feces. Much of the colonic water absorption has already occurred upstream.

**C. Transverse colon:** The transverse colon also absorbs water and electrolytes, but the proximal colon is the main absorptive region emphasized here.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The small intestine absorbs most gastrointestinal water.
  - The proximal colon absorbs much of the water and electrolytes remaining in intestinal contents.
  - Water follows osmotic gradients created by solute absorption; the distal bowel increasingly serves storage and propulsion functions.

> Bottom Line: The proximal colon absorbs much of the water that reaches the large intestine; most gastrointestinal water absorption has already occurred in the small intestine.

==============================================================================
Secretin, released from S-cells of the duodenum, primarily stimulates the pancreas to secrete:

  A) Enzyme-rich juice
  B) Bile salts
  C) Insulin
  D) Bicarbonate-rich fluid

### Correct Answer: D) Bicarbonate-rich fluid

Secretin is released when acidic chyme (pH < 4.5) enters the duodenum from the stomach. It stimulates pancreatic duct cells to secrete a watery, bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralizes acidic chyme and raises duodenal pH toward a range suitable for pancreatic enzymes. CCK, rather than secretin, is the primary stimulus for enzyme-rich pancreatic juice.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Enzyme-rich juice:** Enzyme-rich pancreatic juice is primarily stimulated by cholecystokinin (CCK), not secretin. CCK is released from I-cells of the duodenum in response to fats and proteins, and it acts on pancreatic acinar cells to release a concentrated mixture of digestive enzymes including trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, pancreatic lipase, and amylase.

**B. Bile salts:** Bile salts are synthesized by hepatocytes in the liver from cholesterol and stored in the gallbladder. Their release into the duodenum is stimulated by CCK (which causes gallbladder contraction), not by secretin. Secretin does mildly stimulate bile flow from the liver (choleresis), but this is a bicarbonate-rich watery bile, not the bile salt-rich concentrated bile from the gallbladder.

**C. Insulin:** Insulin is a peptide hormone produced by β cells of the pancreatic islets (endocrine pancreas), not by the exocrine pancreas. Its release is primarily stimulated by elevated blood glucose, certain amino acids, and incretin hormones (GLP-1, GIP). Secretin acts on the exocrine portion of the pancreas (duct cells), which is functionally separate from the hormone-producing islets.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Acidic chyme stimulates duodenal S cells to release secretin.
  - Secretin stimulates pancreatic duct cells to secrete bicarbonate-rich fluid.
  - Secretin mainly promotes bicarbonate secretion; CCK mainly promotes pancreatic enzyme secretion and gallbladder contraction.

> Bottom Line: Secretin promotes bicarbonate secretion to neutralize gastric acid in the duodenum; CCK promotes enzyme secretion.

==============================================================================
The hepatic portal vein carries nutrient-rich blood from the gastrointestinal tract directly to which organ?

  A) Heart
  B) Kidneys
  C) Liver
  D) Spleen

### Correct Answer: C) Liver

The hepatic portal vein carries venous blood from the digestive organs to the liver, including most newly absorbed water-soluble nutrients. Most long-chain dietary fats initially travel through lymph in chylomicrons instead. The vein collects blood from the stomach, intestines, spleen and pancreas and delivers it to the liver for processing. In the liver, nutrients are metabolized, stored, or released into the systemic circulation. The liver also detoxifies absorbed substances and drugs (first-pass metabolism). This portal circulation ensures that absorbed nutrients are processed before reaching the general circulation.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Heart:** The heart receives blood from the systemic and pulmonary circulations through the superior/inferior vena cava and pulmonary veins. It does not directly receive nutrient-rich blood from the GI tract. Blood from the liver eventually reaches the heart via the hepatic veins draining into the inferior vena cava, but only after hepatic processing.

**B. Kidneys:** The kidneys receive blood from the renal arteries (branches of the abdominal aorta) for filtration and waste removal. Approximately 20-25% of cardiac output flows through the kidneys, but this is arterial blood from the systemic circulation, not the nutrient-rich venous blood from the GI tract.

**D. Spleen:** The spleen actually drains into the hepatic portal system via the splenic vein, which joins the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein. So the spleen is a source of blood entering the portal system, not its destination. The spleen filters blood, recycles old red blood cells, and serves as a reservoir for immune cells.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Portal blood carries most absorbed water-soluble nutrients from the intestine to the liver for processing.
  - Blood leaves the liver through hepatic veins and then enters the inferior vena cava.
  - Most dietary long-chain fats initially enter lymph rather than the hepatic portal vein.

> Bottom Line: The hepatic portal system directs absorbed nutrients to the liver for processing before entering systemic circulation.

Q6  [bio-196]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Gas exchange in the lungs occurs primarily across the walls of which structures?

  A) Bronchi
  B) Bronchioles
  C) Alveoli
  D) Trachea

### Correct Answer: C) Alveoli

Alveoli are the tiny, thin-walled air sacs (approximately 300 million in both lungs) where gas exchange occurs. The respiratory membrane consists of the alveolar epithelium (type I pneumocytes), a fused basement membrane, and the capillary endothelium-together only about 0.5 μm thick. Oxygen diffuses from the alveolar air into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveolar air, driven by partial pressure gradients.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Bronchi:** The bronchi are the large airways that branch off the trachea and conduct air into the lungs. They are lined with ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium and have cartilaginous support. Bronchi are part of the conducting zone of the respiratory system, meaning they transport air but do not participate in gas exchange. Their walls are too thick for diffusion.

**B. Bronchioles:** Bronchioles are smaller airways that lack cartilage and are primarily surrounded by smooth muscle. They include terminal bronchioles (last part of the conducting zone) and respiratory bronchioles (which begin the respiratory zone and have a few alveoli budding from their walls). While some gas exchange may begin at respiratory bronchioles, the vast majority occurs at the alveoli.

**D. Trachea:** The trachea (windpipe) is the main airway connecting the larynx to the bronchi, reinforced by C-shaped cartilage rings. It functions solely as an air conduit and is lined with ciliated epithelium that traps and sweeps particles upward (mucociliary escalator). No gas exchange occurs here because its walls are far too thick.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Gas exchange takes place in the alveoli, not in the airways that lead to them.
  - The conducting zone runs from the trachea through the bronchi to the terminal bronchioles: it moves air but its walls are too thick for exchange, which is why it forms the anatomical dead space. Respiratory bronchioles belong to the respiratory zone and carry a few alveoli in their walls.
  - The respiratory membrane is only about 0.5 µm thick - type I pneumocyte, fused basement membrane, and capillary endothelium.
  - An enormous surface area (roughly 70 m² across some 300 million alveoli) combined with that thin barrier is what makes diffusion efficient.
  - Exchange is entirely passive, driven by partial pressure gradients; no energy is spent moving the gases themselves.

> Bottom Line: Alveoli provide an enormous surface area (~70 m²) and thin barrier for efficient gas exchange by diffusion.

Q7  [bio-197]  Answer: D
==============================================================================
Surfactant produced by type II pneumocytes in the alveoli serves primarily to:

  A) Increase the surface tension of the alveolar fluid
  B) Destroy inhaled pathogens
  C) Transport oxygen across the respiratory membrane
  D) Reduce surface tension to prevent alveolar collapse

### Correct Answer: D) Reduce surface tension to prevent alveolar collapse

Pulmonary surfactant is a phospholipid-rich substance (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) produced by type II alveolar cells. It reduces the surface tension of the thin fluid lining the alveoli, preventing them from collapsing (atelectasis) during expiration, especially in smaller alveoli. Surfactant also increases lung compliance, making it easier to inflate the lungs. Premature infants who lack sufficient surfactant develop respiratory distress syndrome.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Increase the surface tension of the alveolar fluid:** Increasing surface tension would make the alveoli more prone to collapse, which is the opposite of what surfactant does. Surface tension is caused by water molecules at the air-liquid interface pulling inward. Without surfactant, the surface tension in small alveoli would be high enough (according to the Law of Laplace) to cause them to collapse into larger alveoli.

**B. Destroy inhaled pathogens:** Destroying inhaled pathogens is primarily the function of alveolar macrophages (dust cells), not surfactant. Alveolar macrophages patrol the alveolar surface, phagocytosing bacteria, dust particles, and other debris. Surfactant proteins SP-A and SP-D do have some innate immune roles (opsonization), but the primary function of surfactant is reducing surface tension.

**C. Transport oxygen across the respiratory membrane:** Oxygen transport across the respiratory membrane occurs by simple passive diffusion driven by partial pressure gradients. No carrier molecules or active transport mechanisms are needed. Oxygen dissolves in the thin fluid layer lining the alveolus and diffuses through the alveolar epithelium, basement membrane, and capillary endothelium into the blood.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Surfactant lowers the surface tension of the thin fluid layer lining the alveoli.
  - Surface tension tends to pull an alveolus closed, so reducing it prevents collapse (atelectasis) and lowers the work of breathing.
  - For the same surface tension, a smaller alveolus has a greater collapsing pressure. That is the assumption behind the simplified Laplace comparison, and it is why surfactant matters most in small alveoli and stops them emptying into larger ones.
  - It is produced by type II pneumocytes and is largely dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, a phospholipid.
  - Surfactant production increases as the lungs mature; premature infants are at increased risk of surfactant deficiency and respiratory distress syndrome.

> Bottom Line: Surfactant reduces alveolar surface tension, preventing collapse and reducing the work of breathing.

Most carbon dioxide in the blood is transported in which form?

  A) Dissolved in plasma as CO₂ gas
  B) Bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin
  C) As bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) in plasma
  D) Bound to albumin in plasma

### Correct Answer: C) As bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) in plasma

Approximately 70% of CO₂ is transported as bicarbonate ions. Inside red blood cells, the enzyme carbonic anhydrase catalyzes the reaction: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. The bicarbonate is then exchanged for chloride ions (chloride shift) and transported in plasma. About 23% of CO₂ binds to hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin), and only 7% is dissolved directly in plasma.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Dissolved in plasma as CO₂ gas:** Only about 7% of CO2 is transported dissolved in plasma. CO2 is more soluble in blood than O2, but this still represents a small fraction of total transport. The partial pressure of dissolved CO2 is what is measured by arterial blood gas analysis (PaCO2, normally ~40 mmHg) and is the key regulator of respiratory drive.

**B. Bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin:** About 23% of CO2 is transported bound to the amino groups of hemoglobin (not to the heme group where O2 binds) forming carbaminohemoglobin. Deoxygenated hemoglobin binds CO2 more readily than oxygenated hemoglobin (the Haldane effect), which facilitates CO2 loading in the tissues and unloading in the lungs.

**D. Bound to albumin in plasma:** Albumin does not play a significant role in CO2 transport. While small amounts of CO2 can form carbamino compounds with any plasma protein, this contribution is negligible. Albumin's primary roles are maintaining plasma oncotic pressure and transporting various substances (fatty acids, hormones, drugs) through the blood.

### Key Educational Points:

  - About 70% of CO₂ travels as bicarbonate, roughly 23% bound to haemoglobin as carbamino compounds, and only about 7% dissolved in plasma.
  - Carbonic anhydrase inside red cells converts CO₂ and water into carbonic acid, which dissociates into H⁺ and HCO₃⁻.
  - The bicarbonate leaves the red cell in exchange for chloride - the chloride shift - which keeps the cell electrically balanced.
  - The H⁺ generated is buffered by haemoglobin, which is why CO₂ transport and blood pH are so tightly linked.
  - Note that CO₂ binds to amino groups on the globin chains, not to the haem iron where oxygen binds - so the two gases do not directly compete for the same site.

> Bottom Line: Most CO₂ is transported as bicarbonate (70%), followed by carbaminohemoglobin (23%) and dissolved CO₂ (7%).

The Hering-Breuer reflex prevents overinflation of the lungs. Which receptors mediate this reflex?

  A) Chemoreceptors in the carotid body
  B) Irritant receptors in the tracheal epithelium
  C) Baroreceptors in the aortic arch
  D) Stretch receptors in the bronchial smooth muscle

### Correct Answer: D) Stretch receptors in the bronchial smooth muscle

The Hering-Breuer inflation reflex is triggered by pulmonary stretch receptors located in the smooth muscle of the bronchi and bronchioles. When the lungs are sufficiently inflated, these mechanoreceptors send inhibitory signals via the vagus nerve to the inspiratory center in the medulla, terminating inspiration and initiating expiration. This reflex prevents excessive lung inflation and contributes to the rhythmic pattern of breathing.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Chemoreceptors in the carotid body:** Chemoreceptors in the carotid body (and aortic body) detect changes in blood PaO2, PaCO2, and pH. They are peripheral chemoreceptors that primarily respond to severe hypoxemia (PaO2 < 60 mmHg). When activated, they stimulate the medullary respiratory center to increase ventilation. They are not involved in the Hering-Breuer inflation reflex.

**B. Irritant receptors in the tracheal epithelium:** Irritant receptors in the tracheal and bronchial epithelium respond to inhaled particles, noxious gases, and mucus accumulation. Their activation triggers protective reflexes such as coughing, bronchoconstriction, and increased mucus secretion. They are rapidly adapting receptors and can respond to mechanical changes, including inflation. They are not, however, the receptor group principally responsible for this reflex: the Hering-Breuer inflation reflex is mediated mainly by slowly adapting pulmonary stretch receptors.

**C. Baroreceptors in the aortic arch:** Baroreceptors in the aortic arch (and carotid sinus) detect changes in arterial blood pressure. When blood pressure rises, they increase their firing rate and signal the medulla to reduce sympathetic output and increase parasympathetic output, lowering heart rate and blood pressure. They regulate cardiovascular function, not respiratory inflation.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The Hering-Breuer inflation reflex is triggered by pulmonary stretch receptors in the smooth muscle of the bronchi and bronchioles.
  - When the lungs inflate sufficiently these receptors signal along the vagus nerve to inhibit the medullary inspiratory centre, ending inspiration.
  - It is protective against overinflation, and is most prominent in newborns and at large tidal volumes rather than during quiet adult breathing.
  - These are mechanoreceptors responding to stretch - do not confuse them with chemoreceptors, which sense CO₂, O₂ and pH.
  - Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense blood pressure and belong to cardiovascular control, not to this reflex.

> Bottom Line: Pulmonary stretch receptors trigger the Hering-Breuer reflex via the vagus nerve to prevent lung overinflation.

Q10  [bio-200]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
Which change produces a rightward shift of the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve through the Bohr effect?

  A) Decreased blood temperature
  B) Increased pH (alkalosis)
  C) Increased CO₂ concentration and decreased pH
  D) Decreased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) levels

### Correct Answer: C) Increased CO₂ concentration and decreased pH

Higher CO₂ and lower pH reduce hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, promoting oxygen unloading. This is the Bohr effect. A right shift means hemoglobin releases O₂ more readily to metabolically active tissues, which matters during exercise when tissues produce more CO₂ and become more acidic.

Increased temperature and increased red-cell 2,3-BPG can also shift the curve right, but through separate mechanisms. The Bohr effect itself concerns the influence of CO₂ and H⁺ on hemoglobin's oxygen affinity.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Decreased blood temperature:** Decreased blood temperature shifts the curve to the left, so hemoglobin holds oxygen more tightly. Temperature is a separate influence from the Bohr effect, which concerns CO₂ and H⁺, and in any case this option moves the curve the wrong way.

**B. Increased pH (alkalosis):** Increased pH (alkalosis) also shifts the curve to the left, increasing hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen and reducing oxygen release to tissues. In respiratory alkalosis the reduced CO₂ and raised pH both favour tighter oxygen binding, which is a leftward shift rather than the rightward one the question asks about.

**D. Decreased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) levels:** Decreased 2,3-BPG (2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, also called 2,3-DPG) shifts the curve to the left. 2,3-BPG is produced by red blood cells during glycolysis and binds to deoxyhemoglobin, stabilizing its low-affinity state. Conditions that decrease 2,3-BPG (such as stored blood in transfusions) result in hemoglobin that does not release oxygen efficiently to tissues.

### Key Educational Points:

  - A right shift of the oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve means lower affinity, so haemoglobin gives up oxygen more readily.
  - Rising CO₂ and falling pH shift the curve right: that is the Bohr effect. Rising temperature and rising 2,3-BPG also shift it right, but through separate mechanisms.
  - These are precisely the conditions found in actively metabolising tissue, so the shift automatically delivers more oxygen exactly where it is needed.
  - The opposite conditions - alkalosis, cold, low 2,3-BPG - shift the curve left and make haemoglobin hold oxygen more tightly.
  - The curve is sigmoidal because oxygen binding is cooperative, which is why these shifts produce large changes in delivery over the steep middle portion.

> Bottom Line: The Bohr effect links increased CO₂ and decreased pH to reduced hemoglobin oxygen affinity and easier oxygen unloading.

Q11  [bio-201]  Answer: C
==============================================================================
The volume of air remaining in the lungs after a maximal forced expiration is called the:

  A) Tidal volume
  B) Expiratory reserve volume
  C) Residual volume
  D) Functional residual capacity

### Correct Answer: C) Residual volume

Residual volume (RV) is the air that cannot be expelled even with maximum effort, approximately 1200 mL in a healthy adult, though values vary with body size, sex, age and disease. The air left in the lungs after an ordinary quiet breath is the functional residual capacity, and that is the reservoir which keeps gas exchange going between breaths. Alveolar stability itself depends on surfactant and on transpulmonary pressure rather than on a measured volume. RV cannot be measured by simple spirometry; it requires helium dilution or body plethysmography. Functional residual capacity = expiratory reserve volume + residual volume.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Tidal volume:** Tidal volume (TV) is the volume of air inhaled or exhaled during normal quiet breathing, approximately 500 mL. It represents the default breathing volume and does not involve maximal effort. Minute ventilation is calculated as tidal volume multiplied by respiratory rate (e.g., 500 mL x 12 breaths/min = 6 L/min).

**B. Expiratory reserve volume:** Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) is the additional air that can be forcefully exhaled after a normal passive exhalation, approximately 1200 mL. After a normal exhalation, you can still actively contract your abdominal and internal intercostal muscles to push out more air. However, even after this maximal effort, residual volume remains in the lungs.

**D. Functional residual capacity:** Functional residual capacity (FRC) is the volume of air remaining in the lungs after a normal passive exhalation. It equals ERV + RV (approximately 2400 mL). FRC is important because it provides a reservoir of air in the lungs that prevents large fluctuations in blood oxygen levels between breaths.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Residual volume is the air remaining after the most forceful expiration possible, about 1200 mL in a healthy adult.
  - Since it cannot be exhaled, spirometry cannot measure it - helium dilution or body plethysmography is needed.
  - It follows that any capacity including RV also cannot be measured by spirometry, which covers functional residual capacity and total lung capacity.
  - Functional residual capacity = ERV + RV, the volume left in the lungs at the end of a normal quiet breath.
  - Functional residual capacity, the air left after an ordinary quiet breath, is the reservoir that keeps gas exchange going between breaths.

> Bottom Line: Residual volume is the air remaining after maximal expiration and cannot be measured by simple spirometry.

Under normal physiological conditions, a rise in arterial CO₂ stimulates ventilation mainly through which mechanism?

  A) Low blood oxygen levels detected by peripheral chemoreceptors
  B) Low blood pressure detected by baroreceptors
  C) High blood pH detected by the carotid body
  D) An increase in H⁺ near central chemoreceptors in the medulla

### Correct Answer: D) An increase in H⁺ near central chemoreceptors in the medulla

CO₂ crosses the blood-brain barrier and contributes to H⁺ formation in brain extracellular fluid and cerebrospinal fluid. Central chemoreceptors respond to this fall in pH and stimulate an increase in ventilation, helping remove excess CO₂. Brainstem networks generate the breathing rhythm, while chemical feedback adjusts its rate and depth.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Low blood oxygen levels detected by peripheral chemoreceptors:** Peripheral chemoreceptors respond to arterial oxygen, CO₂ and pH. Their response to low oxygen becomes especially strong when arterial PO₂ falls substantially, but the mechanism asked here is the central response to increased CO₂.

**B. Low blood pressure detected by baroreceptors:** Baroreceptors detect changes in blood pressure, not blood gases. They are located in the carotid sinus and aortic arch and regulate cardiovascular function through the autonomic nervous system. While extreme hypotension can indirectly affect breathing, baroreceptors are not chemosensory and do not directly drive respiratory rhythm.

**C. High blood pH detected by the carotid body:** The carotid body detects low PaO2, high PaCO2, and low pH (not high pH). High blood pH (alkalosis) actually reduces ventilatory drive because fewer H+ ions are available to stimulate chemoreceptors. This is why hyperventilation (which causes respiratory alkalosis) can temporarily suppress the urge to breathe.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Brainstem networks generate the breathing rhythm; chemical feedback adjusts its rate and depth rather than creating it.
  - Central chemoreceptors in the medulla detect H⁺, not CO₂ directly. CO₂ crosses the blood-brain barrier freely and forms H⁺ on the other side.
  - H⁺ itself crosses the barrier poorly, which is why arterial CO₂ rather than arterial pH is the effective stimulus for the central response.
  - The system is sensitive: a small rise in arterial PCO₂ produces a large increase in ventilation.
  - Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies sense arterial oxygen, CO₂ and pH, and their oxygen response becomes especially strong when PO₂ falls substantially.

> Bottom Line: A rise in arterial CO₂ raises H⁺ near the central chemoreceptors, and that is the main chemical signal increasing ventilation under normal conditions.

During normal quiet breathing, which of the following muscles is the primary muscle of inspiration?

  A) External intercostals
  B) Internal intercostals
  C) Diaphragm
  D) Abdominal muscles

### Correct Answer: C) Diaphragm

The diaphragm is the most important muscle of inspiration, responsible for approximately 75% of the air entering the lungs during quiet breathing. When it contracts, it moves inferiorly and flattens, increasing the vertical dimension of the thoracic cavity. This decreases intrapleural and intrapulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure, drawing air in. The external intercostals assist by elevating the ribs. Expiration during quiet breathing is passive, relying on elastic recoil of the lungs.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. External intercostals:** The external intercostals assist quiet inspiration by elevating the ribs and widening the thorax, but the diaphragm accounts for most of the volume change.

**B. Internal intercostals:** The internal intercostal muscles are primarily muscles of forced expiration. When they contract, they pull the ribs downward and inward, reducing the thoracic volume and forcing air out of the lungs. They are recruited during active expiration such as coughing, sneezing, or vigorous exercise. During quiet breathing, expiration is passive and does not require their contraction.

**D. Abdominal muscles:** The abdominal muscles (rectus abdominis, external and internal obliques, transversus abdominis) are accessory muscles of expiration. When they contract, they increase intra-abdominal pressure, pushing the diaphragm upward and forcefully expelling air. They are used during active expiration, Valsalva maneuver, coughing, and core stabilization, but not during quiet inspiration.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The diaphragm is the primary muscle of inspiration, responsible for roughly three-quarters of quiet tidal breathing.
  - Its contraction flattens the dome and increases the vertical dimension of the thorax, dropping intrapulmonary pressure below atmospheric so air flows in.
  - The external intercostals assist quiet inspiration by elevating the ribs; the diaphragm remains the primary inspiratory muscle.
  - Quiet expiration is passive, powered by elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall rather than by muscle contraction.
  - Forced expiration is active and recruits the abdominal muscles and internal intercostals.

> Bottom Line: The diaphragm is the primary inspiratory muscle; quiet expiration is passive due to lung elastic recoil.

Q14  [bio-204]  Answer: D
==============================================================================
Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning is dangerous primarily because CO:

  A) Destroys red blood cells, causing hemolytic anemia
  B) Directly inhibits the respiratory center in the medulla
  C) Stimulates excessive breathing leading to respiratory alkalosis
  D) Binds to hemoglobin with approximately 200–250 times greater affinity than oxygen

### Correct Answer: D) Binds to hemoglobin with approximately 200–250 times greater affinity than oxygen

Carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin's iron-containing heme groups at the same site as oxygen but with about 200–250 times greater affinity, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). This prevents oxygen binding and also causes the remaining oxygen-bound subunits to hold onto their oxygen more tightly (left-shifting the dissociation curve), severely impairing oxygen delivery to tissues. Arterial PO₂ may remain normal, because it measures dissolved oxygen rather than how much oxygen hemoglobin can carry. Oxygen delivery can therefore be severely impaired despite a normal PO₂. A conventional pulse oximeter may also give a misleadingly reassuring reading, because optically it cannot reliably distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin. Breathlessness or rapid breathing can occur.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**D. Contraction of the extraocular muscles to converge the eyes only:** Convergence of the eyes through extraocular muscles is one component of the near response, but it is not accommodation itself. Convergence allows both eyes to fixate on the same near object, preventing double vision. True accommodation specifically refers to the change in lens shape that adjusts focusing power, mediated by the ciliary muscle and zonular fibers.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Near accommodation is a chain: ciliary muscle contracts, zonules relax, lens rounds, refractive power increases.
  - The counterintuitive step is that muscle contraction releases tension on the lens rather than increasing it.
  - Innervation is parasympathetic via the oculomotor nerve (CN III).
  - The near reflex triad combines accommodation with convergence and pupillary constriction.
  - Presbyopia results from progressive loss of lens elasticity with age, not from ciliary muscle failure.

> Bottom Line: Near accommodation requires ciliary muscle contraction → zonule relaxation → lens rounding → increased refractive power.

  B) Rotational (angular) acceleration
  C) Sound vibrations
  D) Changes in atmospheric pressure

The photopigment rhodopsin found in rod cells consists of which protein and which chromophore?

  A) Opsin and retinal (a derivative of vitamin A)
  B) Melanopsin and retinal
  C) Opsin and beta-carotene
  D) Transducin and cGMP

### Correct Answer: A) Opsin and retinal (a derivative of vitamin A)

Rhodopsin is the visual pigment in rod cells, composed of the protein opsin bound to the chromophore 11-cis-retinal (derived from vitamin A). When light strikes rhodopsin, 11-cis-retinal isomerizes to all-trans-retinal, activating opsin and triggering a phototransduction cascade involving the G-protein transducin. This leads to decreased cGMP and closure of cGMP-gated cation channels that carry Na⁺ and Ca²⁺; the resulting fall in inward current hyperpolarizes the rod cell. Vitamin A deficiency impairs rhodopsin regeneration, causing night blindness.

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Melanopsin and retinal:** Melanopsin is a photopigment found in intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs), not in rod cells. These specialized ganglion cells detect ambient light levels and are critical for non-image-forming visual functions: regulating circadian rhythms (entraining the suprachiasmatic nucleus), controlling the pupillary light reflex, and suppressing melatonin production. Melanopsin does use retinal as its chromophore but is structurally distinct from rhodopsin.

**C. Opsin and beta-carotene:** Beta-carotene is a provitamin A carotenoid found in orange and yellow vegetables. In the body, it is cleaved to form retinal (vitamin A aldehyde), which is the actual chromophore in rhodopsin. Beta-carotene itself is not directly incorporated into rhodopsin; it must first be converted to retinal by the enzyme beta-carotene 15,15'-dioxygenase in the intestinal mucosa.

**D. Transducin and cGMP:** Transducin is a G-protein (not a photopigment) that is activated by photoexcited rhodopsin during the phototransduction cascade. Activated transducin stimulates phosphodiesterase, which hydrolyzes cGMP, leading to closure of cGMP-gated cation channels and rod cell hyperpolarization. Transducin and cGMP are signaling molecules downstream of rhodopsin, not components of the pigment itself.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Rhodopsin is opsin (the protein) bound to 11-cis-retinal (the chromophore, derived from vitamin A).
  - Light isomerises 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal, which activates opsin.
  - The cascade runs through transducin, lowering cGMP and closing cGMP-gated cation channels that carry Na⁺ and Ca²⁺.
  - Rod cells therefore hyperpolarise in response to light - the reverse of most sensory receptors.
  - Vitamin A deficiency impairs rhodopsin regeneration, which is the mechanism of night blindness.

> Bottom Line: Rhodopsin (opsin + 11-cis-retinal) is the rod photopigment; vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness.

Which hormone surge triggers ovulation approximately mid-cycle in the menstrual cycle?

  B) Luteinizing hormone (LH)
  C) Progesterone

### Key Educational Points:

  - Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K are absorbed with dietary fat, carried in chylomicrons, and can be stored in liver and adipose tissue.
  - Because they are stored, they need not be consumed daily - but they can accumulate to harmful levels.
  - Storage duration differs between them: vitamin K has relatively small stores and rapid turnover.
  - Hypervitaminosis is most important for A and D.
  - Many water-soluble vitamins have smaller stores and excess is more readily excreted, but B12 stores last years and excessive doses of some water-soluble vitamins can be harmful.
  - Any condition causing fat malabsorption - cholestasis, pancreatic insufficiency, coeliac disease - risks deficiency of all four at once.

> Bottom Line: Fat-soluble vitamins can be stored and excessive intake can be harmful, especially for A and D. Water solubility does not guarantee an absence of storage or toxicity.

Night blindness (nyctalopia), dry skin, and impaired immune function are characteristic features of a deficiency of which vitamin?

  A) Vitamin A (retinol)
  B) Vitamin E (tocopherol)
  C) Vitamin B1 (thiamine)
  D) Vitamin B3 (niacin)

### Correct Answer: A) Vitamin A (retinol)

Vitamin A is essential for the synthesis of rhodopsin (visual pigment in rod cells), maintenance of epithelial tissue integrity, and proper immune function. Its deficiency leads to night blindness (impaired rod cell function), xerophthalmia (dry eyes, potentially progressing to corneal damage and blindness), dry and keratinized skin, and increased susceptibility to infections. Vitamin A is a fat-soluble vitamin found in animal products (as retinol) and in plant sources (as beta-carotene, a provitamin).

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**B. Vitamin E (tocopherol):** Vitamin E (tocopherol) is the body's primary fat-soluble antioxidant, protecting cell membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidative damage by free radicals. Vitamin E deficiency typically produces neurological or red-cell problems, such as ataxia, peripheral neuropathy and haemolysis; the combination described here is much more characteristic of vitamin A deficiency.

**C. Vitamin B1 (thiamine):** Vitamin B1 (thiamine) serves as a cofactor for key metabolic enzymes: pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and transketolase. Deficiency causes beriberi (wet beriberi with heart failure; dry beriberi with peripheral neuropathy) and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, which can arise whenever thiamine intake or absorption is inadequate. That syndrome pattern is quite different from the night blindness, dry skin and immune impairment of vitamin A deficiency.

**D. Vitamin B3 (niacin):** Vitamin B3 (niacin) is a precursor of NAD+ and NADP+, essential coenzymes in hundreds of redox reactions including glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Deficiency causes pellagra, remembered by the "3 Ds": dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia (a 4th D, death, follows if untreated).

### Key Educational Points:

  - Vitamin A is required for rhodopsin synthesis in rod cells, and night blindness is a common early sign of deficiency.
  - It also maintains epithelial integrity, and its lack produces dry, keratinised skin and xerophthalmia.
  - Progressive xerophthalmia can destroy the cornea and is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide.
  - Vitamin A supports immune function, partly by maintaining mucosal barriers.
  - It comes preformed as retinol from animal sources and as the provitamin beta-carotene from plants; excessive intake of preformed vitamin A can be toxic and teratogenic.

> Bottom Line: Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness, xerophthalmia, skin changes, and immune dysfunction.

A mutation prevents newly synthesized secretory proteins from leaving the rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Which process would be most directly impaired?

  A) Synthesis of cytoplasmic proteins
  B) Golgi-dependent processing and sorting of newly synthesized secretory proteins
  C) Degradation of misfolded cytoplasmic proteins by proteasomes
  D) Replication of mitochondrial DNA

### Correct Answer: B) Golgi-dependent processing and sorting of newly synthesized secretory proteins

Proteins traveling through the classical secretory pathway enter the rough ER, where folding and initial N-linked glycosylation occur. They then travel to the Golgi apparatus for further processing and sorting. If newly synthesized secretory proteins cannot leave the ER, they cannot reach the Golgi for these later steps. This does not mean that every form of protein glycosylation stops.

  |  | Organelle |  | Function | 
  |  | Rough ER |  | Initial folding and N-linked glycosylation of proteins entering the secretory pathway | 
  |  | Golgi apparatus |  | Further processing and sorting for delivery | 
  |  | Proteasome |  | Degradation of selected proteins in the cytosol and nucleus | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Synthesis of cytoplasmic proteins:** Many cytoplasmic proteins are synthesized on free ribosomes and do not need to travel from the ER to the Golgi. Their synthesis is not the transport-dependent step asked about.

**C. Degradation of misfolded cytoplasmic proteins by proteasomes:** Proteasomes degrade selected proteins, including misfolded cytoplasmic proteins. That degradation is a different process from delivery of secretory proteins to the Golgi.

**D. Replication of mitochondrial DNA:** Mitochondrial DNA is replicated within mitochondria. Its replication is not a step in the ER-to-Golgi secretory pathway.

### Key Educational Points:

  - The classical secretory pathway carries proteins from the rough ER to the Golgi and then toward their destinations.
  - Folding and initial N-linked glycosylation begin in the ER.
  - The Golgi performs additional processing and sorts proteins for delivery.
  - Blocking ER export directly prevents newly synthesized cargo from reaching these Golgi steps.
  - Other cellular processes may be affected indirectly by severe transport disruption, but they are not the direct step tested here.

> Bottom Line: Secretory proteins must leave the ER and reach the Golgi for normal Golgi-dependent processing and sorting.

Which organelle contains the enzyme catalase and is primarily involved in the breakdown of very long-chain fatty acids and detoxification of hydrogen peroxide?

  A) Lysosome
  B) Smooth endoplasmic reticulum
  C) Peroxisome
  D) Mitochondrion

### Correct Answer: Peroxisome

Peroxisomes contain catalase, which converts hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) into water and oxygen. They are also involved in β-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids (>22 carbons), which mitochondria cannot process directly.

  |  | Organelle |  | Key Enzymes/Functions | 
  |  | Peroxisome |  | Catalase, oxidases; fatty acid β-oxidation, H₂O₂ detoxification | 
  |  | Lysosome |  | Acid hydrolases; intracellular digestion | 
  |  | Smooth ER |  | Lipid synthesis, drug detoxification (cytochrome P450) | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Lysosome:** Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles filled with acid hydrolases (lipases, proteases, nucleases) that function optimally at pH ~5. They digest material brought in by endocytosis, phagocytosis, or autophagy. While lysosomes are involved in intracellular digestion, they do not contain catalase and are not specialized for fatty acid β-oxidation or H_2O_2 detoxification.

**B. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum:** The smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER) is involved in lipid and steroid synthesis, drug detoxification via cytochrome P450 enzymes, calcium storage, and carbohydrate metabolism. While the SER does perform detoxification, it uses different enzymes (cytochrome P450 oxidases) and does not contain catalase or specialize in very long-chain fatty acid breakdown.

**D. Mitochondrion:** Mitochondria are the primary site of aerobic ATP production through the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. They also perform β-oxidation of most fatty acids, but they cannot process very long-chain fatty acids (>22 carbons). Those must first be shortened in peroxisomes before mitochondria can complete their oxidation.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Peroxisomes contain catalase, which breaks hydrogen peroxide down into water and oxygen.
  - They also carry out beta-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids, which mitochondria cannot shorten directly.
  - Their own oxidase reactions generate the H₂O₂, so peroxide is produced and destroyed within the same compartment.
  - Do not confuse them with lysosomes, which use acid hydrolases at low pH to digest material, or with smooth ER, which handles lipid synthesis and drug detoxification.
  - Peroxisomal failure causes disorders such as Zellweger syndrome and X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, in which very long-chain fatty acids accumulate.

> Bottom Line: Peroxisomes use oxygen to carry out oxidative reactions and catalase to neutralize the toxic H₂O₂ byproduct.

A red blood cell is placed in a 0.45% NaCl solution (normal saline is 0.9% NaCl). What will happen to the cell?

  A) The cell will crenate due to water loss
  B) The cell will swell and potentially lyse due to water influx
  C) The cell will remain unchanged
  D) The cell will undergo plasmolysis

### Correct Answer: The cell will swell and potentially lyse due to water influx

A 0.45% NaCl solution is hypotonic relative to the red cell; 0.9% NaCl is approximately isotonic with human cells, although intracellular fluid is not simply a 0.9% NaCl solution. Water moves into the cell by osmosis, causing swelling. If swelling exceeds the membrane's capacity, the cell lyses.

  |  | Solution Type |  | Effect on RBC | 
  |  | Hypertonic (>0.9%) |  | Crenation (shrinkage) | 
  |  | Isotonic (0.9%) |  | No change | 
  |  | Hypotonic (<0.9%) |  | Swelling; possible hemolysis if sufficiently severe | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. The cell will crenate due to water loss:** Crenation (cell shrinkage with a scalloped edge) occurs when a red blood cell is placed in a hypertonic solution (greater than 0.9% NaCl). In hypertonic conditions, water moves out of the cell by osmosis, causing it to shrivel. Since 0.45% NaCl is hypotonic (not hypertonic), water flows into the cell, causing swelling rather than crenation.

**C. The cell will remain unchanged:** The cell would remain unchanged only in an isotonic solution (approximately 0.9% NaCl for human cells), where the solute concentration outside equals the concentration inside. At 0.45% NaCl, there is a significant osmotic gradient driving water into the cell, so the cell will definitely be affected.

**D. The cell will undergo plasmolysis:** Plasmolysis describes the plasma membrane pulling away from the cell wall in a walled cell, such as a plant cell, that has lost water in a hypertonic solution. Red blood cells have no cell wall, so they instead crenate in hypertonic conditions or swell and potentially lyse in hypotonic ones.

### Key Educational Points:

  - 0.45% NaCl is hypotonic to the red cell, because 0.9% NaCl is approximately isotonic with human cells.
  - Water moves by osmosis from the more dilute solution into the more concentrated cytoplasm, so the cell swells.
  - Red blood cells have no cell wall, so swelling beyond the membrane's capacity ruptures it, producing haemolysis.
  - A hypertonic solution does the reverse, drawing water out and leaving the cell crenated, or shrunken and notched.
  - Plasmolysis does not apply here, because it describes the membrane pulling away from a cell wall and red cells have none.

> Bottom Line: Water moves from hypotonic to hypertonic regions by osmosis. Animal cells lack cell walls and are vulnerable to lysis in hypotonic solutions.

Q60  [bio-256]  Answer: B
==============================================================================
The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump transports 3 Na⁺ ions out and 2 K⁺ ions into the cell per ATP hydrolyzed. This pump is an example of:

  A) Facilitated diffusion
  B) Primary active transport
  C) Secondary active transport
  D) Simple diffusion

### Correct Answer: Primary active transport

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase directly uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to transport ions against their concentration gradients, making it primary active transport. It pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle, creating a net movement of one positive charge outward (electrogenic pump), which contributes to the resting membrane potential.

  |  | Transport Type |  | Energy Source |  | Example | 
  |  | Primary active |  | ATP directly |  | Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase | 
  |  | Secondary active |  | Ion gradient |  | Na⁺/glucose symporter | 
  |  | Facilitated diffusion |  | No direct ATP; down the relevant gradient |  | GLUT transporters | 

### Why the other choices are wrong:

**A. Facilitated diffusion:** Facilitated diffusion uses a channel or carrier to move a substance down its relevant gradient without direct ATP consumption. For ions, the relevant gradient includes both concentration and electrical forces. The Na+/K+-ATPase instead directly uses ATP to move ions against their electrochemical gradients.

**C. Secondary active transport:** Secondary active transport uses the electrochemical gradient established by primary active transport (like the Na+/K+ ATPase) to drive the movement of another substance against its gradient. The Na+/glucose symporter (SGLT1) in the small intestine is a classic example: Na+ flows down its gradient (established by the Na+/K+ pump), and glucose is co-transported uphill. No ATP is directly consumed in secondary active transport itself.

**D. Simple diffusion:** Simple diffusion is the passive movement of small, nonpolar molecules directly through the phospholipid bilayer without any protein assistance. Examples include O2, CO2, and steroid hormones crossing the membrane. It requires no energy and only moves substances down their concentration gradient, making it fundamentally different from the ATP-powered Na+/K+ pump.

### Key Educational Points:

  - Primary active transport uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis directly to move solutes against their gradients.
  - The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is the standard example, exporting 3 Na⁺ for every 2 K⁺ it imports per ATP.
  - That unequal exchange moves one net positive charge out per cycle, making the pump electrogenic and contributing to the resting membrane potential.
  - Secondary active transport is powered by the gradient this pump creates rather than by ATP itself, as in the Na⁺/glucose symporter.
  - Facilitated diffusion is passive: it uses a protein channel or carrier and moves solute down its relevant gradient with no direct ATP input. For ions that gradient is electrochemical, so an ion can move passively against its concentration gradient when the electrical component is sufficiently favourable.

> Bottom Line: Primary active transport uses ATP directly; secondary active transport uses an electrochemical gradient established by primary active transport.
